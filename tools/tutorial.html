Every clue has two parts

A cryptic clue is two clues joined into one sentence:

  • The definition. This is a plain clue for the answer, like one in an ordinary crossword. It can be one word or several. It always sits at the start or the end of the clue, never in the middle.
  • The wordplay. This is a set of instructions for building the answer out of letters. It fills the rest of the clue.

The number in brackets at the end is the letter count: how many letters the answer has. (3,4) means two words, of 3 and then 4 letters.

Read as a normal sentence, the clue is about something else entirely. That normal reading is called the surface, and it is there to mislead you. Ignore it. Your job is to find where the definition stops and the wordplay starts.

Here is how you know you are right. The definition means the answer, and the wordplay builds the same answer. When both work, you have it.

Indicators and link words

Most wordplay contains an indicator: a word that tells you what to do with the words right next to it. "Broken" might mean "rearrange these letters". "Back" might mean "write this word backwards". The words an indicator works on are called the fodder. An indicator can come just before its fodder or just after it.

Each clue type below lists its most used indicators. The number after each word is how many clues in these puzzles used it that way, and each list links to every indicator of that type. Even the full lists are not complete: any word with a similar meaning can do the same job.

A few clues also have a link word between the definition and the wordplay, such as for, is, 's, gives, makes or from. It joins the two parts, like an equals sign. It adds no letters.

Punctuation is mostly there for the surface, so you can usually ignore it. Two exceptions: a question mark often warns you that a word is being used in an odd sense, and an exclamation mark can mean an &lit (see the end of the list below).

Every example below is a real clue from a puzzle on this site, picked because it reads as a natural sentence and shows its clue type plainly. The definition is green and the indicator is pink. Here is one clue with every part labelled:

Her plans unexpectedly change (8)
The definition is "change", at the end. The rest is the wordplay. "Unexpectedly" is the indicator: it says "rearrange". HER PLANS is the fodder, 8 letters. Rearrange them to get SHRAPNEL, which is slang for loose change, and has 8 letters. The surface is about someone's plans going wrong. The clue is about coins.

The main clue types

Related types sit together. Anagram and cycling shuffle letters. Container, reversal, deletion, substitution, move and palindrome change a piece. Hidden word and letter selection pick out letters already in the clue. Homophone and spoonerism work by sound. The last three have no separate wordplay.

Anagram

Clearly identify evil criminal (12)
Rearrange the letters of some words in the clue. "Criminal" is the indicator: a criminal is bent, and so are these letters. IDENTIFY EVIL is the fodder: 12 letters, the same as the letter count. Rearranged, they spell DEFINITIVELY, which means "clearly".
Anagram indicators suggest mess, change or wrongdoing. Always count the fodder's letters. They must match the letter count exactly, unless the clue names letters to take out, as in the next section.

Composite anagram

Stick, broken, reunited without it (6)
The fodder holds the answer's letters plus some extras, and the clue names the extras. Here the fodder is REUNITED, 8 letters, and the answer has 6. "Without it" takes out I and T, leaving R, E, U, N, E, D. "Broken" rearranges those six into ENDURE, which means "stick", as in stick it out.
The anagram indicator is an ordinary one. The giveaway is a fodder that is too long, beside a few letters that are named and are not part of the answer. Think of it as subtracting first and shuffling second.

Cycling

Gives the cold shoulder to Italian fellow cycling (7)
Move a letter from one end of a word to the other, keeping the rest in order. An Italian fellow is a SIGNORE. Cycling sends its first letter, S, round to the end: IGNORES, which means "gives the cold shoulder to". It looks like an anagram until you notice the order survives.

Move

Dog performing with duck coming last? (5)
Take a letter (or a few) out of a word and put it back somewhere else in the same word. "Performing" is DOING. A duck, in cricket, is a score of nothing: O. "Coming last" lifts the O out of DOING and puts it at the end: DING + O = DINGO, a wild dog. It is one step, not a deletion followed by an insertion: nothing leaves the word for good.
Move indicators: coming last, dropping, moving, shifted, brought down, going up (in a down clue, up and down point along the word).

Charade

Charm a stubborn person over time (6)
Build the answer from small pieces placed one after another, like beads on a string. "A" stays as A. A "stubborn person" is a MULE. "Time" is T, a standard abbreviation. This is a down clue, so "over" stacks the first pieces on top of the last: A + MULE + T = AMULET, a lucky charm. The surface wants "charm" to be a verb. It is the noun.
Many charades have no indicator: the pieces follow each other in the order the clue gives them, and small words like and, with or by join them without adding letters. When there is an indicator, it says which piece goes first.

Container

Shoe absorbs cold mud (7)
Put one piece inside another. "Shoe" is SANDAL. "Cold" is C, as on a tap. The sandal absorbs the C: SAN + C + DAL = SCANDAL. The "mud" is the kind that is slung at a reputation, not the kind on your shoe. The outer word can split at any point. Try each gap until you get a real word of the right length.

Reversal

Break crockery up (4)
Write a word backwards. "Crockery" is POTS. This is a down clue, where the answer is written from top to bottom, so "up" means "read it from the bottom up": POTS becomes STOP, which means "break", as in a break in play. In an across clue the same job is done by words like back or returned. Up and rising only work in down clues.

Deletion

Student lacks head for income-generating activity (6)
Take letters away from a word. A "student" is a LEARNER. "Lacks head" removes its first letter, L: (L)EARNER gives EARNER, an "income-generating activity". "For" is a link word.
Deletion indicators say which letters go: headless the first, endless or mostly the last, heartless the middle, and without or not a piece the clue names.

Substitution

Tranquil moon goddess changes sides (6)
Swap one letter or piece of a word for another. The Greek goddess of the moon is SELENE. "Changes sides" swaps L (left) for R (right): SELENE becomes SERENE, which means "tranquil".

Palindrome

On reflection, this sister doesn't change (3)
The answer reads the same forwards and backwards. "On reflection" mirrors the word and "doesn't change" says the mirror image is the same word. A sister whose name works both ways is a NUN. These clues are rare, and short.

Hidden word

They're grounded and somewhat bemused (4)
The answer is written inside the clue, in consecutive letters, often running across the gaps between words. Here it sits inside one word: bEMUSed gives EMUS. "Somewhat" says take only part of it. "They're grounded" sounds like sensible people, but it means birds that cannot fly.

First, last and other letters

Whisky makers: Ardbeg, Laphroaig and Talisker for starters (4)
Take the first letter of each word. "For starters" is the indicator, so take the first letters of "makers, Ardbeg, Laphroaig and Talisker": M, A, L, T gives MALT, a whisky. "And" only joins the list. The surface works because those three really are malt whisky distilleries. Take letters only from the words the indicator points at, and stop when you have the letter count.
Other selections work the same way: finally takes last letters, regularly or oddly every other letter, heart of the middle, and extremely the two ends.

Homophone

Cross and abusive on the phone (4)
The answer sounds like another word but is spelled differently. "Abusive" is RUDE. Heard down a phone line, RUDE sounds just like ROOD, an old word for a cross, as in the rood screen of a church. The surface wants "cross" to mean angry.

Spoonerism

Spooner's crew decline help with water coming in (7)
Swap the opening sounds of two words, as the Reverend Spooner was said to. A "crew" is a BAND and to "decline" is to SAG. Said Spooner's way, BAND SAG becomes SANDBAG, a "help with water coming in". The clue names Spooner, so you always know when to do it.

Double definition

Means business (6)
The clue is two definitions of the same word, and there is no wordplay. Each definition can be one word or several. An agency is a means (things happen "through the agency of" something), and an agency is also a business, like a travel agency. So the answer is AGENCY. The clue hides the split by reading as a familiar phrase.
These are often the shortest clues in the puzzle.

Cryptic definition

Domestic flight (9)
The whole clue is one definition with a trick in it, and there is no wordplay. It reads like air travel, but a "flight" is also a run of stairs, and a domestic one is in a house: STAIRCASE. The letter count is what rules out the aeroplane. Clues like this often end with a question mark, to warn you that a word means something unusual.

&lit

A sun proven briefly to be unstable (9)
The whole clue is the wordplay and the whole clue is the definition, so all of it is green, and the indicators are pink with a green underline. As wordplay: "proven briefly" is PROVEN cut short, PROVE, and "unstable" says rearrange A, SUN and PROVE, which spells SUPERNOVA. As a definition: a supernova is a sun that has become unstable and blown apart. The name is short for "and literally so". These clues are rare, and often end with an exclamation mark.

Rebus

HIJKLMNO (5)
The clue is a picture made of letters, figures or symbols, and no words in it define the answer. Say what you see: these are the letters from H to O. Said aloud, "H to O" is H2O, the formula for WATER. The picture can lean on which letters are shown, their order, where they sit, or what is missing. These clues have no lower-case letters, and often end with a question mark.

Reverse clue

Heaps in poor condition? (3,5)
The clue reads like wordplay, but it describes what the answer does, not how to build it. Run it backwards. The answer means "in poor condition": BAD SHAPE. Now read that answer as if it were a clue: "bad" is an anagram indicator, and SHAPE rearranged gives HEAPS, the clue's first word. The question mark warns you that something odd is going on. These clues are rare.

How to solve a clue, step by step

  1. Read the clue once. Then ignore what it seems to be about.
  2. Test the first word or two as the definition, then the last word or two. Whatever is left is the wordplay. If both ends could work, pick the one where the rest of the clue builds a word with the right letter count.
  3. Look for indicators. They tell you which clue type you have.
  4. Count letters. If "confused" sits next to words with 9 letters in total, and the letter count is (9), it is probably an anagram.
  5. Use the letters you already have from answers that cross this one.
  6. Use the hints when you are stuck. Each hint teaches you one step, without giving away the answer. The first three are "Spot the indicator words", "Where is the definition?" and "What kind of clue is this?". You can take them in any order. "The building blocks" opens after those, and "Full walkthrough" opens last, because they give more away.

Reading solving blogs

Solvers write free blogs that explain every clue in the big daily cryptics, the morning after each puzzle. Fifteensquared covers the Guardian and the Independent. Big Dave's Crossword Blog covers the Telegraph. Times for the Times covers the Times. They are the best place to look up a puzzle this site does not have.

The bloggers use a shorthand that they rarely explain. Here is what it means:

You'll seeIt means

CAPITALSthe fodder, or the letters that go into the answer
(round brackets)the indicator, copied from the clue
[square brackets]letters that are removed, or the words that remove them
*an anagram: RETAIL* means "rearrange RETAIL"

&litthe whole clue is both the definition and the wordplay

For example, a blogger might write: Chaperone (shredded) CORSET* = ESCORT. That means: the definition is "Chaperone", the indicator is "shredded", and rearranging CORSET gives ESCORT, which means "chaperone". The hints on this site tell you the same things, one at a time.
