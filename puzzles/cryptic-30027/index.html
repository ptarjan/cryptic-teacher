Across

1 across RESTFUL

Relaxing, interval nearly complete (7)

Definition: Relaxing · charade + deletion

Relaxing → RESTFUL: an adjective for what leaves you rested, said of an afternoon or a room rather than of the person in it.

  • interval → REST the break between two acts is the interval, and a pause of any kind is a rest
  • complete → FUL complete means full, and the last of those four letters never gets written
  • nearly — Nearly there is not quite there, so the word governed by it stops one letter short of its own ending.

Somebody unwinding while the interval is almost over.

'nearly complete' describes the interval in the surface — the break all but finished — while in the wordplay it is an instruction aimed at one word.

5 across GLAZIER

Workman is good but not so hard-working (7)

Definition: Workman · charade

Workman → GLAZIER: definition by example — a glazier is one particular trade, the man who cuts and fits window glass, not a synonym for workman.

  • good → G g is the standard short form of good, the grade on a school report
  • but not so hard-working → LAZIER someone who works less hard than the next man is lazier

A tradesman rated decent enough but not the keenest on site.

'Workman is good' opens like a sentence about somebody's character, so the definition looks longer than it is: it stops at the first word and 'is' only joins it on.

9 across BRILL

Fish kingfisher finally caught in beak (5)

Definition: Fish · container + last letter

Fish → BRILL: definition by example — the brill is a European flatfish, a close relation of the turbot, rather than a synonym for fish.

  • beak → BILL a bird's beak is its bill
  • kingfisher finally → R the last letter of kingfisher, produced by the operation rather than looked up as a short form
  • finally — Finally comes at the end of things, so what is taken is the last letter of the word in front of it.
  • caught in — Something caught in a thing is stuck inside it, so the letter just found lands in the middle of the word that follows.

A kingfisher with its catch wedged in its beak.

'Fish kingfisher' reads as one phrase, the bird that fishes, so the definition looks two words long when it is only the first.

10 across RETREATED

Shrank, offered another freebie? (9)

Definition: Shrank · double definition

Shrank → RETREATED: pulled back, as troops or a shy person do; and 'offered another freebie' → RE-TREATED, treated to something free all over again.

  • Shrank → drew back — what an army does when it gives ground, and what anybody does from a bad smell
  • offered another freebie? → to treat someone is to stand them something for nothing, and the question mark asks you to read that verb with a hyphen and a second helping

Somebody backed away after being handed one more thing for nothing.

The two senses are not even pronounced alike: the second wants a hyphen and a stress on the first syllable, which is why 'offered another freebie' looks nothing like a definition.

11 across SCOREBOARD

Here’s the state of play: having small heart, pig died (10)

Definition: Here’s the state of play · charade

Here’s the state of play → SCOREBOARD: the board is where you look to see how a game stands, so the definition points at the place you read it.

  • small → S s is the clothes-label short form of small
  • heart → CORE the heart of the matter is its core
  • pig → BOAR a boar is the male pig
  • died → D d is the standard short form of died, the one on gravestones and in family trees

A bulletin from the farm: the pig with the weak heart has died.

The colon makes the rest of the clue read as the state of play being reported, which is what hides a four-piece build behind it.

12 across LEAN

Incline to be thin (4)

Definition: Incline · double definition

Incline → LEAN: to tip from the upright; and 'thin' → LEAN, the adjective for meat without fat or a spare, rangy figure.

  • Incline → the verb: to tip away from the upright, as a tower does and as a person against a wall does
  • thin → the adjective used of meat with no fat on it, and of a build with nothing to spare

Somebody who tends towards thinness.

'Incline to be' reads as one phrase meaning tend to, so the whole clue looks like a single remark about a build, and the join between the two definitions falls inside it.

14 across BENEFACTRESS

Free cabs sent out for patron (12)

Definition: patron · anagram

patron → BENEFACTRESS: a woman who funds or supports a person or a cause, so a patron, with the answer merely specifying which sex.

  • Free cabs sent → FREECABSSENT twelve letters of fodder, exactly the width of the light
  • out — Out here is out of true and out of order, so the letters in front of it are no longer in the arrangement they arrived in.

A taxi firm sending free rides out at somebody else's expense.

'Free' looks like the anagram signal standing in front of its fodder, and it is fodder itself; the instruction is the single word after 'sent'.

18 across BREATHALYSER

Device held by police, the barrel say trembling (12)

Definition: Device held by police · anagram

Device held by police → BREATHALYSER: the roadside instrument an officer holds out for a driver to blow into, which measures the alcohol in the breath.

  • the barrel say → THEBARRELSAY twelve letters of fodder, exactly the width of the light
  • trembling — Something trembling will not hold still, and letters that cannot hold still do not stay in the order printed.

An officer holding a gun whose barrel is shaking.

'say' looks like the 'for example' marker attached to 'barrel' and it is three of the twelve letters, which is what keeps the fodder out of sight.

21 across RUCK

Mass of people in lorry, the first to leave (4)

Definition: Mass of people · deletion

Mass of people → RUCK: the undistinguished crowd, as in 'the common ruck', the ordinary many that somebody rises out of.

  • lorry → TRUCK a lorry is a truck
  • the first → T the first letter of that word, and the one that goes
  • to leave — What leaves goes away and does not come back, so the letter named just before it is gone from the word.

A crowd packed into a lorry, with the one at the front getting off.

'the first to leave' reads as a person stepping down from the crowd and names a letter instead. The ruck is also the heap of players over the ball in rugby, the same idea of an undifferentiated mass.

22 across PASSED PAWN

One in line to become queen was accepted, with knight on hand (6,4)

Definition: One in line to become queen · charade

One in line to become queen → PASSED PAWN: a pawn with no enemy pawn left to stop it, so it has a clear run to promotion.

  • was accepted → PASSED a motion that was accepted at a meeting was passed
  • hand → PAW a paw is a hand in the joking sense — 'get your paws off'
  • knight → N N is the chess notation for the knight, since K is taken by the king
  • on — In an across clue one thing set on another follows it along the row, so the knight lands after the hand.

A woman next in line to the throne was approved, with a knight standing by.

'One in line to become queen' reads as royal succession and belongs to a chessboard: a pawn that reaches the far rank is promoted, and almost always to a queen.

25 across EMAIL LIST

Time is all wrong for this marketing tool (5,4)

Definition: marketing tool · anagram

marketing tool → EMAIL LIST: the file of addresses a business sends its offers to, one of the standard instruments of direct marketing.

  • Time is all → TIMEISALL nine letters of fodder, with the little verb counted in among them
  • wrong — Wrong means not as it should be, and letters not as they should be are letters standing in the wrong order.

Someone complaining that the timing of the whole thing is off.

'Time is all wrong' reads as one complaint about timing, and every word of it but the last is fodder, the verb included.

26 across NOBLE

For one, count boxing as such an art? (5)

Definition: For one, count · double definition

For one, count → NOBLE: a count is one rank of nobleman, an example rather than a synonym; and boxing's nickname, the noble art, supplies the adjective.

  • For one, count → 'for one' means 'for example', so a count is offered as one instance of the rank wanted rather than as a synonym for it
  • boxing as such an art? → boxing has a nickname of the shape 'the — art', and the missing word there is the one the first half is after

A judge at the ringside treating boxing as an art form.

'count boxing' reads as scoring a bout, which is where the surface sends you. 'As such' is the stitch between two definitions, one of a rank and one of an adjective.

27 across KITCHEN

Be keen to fetch range round, for here? (7)

Definition: for here? · container

for here? → KITCHEN: the question mark carries the joke — the room named is the very room the wordplay's 'range' would be standing in.

  • range → KEN beyond your ken is beyond your range of knowledge
  • Be keen to → ITCH to itch to do something is to be keen to do it
  • round — What goes round a thing encloses it, so the word beside it wraps itself about the letters already found.

Being eager to fetch the cooker round to where it belongs.

'range' is the cooker in the surface and a range of knowledge in the wordplay, and the answer is the room a cooker stands in, so both senses are live at once.

28 across PIEBALD

Horse meat baked in pastry, plain (7)

Definition: Horse · charade

Horse → PIEBALD: a piebald is a horse marked in irregular patches of black and white, the word serving as the name of the animal itself.

  • meat baked in pastry → PIE meat under a pastry lid is a pie
  • plain → BALD a bald statement is a plain one, with nothing added to soften it

Horse meat in a pastry case, served plain.

'Horse meat' reads as one thing, the scandal food, and the definition is 'Horse' by itself. The colouring is named after the magpie, pied and black and white.

1 down ROBUST

Strong or, when knocked over, broken (6)

Definition: Strong · reversal + charade

Strong → ROBUST: strong in the sturdy, able-to-take-punishment sense, used of machines, health and arguments rather than of muscles.

  • or, when knocked over → RO the clue's own 'or', its two letters turned end for end
  • broken → BUST a bust washing machine is a broken one
  • knocked over — A thing knocked over lands the wrong way up, so the two letters beside it are written out from the back.

Something sturdy that only gives way once it has been knocked over.

'or' looks like the smallest word in the sentence, there only to hold 'Strong' and 'broken' together, and it is the entire first piece.

2 down SAIGON

Miss this musical (6)

Definition: Miss this musical · cryptic definition

the whole clue → SAIGON: the city is the second half of the title 'Miss Saigon', so the answer is completed by the clue's first word rather than defined.

  • Miss → not the verb 'fail to catch', which is how the surface reads it: this is the courtesy title, and it is the first word of a show's name
  • this musical → 'this' points at the answer itself — set it after that title and you have the name of a Boublil and Schonberg show

Advice to give a show a miss.

'Miss' reads as a verb, an instruction to skip the evening, and it is a title instead. Nothing here defines the answer: the clue asks you to finish a name.

3 down FALSE TEETH

Irregular force that may be out at night (5,5)

Definition: that may be out at night · charade

that may be out at night → FALSE TEETH: dentures are taken out at bedtime and left in a glass overnight, so 'out' is literal removal.

  • Irregular → FALSE an irregular is a soldier outside the regular army, not the genuine article, which is the sense wanted
  • force → TEETH a law with teeth has force behind it — teeth are what let a thing bite

An unofficial armed band that only comes out after dark.

'Irregular force' reads as guerrillas, one phrase, and the two words are separate pieces. Dentures spend the night in a glass, which is the 'out at night' meant.

4 down LARGO

Piece of music popular gospeller introduces (5)

Definition: Piece of music · hidden word

Piece of music → LARGO: the tempo marking used as the name of the movement itself, the slow broad one in a concerto or a sonata.

  • popular gospeller → LARGO five consecutive letters running over the gap between the two words, starting four letters into the first
  • introduces — To introduce a thing is to bring it inside, and these letters have already been brought inside the two words in front.

A well-liked preacher opening proceedings with a tune.

'introduces' is the only instruction, and it sits at the end where a solver has stopped looking for one. A largo is a movement played slowly and broadly.

5 down GETS READY

Greets day for moving and prepares (4,5)

Definition: prepares · anagram

prepares → GETS READY: the everyday phrasal version of preparing yourself, and both sit in the third person singular so the swap works.

  • Greets day → GREETSDAY nine letters of fodder, exactly the length wanted
  • moving — What is moving will not stay where it was put, and letters that will not stay put end up in new places.

Someone welcoming the morning before setting off somewhere.

'day for moving' reads as an appointment being shifted to another date, so the instruction hides inside the picture. The definition then arrives after 'and', where a second verb about the day is expected.

6 down A FEW

A sigh of relief heard, not many (1,3)

Definition: not many · charade + homophone

not many → A FEW: the small-number idiom, which keeps its article — which is why the enumeration reads (1,3).

  • A → A the clue's own opening letter, taken exactly as it stands
  • sigh of relief heard → FEW the sigh of relief you let out is phew, and that noise is spelt another way here
  • heard — What is heard reaches you as a noise, so what you write down is the spelling of the sound and not of the word that made it.

One breath of relief, overheard — and not many more than that.

Ph and f are the same noise, so the whole distance between the sigh and the letters is spelling. The clue's own 'A' is a piece of the answer, not joinery.

7 down IN THE RED

Being so placed is nothing to your credit (2,3,3)

Definition: Being so placed is nothing to your credit · cryptic definition

the whole clue → IN THE RED: being in the red is being overdrawn, nothing on the credit side, from the red ink once used for debit balances.

  • so placed → the surface offers a position in a queue or a league table; the position meant is one in a set of accounts
  • nothing to your credit → not 'nothing to be proud of', which is how it reads: credit is the bookkeeping column, and having nothing in it is the state described

A dig at somebody whose position does them no honour at all.

'nothing to your credit' reads as a verdict on a character and means the credit column of an account. Red ink was how a bookkeeper wrote a debit balance, and the phrase kept the colour.

8 down RUDENESS

Bad manners of English in undress, playing (8)

Definition: Bad manners · anagram

Bad manners → RUDENESS: the quality that bad manners consist of, discourtesy in general rather than any single ill-mannered act.

  • English in undress → EUNDRESS E is the standard short form of English, joined to the seven letters beside it to make eight
  • playing — Playing here is play in the sense of messing about with a thing, and letters messed about with come out in some other order.

The English are accused of larking about half-dressed.

'in' looks like an insertion instruction and is only holding the fodder together — every letter on both sides of it goes into the shuffle.

13 down ATTENDANCE

Towards end of evening, jig around gate (10)

Definition: gate · charade

gate → ATTENDANCE: at a British football or cricket ground the gate is the number of people who came through the turnstiles, so it is the crowd figure itself.

  • Towards → AT aiming at a thing and aiming towards it come to the same
  • end of evening → TEN ten o'clock at night, when the evening is drawing to a close — not a letter taken from the word
  • jig around → DANCE to jig around is to dance

Late in the evening there is dancing by the gate.

'end of evening' looks like an instruction to take a final letter and is a time of night instead. Gate is the British word for the crowd through a turnstile, counted in heads or in money.

15 down EMANATION

Effusion from armies regularly upset people (9)

Definition: Effusion · alternate letters + reversal + charade

Effusion → EMANATION: something that flows or streams out from a source — a smell, a light, a warmth — which is what an effusion is.

  • armies regularly upset → EMA the first, third and fifth letters of armies, then set down running the other way
  • people → NATION a nation is a people, the whole population of a country taken together
  • regularly — Taken regularly is taken at a fixed interval, and the interval here is every other letter of the word beside it.
  • upset — Upset in a down clue tips the letters back up the column, so they are read the other way round.

Something seeping out of armies that keep upsetting the locals.

Nothing here looks like an instruction: the clue is one sentence about armies annoying people, and both 'regularly' and 'upset' are doing mechanical work inside that picture.

16 down UBERGEEK

Taxi from Greece not realistic initially for computer obsessive (8)

Definition: computer obsessive · charade + deletion + first letter

computer obsessive → UBERGEEK: a geek is someone absorbed in computers, and the uber- prefix pushes that absorption to the far end of the scale.

  • Taxi → UBER Uber is the ride-hailing app people summon instead of hailing a cab
  • from Greece → GREEK somebody from Greece is a Greek
  • realistic initially → R the first letter of realistic, and the one that comes off the word above
  • not — 'Not' is the removal: the word in front has to do without the letter named just after it.
  • initially — Initially means at the beginning, so only the first letter of the word before it is taken.

A cab all the way from Greece, which hardly sounds realistic, ordered by someone who lives at a screen.

'Taxi from Greece' reads as one journey, and its two halves belong to different pieces. The uber- in front is also German for over, which is what makes the compound mean an extreme one.

17 down PENCHANT

Liking to write a melody (8)

Definition: Liking · charade

Liking → PENCHANT: a penchant for a thing is a settled liking or leaning towards it, a shade stronger than a preference.

  • to write → PEN to pen a letter is to write it
  • a melody → CHANT a chant is a melody of the plainest kind, one line sung over and over

A fondness for composing tunes.

'Liking to write' reads as one appetite, somebody who enjoys writing, so the definition looks longer than the single word it is.

19 down LAMBDA

Letter from prosecutor supporting one simply innocent (6)

Definition: Letter · charade

Letter → LAMBDA: definition by example — lambda is the eleventh letter of the Greek alphabet, one letter rather than a synonym for the word.

  • one simply innocent → LAMB the proverbially innocent one, as in 'innocent as a lamb'
  • prosecutor → DA DA is the district attorney, the lawyer who prosecutes in an American court
  • supporting — What supports a thing sits underneath it, and in a down entry underneath means later along the answer.

A note from the prosecution about somebody plainly innocent.

'Letter from prosecutor' reads as something arriving in the post, and 'Letter' alone is the definition — a Greek one. Lambda is the eleventh of that alphabet, written as a small pointed arch.

20 down INDEED

Definitely denied being replaced (6)

Definition: Definitely · anagram

Definitely → INDEED: the emphasiser you add to agree flatly with something, 'indeed it is' standing in for 'definitely it is'.

  • denied → DENIED six letters of fodder, exactly the length wanted
  • being replaced — To replace a thing is to put it back down somewhere, and letters put back down do not have to go where they came from.

One denial swapped out for another.

'Definitely denied' reads as one phrase, a flat refusal, so the definition looks two words long when it is only the first.

23 down SIT-UP

An exercise for you – don’t go to bed (3-2)

Definition: An exercise · double definition

An exercise → SIT-UP: the abdominal exercise done lying on the floor; and 'don’t go to bed' → SIT UP, to stay up instead of turning in.

  • An exercise → the gym movement: lying flat on the floor and raising your trunk without help from your arms
  • don’t go to bed → to stay up rather than turn in — what somebody does who waits up for a latecomer

A bit of exercise prescribed, and an instruction to stay awake.

The dash reads as a pause in one piece of advice and is the seam between two definitions. The hyphen the answer prints belongs only to the first of them.

24 down BLAH

Balderdash, lies and hooey from the start (4)

Definition: Balderdash, lies and hooey from the start · &lit + first letters

the whole clue → BLAH: read straight it is a list of words for empty talk, which is exactly what blah is, while the same words spell it out.

  • Balderdash → B the opening letter of balderdash, the old-fashioned word for nonsense
  • lies → L the opening letter of lies
  • and → A the little joining word gives up its letter as well, which is what makes the run come out
  • hooey → H the opening letter of hooey, American slang for the same nonsense
  • from the start — The start of a word is its opening letter, and each word in front of this phrase gives up that letter and no more.

Somebody reeling off every word they know for nonsense.

'and' is fodder too, and that is what keeps the run of initials hidden: solvers skip the joining words when scanning for first letters.
