Across

1 across GOODNESS ME

Everyman adhering to decency: that's surprising (8,2)

Definition: that's surprising · charade

It is what people blurt out when something takes them aback, so the definition names the reaction the phrase exists to express.

  • decency → GOODNESS Decency is the abstract moral quality — the noun for what a decent person has.
  • Everyman → ME Everyman is the pen name of the Observer's setter, so the clue is pointing at whoever wrote it.
  • adhering to — To adhere to something is to stick fast against it, so this piece is glued on to the end of the word beside it rather than tucked inside it.

The setter sticking to the proprieties, which apparently comes as a shock.

Everyman signs the Observer's puzzle every week, so a clue naming him is naming its own author — and setters write themselves in as ME.

6 across SCAT

Jazz fans with bit of sax moved to intro? Improvise lyrics (4)

Definition: Improvise lyrics · cycling

To scat is to sing improvised nonsense syllables in place of a song's words, which is what a jazz singer does through a solo chorus.

  • Jazz fans → CATS Cats is jazz slang for the players and for the people who love them.
  • with bit of sax moved to intro — The bit of 'sax' is the S it starts with, and an intro is an opening, so that letter travels from the back of the word to the front.

A band shifting a snatch of saxophone to the top of the number.

Nothing is lost in the move: the letter pushed off the end rejoins at the front, which is what separates this from a deletion.

9 across MONOCHROME

Hero - common ground - it's all black-and-white (10)

Definition: all black-and-white · anagram

A monochrome image is made in a single colour — in practice black, white and the greys between — which is what the phrase names in everyday speech.

  • Hero - common → HEROCOMMON anagram fodder, ten letters
  • ground — Coffee is ground down to powder, and letters given that treatment can be swept back together in any order at all.

A hero, some common ground, and a situation with no grey areas anywhere in it.

'Common ground' is such a natural pair that splitting it takes some doing — the second word is the instruction, not half of the phrase.

10 across ASTI

Sparkling wine in course of Lammas-tide (4)

Definition: Sparkling wine · hidden word

Asti is the sparkling white from the Piedmont town of the same name — Asti Spumante on the label — so the definition names it by type.

  • Lammas-tide → ASTI Four consecutive letters, starting in the middle of the word and running straight across its hyphen.
  • in course of — Whatever happens in the course of something happens inside it, so the letters wanted lie within the word that follows.

A glass of fizz drunk during the harvest festival.

Lammas is the old English harvest festival on 1 August and Lammas-tide the season around it; the word is here only for the letters it carries.

11 across WALK THE PLANK

In the main, disembark? (4,3,5)

Definition: In the main, disembark? · cryptic definition

Walking the plank is how a pirate crew made a prisoner leave the ship, so 'disembark' is the polite word for a forced exit over the side.

  • In the main → The reading the surface pushes is the everyday one — 'mostly', 'on the whole'.
  • disembark? → The setter's reading is the pirate one: leaving the ship at cutlass point, with 'the main' as the open sea.

A general remark about the business of getting off a boat.

'The main' is an old word for the open sea — the Spanish Main — which is what turns a mild qualifier into a location.

15 across MATADOR

Adjusting to drama, I'm a fighter (7)

Definition: a fighter · anagram

A matador is the bullfighter who faces the bull and makes the kill, so 'fighter' names him by his trade rather than by his temperament.

  • to drama → TODRAMA anagram fodder, seven letters
  • Adjusting — Adjusting something shifts its parts into new positions, which is exactly what happens to the two words after it.

An actor settling into a play and declaring himself a scrapper.

The clue speaks in the answer's own voice — 'I'm a fighter' — which is why the definition arrives sounding like a boast.

16 across STRETCH

Type of jeans - covering expanse? (7)

Definition: Type of jeans · double definition

Stretch jeans are the ones woven with elastane so they give, and a stretch of road or water is an expanse of it: the give and the extent.

  • Type of jeans → The denim sold with elastane in it takes this word in front of its name.
  • expanse → A long unbroken run of road, water or open country goes by the same word.

A pair of jeans roomy enough to cover a lot of ground.

'Covering' does nothing but join the two senses, and it is what makes the clue read as a single thought about oversized denim.

17 across LEANEST

Bucolic site, comfy home, minimal in extremis ... (7)

Definition: minimal in extremis · charade

The leanest of anything has had every spare ounce taken off it, which is what 'minimal' pushed to its limit describes.

  • Bucolic site → LEA A lea is a meadow, the poets' word for open pasture.
  • comfy home → NEST A nest is the snuggest home a bird could ask for.

An estate agent's advert for a snug country place with nothing surplus in it.

'In extremis' looks like the standard instruction to take a word's outer letters, and it does no such thing: it belongs to the definition, pushing 'minimal' to its limit.

19 across GAMIEST

... extremely stinky component of amalgam - i.e., stinkiest (7)

Definition: extremely stinky · hidden word

Gamy meat has hung long enough to smell high, so the superlative is the strongest-smelling of a set and 'extremely' carries the -est.

  • amalgam - i.e., stinkiest → GAMIEST Seven consecutive letters starting inside the first word, running through the two-letter abbreviation and on into the last.
  • component of — A component sits inside the thing it belongs to, so what is wanted is a run of letters lying within the words that follow.

The part of a mixture that reeks worse than anything else in it.

The dash and the full stops make the letters look separated when they run straight on, and 'i.e.' does surface duty while lending two letters.

20 across BREAK THE BANK

As a flooded river may come at too high a price (5,3,4)

Definition: As a flooded river may · double definition

Two senses of bank in one phrase: the river's edge a flood bursts through, and the store of money an expense empties.

  • As a flooded river may → Taken literally, going over the top is what a river in spate does to its edges.
  • come at too high a price → The idiom for costing more than anybody can afford, where the bank is the one that holds money.

A river in spate and the bill for the damage it leaves behind.

The phrase started at the gaming table, where a player wins more than the house has set aside to pay out; the flood is the older, more literal picture.

23 across UNIT

Military subdivision incapable: France lost (4)

Definition: Military subdivision · deletion

A unit is the army's standard block of organisation — a platoon, a company, a regiment — so it is any subdivision of a larger force.

  • incapable → UNFIT Somebody incapable of a job is unfit for it.
  • France → F is the international vehicle registration letter for France — the oval sticker on the back of the car — and it is what goes.
  • lost — Something lost has gone missing, so the letter named just before it is the one that drops out of the word.

A section of the army written off as useless after a defeat in France.

'France lost' reads as a result on a scoreboard and works as an instruction to drop a letter, which is what keeps the military surface intact.

24 across OPTIMALISE

Maximally improve A.I. - almost too simple, dubiously (10)

Definition: Maximally improve · anagram + deletion

To optimalise something is to make it as good as it can possibly be — the verb built straight off 'optimal', a longer form of optimise.

  • A.I. - almost too simple → AITOSIMPLE Ten letters of fodder: the abbreviation for artificial intelligence, then 'too' docked to almost all of itself, then the last word whole.
  • almost — Almost all of a word is that word with its last letter gone, so the little word it sits in front of arrives one letter short.
  • dubiously — Something done dubiously has not been done properly, so these letters have not been set down in their proper order.

Somebody claiming, not very believably, that improving artificial intelligence is almost too easy.

'Almost' is the piece solvers lose: it takes a letter off 'too' before anything is shuffled, so the fodder is ten letters and not eleven.

25 across TA-TA

Flipping American junk: I'm off (2-2)

Definition: I'm off · reversal

Ta-ta is the British goodbye, nursery and pub alike, so 'I'm off' is what somebody is saying at the moment they use it.

  • American → A A is the standard abbreviation for American.
  • junk → TAT Tat is British for cheap rubbish — the stuff on a jumble-sale table.
  • Flipping — Flipping something turns it over end for end, so the two pieces once assembled are read the other way round.

Dumping some cheap American rubbish and walking out.

'Flipping' is a mild British swear word in the surface, where it seems to be insulting the junk rather than turning anything round.

26 across ADOLESCENT

Youngster's amateur haircut with, after vacation, loathsome cologne (10)

Definition: Youngster · charade + outer letters

An adolescent is somebody in their teens, between child and adult, so 'youngster' is the everyday word for the same person.

  • amateur → A A is the standard abbreviation for amateur, as on an amateur golfer's entry.
  • haircut → DO A do is a hairdo, what you come out of the salon with.
  • loathsome → LE Emptied out, the word is left with only the letters at its two ends.
  • cologne → SCENT Cologne is a scent, and the lower-case c says the perfume rather than the city.
  • after vacation — A vacated word has had its insides emptied out, leaving only the letters standing at either end.

A kid with a home-done haircut and a bottle of horrible aftershave.

Cologne keeps a lower-case c because it is the perfume, not the German city that gave the perfume its name — a capital would have given the game away.

Down

1 down GEMS

Runs away from bugs in stones (4)

Definition: stones · deletion

Gems are cut precious stones — the jeweller's word for the stones themselves rather than the rings they end up in.

  • bugs → GERMS Bugs in the infection sense are germs — what you catch, not what crawls.
  • Runs → R is what a cricket scorecard writes for runs, and that is the letter taken out.
  • away from — Taking something away from a word removes it, so the letter just named is lifted out of the longer word that follows.

Somebody bolting from the creepy-crawlies under the rocks.

'Runs away' reads as fleeing the insects; the only thing running away is a single letter, and cricket is where that letter comes from.

2 down OINK

'Hey you, getting nookie regularly?' - swinish outburst (4)

Definition: swinish outburst · charade + regular letters

It is the noise a pig makes: 'swinish' points at the animal, and 'outburst' says this is a sound let out rather than a word.

  • Hey you → OI 'Oi!' is the British shout for hailing somebody rudely across a street.
  • nookie → NK Count through the word in threes and only its first and its fourth letters are left standing.
  • getting → Surface only: it turns the quoted line into a question about somebody's love life and hands over no letters.
  • regularly — Regularly means picked out at a fixed step, and unusually the step here is three rather than the every-other-letter it normally asks for.

A crude proposition yelled across a room.

Most solvers try alternate letters first, because that is what 'regularly' almost always asks for; the step here is three, so only two letters survive.

3 down NECK AND NECK

Even pair of cheeks? (4,3,4)

Definition: Even · charade

Two horses running neck and neck are exactly level, so 'even' is the racing sense of dead level rather than anything to do with numbers.

  • cheeks → NECK Cheek in the British sense of brazen nerve — 'what a cheek!' — has a one-syllable slang twin, and that is what these letters spell.
  • pair of → AND A pair is two of the same thing, and the little word that joins two of anything stands between them.
  • cheeks → NECK The plural is asking for that same slang word a second time.

A well-matched pair of buttocks.

Cheek meaning nerve is the sense in play — 'he had the neck to ask' — not the face or the backside the surface is pointing at.

4 down STRIKER

Sportsperson, senior, astride pedalled vehicle (7)

Definition: Sportsperson · container

A striker is the forward whose job is scoring goals in football, so 'sportsperson' defines by category, one kind of player standing for the whole.

  • senior → SR Sr is the tag added to a father's name to tell him apart from a son called the same thing.
  • pedalled vehicle → TRIKE A trike is a tricycle, pedalled like a bicycle but on three wheels.
  • astride — Sitting astride something means having a leg on either side of it, so the short piece ends up split around the longer one.

An elderly athlete perched on a tricycle.

Nothing points at football until the first word, and 'sportsperson' is deliberately vague — the surface would much rather you pictured a veteran cyclist.

5 down MEMPHIS

The writer, politician: that man's ruined city (7)

Definition: ruined city · charade

Memphis, the capital of ancient Egypt, survives only as ruins south of Cairo, so the definition names it by the state it is in.

  • The writer → ME The writer of a clue is its setter, and a setter writes themselves in with this two-letter word.
  • politician → MP MP is a Member of Parliament, the standard British shorthand for a politician.
  • that man's → HIS What belongs to that man is his.

A writer and a politician standing about in some other man's wrecked city.

'Ruined' is the trap: it looks like an instruction to shuffle letters, and it is part of the definition, since the Egyptian city has been ruins for centuries.

7 down CASUALTIES

After reshuffle, actualises hospital wards (10)

Definition: hospital wards · anagram

Casualty is the British hospital's emergency department, where ambulances bring the injured, so the plural names those wards.

  • actualises → ACTUALISES anagram fodder, ten letters
  • After reshuffle — A reshuffle deals the same cards out into new places, so the word beside it keeps its letters and loses their order.

A shake-up that finally makes the hospital's wards happen.

Casualty is what British hospitals call A&E, the department the ambulances pull up at, and 'wards' in the plural is the setter's extension of it.

8 down TRICK SHOTS

Short stick in play might effect these? (5,5)

Definition: Short stick in play might effect these? · &lit + anagram

Read straight it describes them: a cue is the stick, and the fancy pots a player brings off with an awkward short one are what 'these' names.

  • Short stick → SHORTSTICK anagram fodder, ten letters
  • in play — A ball in play is loose and being knocked about, so the letters beside it will not stay in the order they are printed.

A snooker player reaching for the short rest cue halfway through a frame.

The same two words are the picture and the fodder: the sentence describes what a cue can do while its opening words spell out the result.

12 down PYROMANIACS

Those drawn to light? (11)

Definition: Those drawn to light? · cryptic definition

A pyromaniac has an uncontrollable urge to set fires, so the phrase only works once 'light' is read as the verb, meaning to set alight.

  • Those drawn to light? → The surface picture is moths circling a lamp — creatures that cannot help heading for a bulb.
  • drawn to light → Taken the setter's way the pull is towards lighting things, and 'drawn' shifts from being attracted to being compelled.

Moths circling a lamp on a summer night.

'Light' reads as a noun the whole way through the clue and is a verb — the one word that has to change job before the answer appears.

13 down EMILY BLUNT

My bulletin edited to provide name of English actress (5,5)

Definition: name of English actress · anagram

Emily Blunt is the English actor of The Devil Wears Prada and A Quiet Place, so what the answer supplies is exactly what the definition asks for: her name.

  • My bulletin → MYBULLETIN anagram fodder, ten letters
  • edited — An edited text has been cut about and reordered, so the words named give up their letters but not their arrangement.

A journalist's own news report going under a sub-editor's pencil.

The surface hides the join by making 'edited' sound like part of the newsroom story rather than the instruction it is.

14 down STRATEGIST

Tactician gets artist in a lather (10)

Definition: Tactician · anagram

A strategist plans the whole campaign; 'tactician' is the loose everyday synonym for the same planning mind, though tactics are strictly the smaller moves inside it.

  • gets artist → GETSARTIST anagram fodder, ten letters
  • in a lather — Something worked into a lather has been whipped up and churned about, so the letters just named come out thoroughly disturbed.

A planner working an artist up into a rage.

'Gets' looks like a link word joining the tactician to the artist, and it is fodder — that misdirection is what costs solvers the letter count.

18 down T-SHAPED

Per Spooner, that woman recorded description of a certain road junction (1-6)

Definition: description of a certain road junction · spoonerism

A T-junction is a side road meeting another head-on, drawing the letter T on the map, so the answer describes its layout.

  • that woman → T That woman is she; the word gives up its SH, takes a T in exchange, and the noise left is a letter's own name.
  • recorded → SHAPED Something recorded has been taped; it hands over its T, takes the SH, and what comes back is spelt this way.
  • Per Spooner — The Reverend Spooner was famous for swapping the opening sounds of two words, so the pair named here trade their fronts.

A woman who taped a description of some road junction, as the Reverend might have put it.

Say 'she taped' aloud and swap the noises at the front of each word; the vowel drifts from 'ay' to 'ee' on the way, which is normal in a spoonerism.

19 down GABRIEL

Primarily, God's archangel bringing reports (I.eleven, Luke)? (7)

Definition: Primarily, God's archangel bringing reports (I.eleven, Luke)? · &lit + first letters

The whole clue reads straight: Gabriel is the archangel who carries God's announcements, and Luke chapter 1, verse 11 is where he turns up.

  • God's archangel bringing reports (I.eleven, Luke) → GABRIEL Seven words in order, each handing over its opening letter, with the bracketed reference supplying the last three.
  • Primarily — Primarily asks for what comes first, so every word in the list gives up only the letter it opens with.

A Bible citation for the angel who brings the messages.

The bracket looks like nothing but a citation and is fodder as well: 'I', 'eleven' and 'Luke' each surrender a first letter, so the reference spells while it cites.

21 down TIME

Upon rising, give out prison sentence (4)

Definition: prison sentence · reversal

Doing time is serving a prison sentence, and the word carries that sense on its own once a judge has handed one down.

  • give out → EMIT To give out heat, light or a smell is to emit it.
  • Upon rising — A down entry read upwards runs from the bottom of the grid to the top, so a word said to be rising arrives back to front.

A judge who gets out of bed and hands down a jail term.

'Give out' looks as though it belongs with the sentencing, since giving out sentences is what courts do, and it is the wordplay instead.

22 down CENT

Money posted (we're told) (4)

Definition: Money · homophone

A cent is the hundredth part of a dollar or a euro, so 'money' defines by example: one small coin standing in for the category.

  • posted → CENT Something posted has been sent, and that word said aloud is written the coin's way.
  • we're told — Being told something means hearing it, so the word wanted is one that sounds the same rather than one that looks the same.

Cash that has gone off in the post, by all accounts.

Nothing is hidden or shuffled here: the whole device is hearing the postal word instead of reading it, in a clue three words long.
