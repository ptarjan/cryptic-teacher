Across

1 across SPITFIRE

Fighter provided hints from behind rope on each side (8)

Definition: Fighter · reversal + charade + outer letters

A Spitfire is the Second World War fighter plane, so 'Fighter' names it by exactly what the aircraft was built to be.

  • hints → SPIT Hints are TIPS, and the turn writes them back to front.
  • provided → FI 'Provided' is IF, caught by the same turn and so written backwards.
  • rope → RE The two letters standing at the sides of ROPE, its first and its last.
  • from behind — Coming at words 'from behind' means starting at their far end and reading back to their start.
  • on each side — It points at the two flanks of a word, the letter standing at each end with the middle left out.

A boxer calling advice from behind the ropes strung along each side of the ring.

The ropes plant a boxing ring in your head, so 'Fighter' reads as a man; it is the wartime aircraft, whose name also belongs to a person of fierce temper.

6 across TOP UP

Increase already high level of canine dedication? (3,2)

Definition: Increase already high level · charade

To top up is to add more to a level that is already part-way there — a refill rather than a first filling.

  • dedication → TO A dedication at the front of a book opens with the word 'To'.
  • canine → PUP A pup is a young canine.

A question about whether a dog's already boundless devotion could be raised any higher.

Split the five letters after the second one and the answer reads as the opening of a dedication addressed to a dog, which is what the question mark is apologising for.

9 across MANNA

God's gift, guy already taken? (5)

Definition: God's gift · charade

Manna is the food God sent the Israelites in the wilderness, so 'God's gift' names it by where it came from.

  • guy → MAN A guy is a man.
  • already taken? → NA Something already taken is not available — N/A on any form.

A dating lament: the perfect fellow turns out to be spoken for.

'God's gift' is the man who thinks himself irresistible in the surface, and in the answer it is the food that fell from heaven for the Israelites.

10 across PAGEANTRY

Display article and essay on piece of paper (9)

Definition: Display · charade

Pageantry is showy display — processions, costume and ceremony staged to be looked at.

  • piece of paper → PAGE A page is a piece of paper.
  • article → AN AN is the indefinite article.
  • and essay → TRY To essay something is to try it — the verb, not the schoolwork.
  • on — In an across clue it names what the earlier pieces rest on, so the sheet comes first and they follow it.

A writer fitting an article and an essay onto a single sheet of paper.

'Essay' hides best of the three: read as a noun it belongs with 'article' in the surface, and as a verb it means to attempt.

11 across TACITLY

You shouldn't have left going into town without saying anything (7)

Definition: without saying anything · charade + container

To do something tacitly is to do it unspoken — agreement understood rather than said aloud.

  • You shouldn't have → TA 'Ta' is British informal for thanks, what you say on being handed a present.
  • town → CITY A city is a town.
  • left → L l = left, the standard abbreviation.
  • going into — It sends the letter before it inside the word named next, rather than alongside it.

A reproach to somebody who set off for town without a word to anyone.

'You shouldn't have' is the polite noise made over a gift, not a telling-off, and the surface exists to make you read it as the telling-off.

13 across OVERHIT

Send the ball long on smash (7)

Definition: Send the ball long · charade

To overhit is to strike the ball harder than intended so it travels too far — sending it long.

  • on → OVER A row on money is a row over money — the two prepositions do the same job.
  • smash → HIT A smash is a hit, on the tennis court and in the charts.

A cricket ball smashed away towards long on.

'Long on' is a real fielding position and the surface leans hard on it; the definition stops at 'long', and the two words after it are separate pieces.

14 across NOISELESSNESS

Silence grunting player getting in top seed's head (13)

Definition: Silence · container + charade

Noiselessness is the state of making no noise at all, which is what 'Silence' names as a noun.

  • top seed's → NOIS The top seed is the No. 1, and 'No. 1's' spells out as NO, the Roman numeral for one, and S.
  • grunting player → SELES Monica Seles, the tennis champion whose loud grunting on court was famous enough to be a story in itself.
  • head → NESS A ness is a headland, which makes 'head' the usual way of asking for it.
  • getting in — It says the player is not tacked on the end but slipped inside the letters already standing there.

A grunting opponent getting under the top seed's skin, and a call for her to be quiet.

Getting in someone's head is the sports-psychology cliché the surface runs on, and it doubles as the literal instruction to go inside.

17 across CHOP AND CHANGE

Two things you get from overpaid butcher show inconsistency (4,3,6)

Definition: show inconsistency · charade

To chop and change is to keep altering your plans, so the phrase means inconsistency in behaviour and cuts nothing at all.

  • butcher → CHOP A chop is the cut of meat you go to a butcher for.
  • Two things you get from → AND Two things named together are joined by 'and', which is the middle word here.
  • overpaid → CHANGE Hand over too much money and you get change back.

A shopper handing the butcher rather more money than the meat costs.

The wordplay is a shopping list rather than a set of instructions — the pieces are simply what the counter hands back, with the joining word supplied by 'two'.

21 across ORINOCO

Venezuelan water company started by several stars switching on (7)

Definition: Venezuelan water · charade + reversal

The Orinoco is one of South America's great rivers, running through Venezuela, so 'Venezuelan water' names it by country and kind.

  • several stars → ORINO Orion is the constellation, several stars taken together, with the ON at its end swapped round.
  • company → CO co = company, as at the end of a firm's name.
  • switching on — 'Switching' asks two letters to trade places and 'on' names exactly which two are to swap.
  • started by — It says the firm has the other piece in front of it, standing at the start.

A Venezuelan water company set up by a group of celebrities.

'Stars' are the celebrity kind in the surface and the astronomical kind in the wordplay — the hunter, the best-known constellation of them all.

22 across PARKOUR

Free running stream swamps shelter during flood (7)

Definition: Free running · container

Parkour is the discipline of crossing city obstacles at a run, and free running is the English name for the same thing.

  • stream → POUR A pour is a stream of liquid.
  • shelter during flood → ARK Noah's ark is the shelter built to ride out the Flood.
  • swamps — To swamp a thing is to close over it entirely, so the shelter ends up inside the water.

A river in spate overwhelming the last shelter left standing.

'Free running' opens the clue looking like water running freely and names the urban sport of vaulting walls and railings at speed.

23 across COLD FRAME

Garden shelter made for Carol's outside being reassembled (4,5)

Definition: Garden shelter · anagram + outer letters

A cold frame is the low glass-topped box a gardener raises seedlings in — a shelter for plants rather than people.

  • made for → MADEFOR anagram fodder, seven letters
  • Carol's → CL The letters at the outside of CAROL, its first and its last.
  • outside — It takes the letters standing at the two ends of the name and leaves the middle where it is.
  • being reassembled — Something reassembled is put back together in a fresh arrangement, which is what happens to these letters.

A shelter built for Carol in the garden, taken to pieces and put back together.

The fodder is hiding as joinery: 'made for' looks like the words holding the sentence up, and it supplies seven of the nine letters.

24 across WASTE

Hearing trouser measurement, lose a lot of weight (5)

Definition: lose a lot of weight · homophone

To waste is to lose flesh and weight through illness or hunger — the sense in 'waste away', not the squandering one.

  • trouser measurement → WASTE The measurement round a pair of trousers is the WAIST, and that sound has a second spelling.
  • Hearing — It asks for the sound of the words rather than their spelling, so you write down what reaches the ear.

Somebody hearing their trouser size read out and resolving to slim.

One sound, two spellings: the tailor's measurement is the WAIST, and the definition wants the other one — to waste is to grow thin, as in wasting away.

26 across MISER

Mize reportedly back on Tour? He's unlikely to chip in (5)

Definition: He's unlikely to chip in · homophone + last letter

A miser hoards his money and will not contribute, so the definition describes him by the thing he refuses to do.

  • Mize → MISE Larry Mize is the American golfer who won the 1987 Masters, and his name said aloud can be written with an S.
  • on Tour → R The letter at the back of 'Tour'.
  • reportedly — A name reported is passed on as a sound, so you write down what you hear rather than how it is spelt.
  • back — It asks for the letter at the rear of the word, the one it finishes on.

A golfer said to be back on tour, and no use at all in a whip-round.

Golf runs through the whole clue: a chip is the short lofted shot played near the green, while chipping in also means paying your share.

27 across ENDANGER

Risk outrage putting finale first (8)

Definition: Risk · charade

To endanger something is to put it at risk — the two verbs take the same object: you endanger, or risk, your health.

  • finale → END A finale is the end of a performance.
  • outrage → ANGER Outrage is anger — the feeling rather than the act that provokes it.
  • putting finale first — It sends the ending round to the front, ahead of the word it would otherwise follow.

A promoter chancing an uproar by moving the finale to the top of the bill.

'Risk' and 'outrage' sit together as though the clue were about a decision certain to annoy people, and only the first of them is the definition.

Down

2 down PENSEE

Shut up and learn, Parisian thought (6)

Definition: Parisian thought · charade

Pensée is French for a thought, which is what 'Parisian' asks for — the same idea in the other language.

  • Shut up → PEN To pen an animal is to shut it up in an enclosure.
  • and learn → SEE To see that something is so is to learn it — 'I see' when the penny drops.

Someone told curtly to be quiet and pay attention.

'Parisian' is the standard signal that the answer is the French word rather than the English one, and the grid quietly drops the accent from pensée.

3 down TOASTIE

Sandwich sample filled with bits separately (7)

Definition: Sandwich · container

A toastie is a toasted sandwich, so 'Sandwich' defines it by the wider class of food it belongs to.

  • sample → TASTE To sample something is to taste it.
  • bits → OI Bits here are binary digits, 0 and 1, and O and I are the letters they are written as.
  • filled with — A filling goes inside its case, so what follows is pushed into the middle of the word named before it.
  • separately — It says the two go in at different points rather than being dropped in together as a pair.

A tasting sample of a sandwich, stuffed with assorted bits.

'Bits' is computing, not crumbs: a bit is a binary digit, and the setter hands over the two of them as the letters they look like.

4 down IMPLY

Suggest taking top off only (5)

Definition: Suggest · deletion

To imply something is to suggest it without stating it — the same verb, stopping one step short of saying it.

  • only → SIMPLY 'Only' in the sense of merely is SIMPLY, and the letter at its top comes away.
  • taking top off — In a down entry the top is the opening letter, and taking it off strips it from the front.

Advice to take the top off and nothing more.

'Top' is not a lid here but the first letter of a word written down the grid, and 'Suggest' stands apart as the definition.

5 down ENGROSSED

Dogs seen swimming across river caught up (9)

Definition: caught up · anagram

To be caught up in a book is to be absorbed in it, which is this word's sense — not caught up as in overtaken.

  • Dogs seen → DOGSSEEN anagram fodder, eight of the nine letters
  • river → R r = river, the abbreviation printed on maps
  • swimming — Something swimming is drifting loose in the water, so its letters will not stay in the order given.
  • across — It has the swim taking in the river, so the river's letter joins the pool being shuffled.

Dogs paddling across a river while somebody watches from the bank.

The definition is the last two words, and 'caught up' means absorbed in something, not snagged on it — which is where the swimming pushes you.

6 down TRAPEZE

Catch close to fence after American's easy swing (7)

Definition: swing · charade + last letter

A trapeze is literally a swing: the bar an acrobat hangs from and swings on, high above the ring.

  • Catch → TRAP A trap catches an animal, and to catch is to trap.
  • American's easy → EZ Americans write easy as E-Z, as on the E-Z Pass toll lanes.
  • close to fence → E The close of 'fence' is the letter it ends on.
  • after — It sets the letter it introduces behind the piece just named, so the fence's letter lands second.

A baseball catch taken near the fence off an American's lazy swing of the bat.

'Swing' is the batter's swing in the surface and a piece of circus apparatus in the answer — a bar hung on two ropes.

7 down PITCHES INTO

Deep hole revolutionary digs for attacks (7,4)

Definition: attacks · charade

To pitch into someone is to set about them, with fists or with words — an attack rather than a mere complaint.

  • Deep hole → PIT A pit is a deep hole.
  • revolutionary → CHE Che Guevara is the crossword's standard revolutionary.
  • digs → SINTO In the slang sense, someone who digs a thing is into it — so 'revolutionary digs' becomes 'Che's into'.

A revolutionary digging himself a deep trench to attack from.

'Digs' looks like the spadework in the surface and is really the sixties slang for being keen on something, which is how three words hand over four pieces.

8 down PEYOTE

Cactus is sort of ecotype that avoids cold (6)

Definition: Cactus · anagram + deletion

Peyote is a small spineless cactus of Mexico and the Texas borderlands, best known for the mescaline in it.

  • ecotype → EOTYPE anagram fodder, once the cold letter has been kept out
  • cold → c = cold, the letter on the tap, and it is the one dropped
  • sort of — Read as an instruction to sort, it asks for the letters put into a different order.
  • avoids — It keeps one letter out of the mix altogether rather than shuffling it in with the others.

A plant described as a variety that cannot stand the cold.

Botanical vocabulary is the disguise: an ecotype is a genuine term for a locally adapted population, sitting there in plain sight to be taken apart.

9 down MATINS

US entreés including tip for table service (6)

Definition: service · container + first letter

Matins is the morning service of the Anglican church, so 'service' names it by the kind of thing it is.

  • US entreés → MAINS In America the entrée is the main course, so American entrées are mains.
  • table → T Nothing looked up here: it is the first letter of the word, taken as its tip.
  • including — What is included is taken inside, so the single letter goes into the middle of the word before it.
  • tip for — A tip is the point a thing starts at, and here that is the letter the following word begins with.

A restaurant bill for American main courses with a tip added for the table service.

'Service' is a church service and not a waiter's: matins is morning prayer, which the restaurant surface is built to keep out of sight.

12 down CHINCHILLAS

Pets Charles keeps at home have a relaxed time (11)

Definition: Pets · container + charade

Chinchillas are South American rodents with famously soft fur, kept in this country as household pets.

  • Charles → CHAS Chas is the familiar short form of Charles.
  • at home → IN Somebody at home is in.
  • have a relaxed time → CHILL To chill is to have a relaxed time.
  • keeps — To keep a thing is to hold on to it, so the name closes around the pieces that follow.

Charles taking it easy indoors with the animals he looks after.

'Keeps' does both jobs at once: Charles looking after animals in the surface, and one word holding others inside it in the wordplay.

15 down EVAPORATE

Old judge under cover with flags raised is dry (9)

Definition: dry · reversal + charade

To dry is what a puddle does when it evaporates — the water leaves as vapour, so the definition names the process by its result.

  • cover with flags → EVAP To cover ground with flags is to PAVE it — flags are flagstones — and the word is then read upwards.
  • Old → O o = old, the standard abbreviation.
  • judge → RATE To rate something is to judge it.
  • raised — In a down entry, raised letters are read bottom to top, so the word arrives the wrong way round.
  • under — It puts the judge lower down the column, which in a down entry means later in the answer.

An elderly judge under a canopy with the flags run up above him.

'Flags' are paving stones, not bunting, and the clue spends its whole surface pointing at the flagpole instead.

16 down HEARSE

Fellow behind vehicle causing tailback, perhaps (6)

Definition: vehicle causing tailback, perhaps · charade

A hearse leads a funeral procession at walking pace, so it is one vehicle apt to cause a tailback — the British word for a long traffic queue.

  • Fellow → HE A fellow is a he.
  • behind → ARSE Somebody's behind is their backside, and this is the blunt British word for it.

A man walking behind a slow vehicle while the traffic piles up.

'Behind' is the noun and not the preposition, which is why the clue reads so smoothly as a fellow standing at the back of something.

18 down ON OFFER

Part-time queen not as expensive (2,5)

Definition: not as expensive · charade

Goods on offer are being sold at a reduced price, which is the shop-window sense of 'not as expensive'.

  • Part-time → ONOFF Part-time work goes on and off rather than running continuously.
  • queen → ER ER = Elizabeth Regina, the crossword's standard queen.

A monarch who works only part of the week, and costs the country less for it.

The definition is a price and not a person: goods on offer are the ones with money knocked off, which the cut-price royal keeps you from seeing.

19 down HARD-WON

Upset since doctor I see is difficult to get hold of (4-3)

Definition: difficult to get hold of · reversal + charade

Something hard-won took real effort to obtain, so it is difficult to get hold of in the sense of difficult to come by.

  • since → NOW 'Now you mention it' and 'since you mention it' do the same job — now can stand for since.
  • doctor → DR dr = doctor, the standard title.
  • I see → AH 'Ah' is the noise you make when you see the point.
  • Upset — Upset means tipped over, and in a down entry that writes the pieces from the bottom of the column up.

A patient complaining that their own doctor is never available.

'Upset' reads as the patient's mood and works as the instruction to turn three small pieces over.

20 down DOTCOM

Internet-based catastrophe stopped by odd bits of tech (6)

Definition: Internet-based · container + alternate letters

A dotcom is a company that trades on the internet, named after the .com at the end of its web address.

  • catastrophe → DOOM Doom is catastrophe — the fate rather than the event.
  • tech → TC The letters in the odd positions of the word, its first and third.
  • stopped by — Being stopped by something is being blocked in mid-flow, so those letters sit inside the word.
  • odd bits of — 'Odd' counts positions rather than describing oddity: the first and third letters, taken as they come.

An online catastrophe halted by a few stray bits of technology.

The definition is the first word and reads as part of the disaster story; the label attaches to any firm whose business address ends in .com.

22 down PLEAD

Playing padel, say in court? (5)

Definition: say in court? · anagram

To plead is to state your case in court — guilty or not guilty is the first thing a defendant says there.

  • padel → PADEL anagram fodder — padel is the racquet sport played on a small glass-walled court
  • Playing — Playing at a thing is not doing it straight, so the letters come out in some other order.

A game of padel under way on court.

'Say' looks as though it is flagging an example or a sound-alike, and it belongs to the definition: by the last word the court is a court of law.
