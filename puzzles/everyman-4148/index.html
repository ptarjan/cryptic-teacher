1 across ROME

Starters of rancid octopus given to Everyman in city on the Tiber (4)

Definition: city on the Tiber · charade + first letters

Rome stands on the Tiber, which runs through the middle of it, so the phrase picks out one city and no other.

  • rancid octopus → RO the opening letters of the two words, taken in the order the clue lists them
  • Everyman → ME the setter's own byline: a crossword writer referring to himself is always ME
  • Starters of — A starter is the course that comes first, so only the opening letter of each word beside it is wanted.
  • given to — What is given to somebody is handed straight over, so the letters just found sit against the ones that follow.

Somebody hands Everyman a starter of rancid octopus while he is out in an Italian city.

Everyman is the name printed above the grid every Sunday, and a setter naming himself is a standing convention worth learning: it is two letters, never more.

3 across ODD NUMBERS

'19' is among these unusual songs (3,7)

Definition: '19' is among these · double definition

The ones not divisible by two are where 19 sits, and 'number' doubles as the showbiz word for a song, so both halves land on the same phrase.

  • '19' is among these → nineteen belongs to the set of whole numbers that will not divide by two
  • unusual songs → in showbusiness a song performed in a set is a number, and the first word is an adjective for out of the ordinary

A claim that a track called '19' belongs among some peculiar songs.

Two definitions with nothing between them, and the second is the harder: 'number' for a song is theatre slang, from the numbered items on a printed bill.

9 across SAGE

Primarily: Solomon / astute / green / edible? (4)

Definition: Primarily: Solomon / astute / green / edible? · &lit + first letters

A sage is a wise elder — Solomon being the type of one — and also the grey-green herb you can cook with, so every item on the list fits.

  • Solomon → S the opening letter of the biblical king's name
  • astute → A the opening letter of the word for shrewd
  • green → G the opening letter of the colour
  • edible → E the opening letter of the word for fit to eat
  • Primarily — Primarily means first and foremost, so what counts is the letter standing at the head of each item listed after it.

A checklist of qualities being ticked off one by one: biblical wisdom, shrewdness, greenness, and fit to eat.

The list is in two halves that never mix: Solomon and astute point at the wise man, green and edible at the herb, and the initials spell both at once.

10 across DAMASK ROSE

Touring Odessa, Mark makes bloomer (6,4)

Definition: bloomer · anagram

A damask rose is the old, heavily scented rose grown for attar, so 'bloomer' defines it by the crossword's joke sense: a thing that blooms.

  • Odessa, Mark → ODESSAMARK ten letters of raw material, the Black Sea port and the name together
  • Touring — A tour wanders from place to place with no fixed route, so the letters it is applied to end up somewhere other than where they started.

Mark drops a clanger while on a sightseeing tour of Odessa.

'Bloomer' means a blunder in British slang, which is exactly what a tourist called Mark would be making, and crosswords use it for anything that flowers.

12 across TRAVEL GUIDE

Spooner's talk of judge's mallet and what he did in book (6,5)

Definition: book · spoonerism

A travel guide is the book you carry round a foreign city to be steered about it, so 'book' names it by class — one kind among many.

  • judge's mallet → TRAVEL the mallet a judge bangs for order is a gavel, and it takes the opening noise of the other word in exchange
  • what he did → GUIDE what a judge did to the case is tried, and handed the hard g of the mallet word the vowel you hear lengthens with it
  • Spooner's talk of — Spooner was the Oxford don remembered for trading the opening sounds of two words, so talk of his is those two things said with their front noises exchanged.

A book recounting a judge's hammer and the verdict he handed down.

The trap is reading 'in book' as more courtroom furniture; it stands apart from the judge altogether and is the definition.

15 across LEAPT AT

Eagerly accepted some suitable - apt - attributions (5,2)

Definition: Eagerly accepted · hidden word

To leap at an offer is to take it without a second's hesitation, so 'eagerly accepted' is the same idea in the same past tense.

  • suitable - apt - attributions → LEAPTAT seven consecutive letters, beginning at the seventh letter of the first word and stopping two letters into the last
  • some — 'Some' asks for a part of what follows rather than all of it, and the part named is already sitting there in order.

Someone agrees that a set of attributions is suitable and apt.

The two dashes look like an aside dropped in for emphasis, and their real job is to break up a run of letters — punctuation counts for nothing once you close the gaps.

16 across HIDEOUT

Den's extreme uncertainty declared (7)

Definition: Den · homophone

A hideout is the place somebody holes up to avoid being found, and a den is the same thing: an animal's or a criminal's private lair.

  • extreme uncertainty → HIDEOUT extreme is high and uncertainty is doubt; run the two together and the same noise divides into different words
  • declared — To declare something is to say it out loud, so what matters about the words beside it is the noise they make together.

Den announcing that he has never been more unsure about anything.

'Den's' looks possessive, as though the man owned the uncertainty, and the apostrophe-s is standing in for 'is' — the definition is that first word on its own.

17 across NATURAL

Organic frank (7)

Definition: Organic · double definition

Natural covers both senses: produced by nature rather than artifice, and, of a person, unaffected and candid, which is what frank means.

  • Organic → grown or occurring without human interference, the sense a food label is reaching for
  • frank → open and unaffected in manner, saying what you actually think without dressing it up

A hot dog being advertised as organic.

Two senses of one adjective sit side by side with no join at all, and 'frank' pulls hard towards the sausage counter — the word for candour is the one wanted.

19 across CAMPARI

I criticise Scotsman that's sent back drink (7)

Definition: drink · charade + reversal

Campari is the bitter red Italian aperitif, so 'drink' defines it by class: one named bottle standing in for the category.

  • Scotsman → MAC Mac is the crossword's stock Scotsman, from the Mac- that opens so many Scottish surnames, and the turn brings it to the front
  • criticise → RAP to rap somebody is to criticise them sharply, as in a rap on the knuckles
  • I → I the pronoun stands for itself, one letter, and the turn sends it to the tail
  • sent back — What is sent back returns the way it came, and in an across entry that means reading the assembled letters from the right-hand end.

The speaker tells off a Scot who has sent his drink back to the bar.

'Sent back' is the whole picture on the surface — a glass returned across the counter — and underneath it is the instruction that turns everything before 'drink' round.

20 across TURN THE TIDE

How crossword setter can clue last across answer (4,3,4)

Definition: How crossword setter can clue last across answer · cryptic definition

The phrase normally means reversing the run of events; here it is taken at face value as an instruction, and reversing that last noun spells 26 across.

  • How crossword setter can clue → the words are not describing the answer, they are quoting it: what goes in the grid is a clue somebody could actually write
  • last across answer → the final across light in this grid is 26, four letters long, and turning those four letters round is what the quoted instruction asks for

A note on how a setter might go about wording a clue for the puzzle's final across entry.

Everyman is clueing his own grid here: read the entry as an instruction and obey it, and out comes 26 across, EDIT. Nothing separates into wordplay because the answer is the wordplay.

23 across THE BELL JAR

Sporting beret, lead character in Jerry Hall novel (3,4,3)

Definition: novel · anagram + first letter

The Bell Jar is Sylvia Plath's only novel, so 'novel' defines it by class: one title standing in for the category.

  • beret → BERET five letters of raw material, the flat French cap
  • Jerry → J the letter the name starts with, joining the material
  • Hall → HALL four more letters of material, the surname going into the shuffle whole
  • Sporting — To sport is to frolic in the older sense of the word, and letters at play do not stay in their line.
  • lead character in — Character here means a written symbol rather than a person, so the lead one is simply the letter at the front of the name.

Jerry Hall in a beret, taking the leading role in something.

Jerry Hall is a model, and her name is in the clue only to be taken apart — Sylvia Plath wrote the book, and wrote no other.

24 across UNDO

Cancel larks before fuss - never starting (4)

Definition: Cancel · deletion + charade

To undo something is to reverse what was done to it — an order, a stitch, a keystroke — which is exactly what cancelling it means.

  • larks → UN larks are fun, and that word loses the F it opens with
  • fuss → DO a fuss is an ado, beheaded of its A in the same way
  • before — 'Before' fixes the order of the two pieces, putting the one it follows in front of the one it introduces.
  • never starting — A word that never starts never gets its first letter out, so each word here arrives with its head missing.

Somebody calling off the jokes before the row can even get going.

One instruction is doing duty twice: it sits at the end of the clue and reaches back over both words, which is the economy that makes a four-letter clue possible.

25 across BREATHIEST

One taking a temperature in check on driver sounding most panicked (10)

Definition: sounding most panicked · substitution

A breathy voice is one you can hear the breath in, so the breathiest is the one that sounds most out of breath — panic made audible.

  • check on driver → BREATHTEST the roadside check the police make on a driver they suspect of drinking is a breath test
  • One → I I is the Roman numeral for one, and it is the letter moving in
  • a temperature → T T is the standard abbreviation for temperature, and this is the letter that makes way for it
  • taking — Taking a thing means having it off somebody, so the letter named moves into the place the other one is giving up.

A nurse taking somebody's temperature while the police check a driver at the roadside.

T for temperature and I for one are two of the conventions worth owning outright, and here the second simply moves into the space the first vacates.

26 across EDIT

Alter order - Conservative storms off (4)

Definition: Alter · deletion

To edit a text is to alter it — cut, change, correct — so the two verbs stand in for each other in the same infinitive form.

  • order → EDICT an order handed down by a ruler is an edict, five letters of raw material
  • Conservative → C C is the standard abbreviation for the Conservative party, and it is the letter that walks out
  • storms off — Somebody who storms off walks out in a temper, so the letter named beside it leaves the word it was part of.

A Conservative walking out in a huff over a change to the running order.

'Order' meaning edict is the sense crosswords lean on hardest. This is also the grid's last across answer, which is exactly what 20 across has been talking about.

1 down RESETTLING

Finding new place for abandoned letterings (10)

Definition: Finding new place for · anagram

To resettle people is to find them a new place to live, so the definition is the plain gerund of that verb.

  • letterings → LETTERINGS ten letters of raw material, exactly the count the enumeration asks for
  • abandoned — Abandoned here is the sense of unrestrained rather than deserted, and letters let go of like that will not stay as they were given.

Somebody is looking for somewhere to put signage nobody wants any more.

'Abandoned' reads as part of the picture — lettering left behind by whoever put it up — and it is the instruction instead.

2 down MAGNA CARTA

John okayed this lively armagnac, thank you (5,5)

Definition: John okayed this · anagram

Magna Carta is the charter John put his seal to in 1215, so the definition names the document by the one act that created it.

  • armagnac → ARMAGNAC eight letters of raw material, the brandy from Gascony
  • thank you → TA ta is the everyday British thank-you, two more letters joining the shuffle
  • lively — Something lively will not sit still, so the material next to it refuses to keep the order it arrived in.

A toast recommending a spirited armagnac that John approved of, with thanks.

King John was forced to seal the charter by his barons at Runnymede in 1215, and the surface buries that history under a round of brandy.

4 down DRAUGHT

Type of ale that's not ready for public, you say? (7)

Definition: Type of ale · homophone

Draught ale is beer drawn from the cask rather than poured from a bottle, so the definition names it by the way it is served.

  • not ready for public → DRAUGHT a version of a document not yet released is a draft, and that noise has a second, older spelling in the beer trade
  • you say — What you say is heard rather than read, so the words beside it are judged by their noise and not their spelling.

A beer that has not been signed off for sale to the public yet.

British English keeps both spellings apart by meaning — the document is a draft, the beer and the cold air through a door are not — while the sound stays identical.

5 down NEARISH

A little burn - ear is hot - I'm not far away (7)

Definition: not far away · hidden word

Nearish is 'near' softened by a vague ending, so it means close by without committing to how close — not far away.

  • burn - ear is hot → NEARISH seven consecutive letters, starting at the fourth letter of the first word and stopping one letter into the last
  • A little — A little of something is a portion of it, not the whole, and a portion lifted out whole keeps its letters standing in place.

Someone reports a small burn and a hot ear, and adds that they are close by.

The dashes are punctuation to read straight through, and the definition is quietly telling the truth: what you are looking for really is sitting a couple of words away.

6 down MAKE ENDS MEET

Live within budget as you may do when tying the knot (4,4,4)

Definition: Live within budget · double definition

The phrase means keeping spending inside income, and taken word by word it describes bringing two ends together, which is what a knot does.

  • Live within budget → the household sense: keeping what goes out no larger than what comes in
  • as you may do when tying the knot → read literally instead — the two loose ends of a rope are brought together before the knot can be pulled tight

A couple managing on a tight budget in the run-up to their wedding.

'Tying the knot' is the idiom for marrying, which is what a couple watching their money would be saving for, and the sense wanted is the rope one.

7 down EROS

Red nose displayed - not Dancer, oddly - Cupid's counterpart (4)

Definition: Cupid's counterpart · anagram + deletion

Eros is the Greek god of love and desire; Cupid is the Roman god who does the identical job, which is what makes one the other's counterpart.

  • Red nose → REDNOSE seven letters of raw material, Rudolph's famous feature
  • Dancer → the letters in its odd positions are D, N and E, and those three come out of the material before anything is rearranged
  • displayed — To display goods is to lay them out afresh on the counter, so the letters left over are set out in a new arrangement.
  • not — 'Not' is the instruction to leave something out: the letters named next are missing from the material rather than added to it.
  • oddly — 'Oddly' counts positions rather than describing manner — the first, third and fifth letters of the name beside it.

Rudolph's red nose is on show, and Dancer is nowhere to be seen among the team.

The reindeer team is the misdirection, and Cupid is one of them too — but the definition wants the god the Romans borrowed from the Greeks, not the sleigh.

8 down SEEP

Spot penny in drain (4)

Definition: drain · charade

To seep is to leak away little by little, and 'drain' carries the same slow-escape sense when it is used as a verb rather than a noun.

  • Spot → SEE to spot somebody in a crowd is to see them, one verb for the other
  • penny → P p is how British prices write pence, as in fifty p

Catching sight of a coin lying in a roadside drain.

'Drain' is the gutter the coin has fallen into on the surface, and the definition wants the verb — which is the whole difference between a picture and a parse.

11 down BEAT A RETREAT

Fled, exhausted by Australian yoga venue? (4,1,7)

Definition: Fled · charade

To beat a retreat is to leave in a hurry rather than stand your ground, which is what 'fled' says, and both are past tense.

  • exhausted → BEAT somebody exhausted is beat, the informal adjective rather than the verb
  • Australian → A A is the standard abbreviation for Australia and Australian
  • yoga venue → RETREAT a weekend of yoga is held at a retreat, a place people withdraw to

Somebody worn out heads off to a yoga retreat in Australia.

Beating a retreat was once a literal drum call ordering soldiers back to camp at nightfall, and the wellness holiday on the surface keeps that soldiering out of sight.

13 down NON-ALIGNED

Neutral one landing in trouble (3-7)

Definition: Neutral · anagram

A non-aligned country has joined neither of two opposing camps, which is precisely what 'neutral' means in the diplomatic sense of the word.

  • one landing → ONELANDING ten letters of raw material, the number and the touchdown together
  • in trouble — Something in trouble is in disarray, and letters in disarray do not stay standing in the order they were handed over.

A pilot in difficulties bringing an aircraft down.

The Non-Aligned Movement was the Cold War bloc of countries that refused to take either side, so the word being defined is a political one, not an aircraft's attitude.

14 down STRIKES OUT

Gets rid of source of scooters - vehicles banned! (7,3)

Definition: Gets rid of · charade + first letter

To strike out a line is to score it through so it no longer counts, so 'gets rid of' matches in sense and in the same third-person form.

  • scooters → S the letter the word begins with, and nothing else of it is used
  • vehicles → TRIKES trikes are three-wheeled vehicles, the everyday clipping of tricycles
  • banned → OUT something banned is out — ruled inadmissible, not allowed near the place
  • source of — A river's source is the point it starts from, so the word beside it gives up only the letter it begins with.

A crackdown that starts with the scooters and ends with vehicles banned altogether.

'Vehicles banned' looks like one phrase from a traffic notice, and it is two separate pieces of wordplay sitting next to each other.

18 down LORELEI

Look - Lake Erie - staggering figure on rocks (7)

Definition: figure on rocks · charade + anagram

The Lorelei is the siren of German legend who sits on a rock above the Rhine luring sailors to wreck, so the definition is literally true of her.

  • Look → LO lo is the old word for look, kept alive in 'lo and behold'
  • Lake Erie → RELEI L is the abbreviation for lake, and those five letters are shaken into a fresh order
  • staggering — Somebody staggering cannot walk a straight line, so the letters beside it lurch out of the order they were in.

An unsteady figure spotted on the rocks beside Lake Erie.

The Great Lake is a decoy: the rock that matters is on the Rhine, where a siren's singing was said to draw boatmen onto the shallows below.

19 down CUTLASS

Definition: sword · charade

A cutlass is the short, broad-bladed sword sailors carried, so 'sword' defines it by class — one particular kind of blade.

  • Do → CUT a do is a hairstyle, and so is a cut — the same salon word twice over
  • miss → LASS a lass is a girl, which is what miss means when it is not being used as a title

An imperative urging somebody to give the sword a miss.

'Do' looks like the helping verb propping up that instruction, and it is a noun here — the hairdresser's word for what has been done to your head.

21 down STAB

Small price for drinks - that's a guess (4)

Definition: a guess · charade

A stab at something is an attempt made more in hope than in knowledge, so 'a guess' is the idiom behind 'have a stab at it'.

  • Small → S S is the size printed on a clothing label, the standard shorthand
  • price for drinks → TAB a tab is the running bill a bar keeps for you until you settle up at the end

Drinks going cheap, at a rough estimate.

'Small price' reads as one phrase about a bargain, and the split falls between its two words — which is where clues of four letters usually hide their join.

22 down MEME

Committee member recalled enthralling viral content (4)

Definition: viral content · hidden word + reversal

A meme is a joke image or clip passed from person to person online, which is what 'viral content' names — material that spreads by being shared.

  • Committee member → MEME four consecutive letters read backwards, starting at the m in the middle of the second word and running into the tail of the first
  • recalled — Something recalled is called back the way it went out, so the letters found here are read in the opposite direction.
  • enthralling — To enthral is to hold somebody captive, and the words beside it are holding the letters wanted captive inside themselves.

A member of a committee remembered for holding a room spellbound.

'Recalled' looks like a word about memory, which fits a committee minute perfectly, and it is telling you which way to read.
