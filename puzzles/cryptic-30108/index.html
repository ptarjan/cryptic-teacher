1 across FLATTENED

Coffee back in canteen, provided cups laid out (9)

Definition: laid out · container + last letter

laid out → FLATTENED: a boxer laid out on the canvas has been flattened, the knocked-flat sense rather than the neatly-arranged one.

  • provided → FED to provide for someone is to feed them; three letters that will be opened up
  • Coffee → LATTE the milky coffee everyone orders, five letters going straight in
  • back in canteen → N the letter at the back of the word canteen, the one it ends on
  • back — The back of a word is its far end, so canteen gives up only the letter it finishes on.
  • cups — A cup holds whatever is put in it, so the piece named beside it closes right around the others.

Coffee goes back to the canteen, where the cups have been set out ready.

'Laid out' reads as a line about table settings, which is what the canteen and the cups are for, and it is the boxing sense instead. Providing for someone is feeding them.

8 across PIE CRUST

One’s filled with various pictures (3,5)

Definition: One’s filled · anagram

One’s filled → PIE CRUST: the pastry case is the thing a filling goes into, so the clue names it by the job it does.

  • pictures → PICTURES eight letters of fodder, and the enumeration splits them three and five
  • various — Various things differ from one another, so the letters beside it are not to be taken in the arrangement shown.

An album or a frame is filled with an assortment of photographs.

'Pictures' wants to be the definition — the cinema, a wall of photographs — and it is the letters. What gets filled here is pastry, and the clue never says so.

9 across TRIVIA

Rat, I suspect, has gobbled six trifles (6)

Definition: trifles · anagram + container

trifles → TRIVIA: trivia are the small unimportant details of a subject, exactly what trifles means when it is not a dessert.

  • Rat, I → TRIA four letters shaken up before anything is let inside them
  • six → VI six written as a Roman numeral
  • suspect — Something suspect is not what it claims to be, and neither is the order these four letters claim.
  • has gobbled — To gobble is to swallow whole, so the letters in front close over the piece that follows.

A rat is suspected of having eaten six trifles from the fridge.

'Trifles' is the definition, and the surface makes it the pudding a rat would go for. The sense wanted is the other one: things too small to matter.

10 across SLOUGH

Shed in Berkshire town (6)

Definition: Shed · double definition

Shed → SLOUGH: a snake sloughs its skin, and the same spelling names the Berkshire town John Betjeman wrote his rudest poem about.

  • Shed → the verb a snake performs on its old skin, casting it off whole
  • Berkshire town → the town west of London on the road out to Windsor, in the county of Berkshire

Something has been shed somewhere in a town in Berkshire.

The two senses are said differently — the verb rhymes with 'rough', the town with 'plough' — which is how the join passes as one ordinary sentence.

11 across BENJAMIN SPOCK

US author referencing issue with mountain, page covered by explanation for sticky foot? (8,5)

Definition: US author referencing issue · charade + container

US author referencing issue → BENJAMIN SPOCK: 'issue' means children, and his Baby and Child Care told American parents how to bring theirs up.

  • mountain → BEN ben is the Scottish word for a mountain — Ben Nevis is the highest of them
  • explanation for sticky foot? → JAMINSOCK jam in a sock would certainly account for a foot that sticks, and the three words are taken exactly as they read
  • page → P p is the standard abbreviation for page, the one used in footnotes
  • covered by — Something covered by another thing lies underneath it, out of sight, so that single letter is buried inside the words that follow.

An American writer is cited alongside a mountain and a page explaining how somebody's foot got sticky.

'Issue' is the old word for offspring, and that is what this doctor's famous book was about — so the definition describes the man rather than just naming his nationality.

12 across STATIC

Rubbish charges thus frozen (6)

Definition: frozen · container

frozen → STATIC: something frozen has stopped moving, and static means standing still, nothing to do with the cold.

  • thus → SIC sic is Latin for thus, the word bracketed into a quotation to show it is copied exactly
  • Rubbish → TAT tat is British for cheap worthless stuff, the junk at the end of a market stall
  • charges — You charge a glass by filling it, so the word in front is packed inside the piece that follows.

A council headline: the charges for taking rubbish away have been held at last year's price.

Read as a headline, 'charges' is what the council bills you and 'frozen' is what happens to a fee. The instruction hiding inside it is the other charging, filling a glass to the brim.

15 across TUTORIAL

Class nuisance, out unfortunately for detention (8)

Definition: Class · anagram

Class → TUTORIAL: a tutorial is a teaching session for one student or a few, so it is a class in the sense of a lesson.

  • nuisance → TRIAL a trial is a person or thing that wears you down — 'that child is a trial'
  • out → OUT the clue's own three letters join the fodder as they stand
  • for detention → surface only: it says where the nuisance is being sent and hands over no letters
  • unfortunately — When things fall out unfortunately they do not land where they were meant to, and neither do these letters.

A disruptive pupil is sent out of the room and given detention.

'Class nuisance' reads as one phrase, the pest at the back of the room, and the setter has cut it in half: the first word is the definition and the second is letters.

16 across UNIRONED

Red upset with union awaiting the press? (8)

Definition: awaiting the press? · anagram

awaiting the press? → UNIRONED: a shirt still waiting for the iron has not been pressed, so the press here is the flat hot one.

  • Red → RED three letters of fodder, sitting in front of the instruction
  • union → UNION five more letters of fodder, taken exactly as spelt
  • upset — Upsetting something tips its contents out anyhow, so the letters land in an order of their own.

An angry socialist has fallen out with his union and is waiting for the newspapers to call.

'The press' is journalists all the way through the surface — a Red at odds with his union is a front page — and the definition wants the household appliance instead.

19 across DITHER

Twitter feed, it heralding gulps? (6)

Definition: Twitter · hidden word

Twitter → DITHER: to be in a twitter and to be in a dither name the same jittery state, unable to settle or decide.

  • feed, it heralding → DITHER run the three words together and six consecutive letters, starting three in, are the ones wanted
  • gulps — To gulp is to swallow something whole, and a word swallowed by its neighbours is sitting inside them unbroken.

A social-media feed that seems to be announcing a series of nervous swallows.

'Twitter' is the old noun here: being all of a twitter is being in a flutter of nerves. The birdsong and the website are both decoys, and so is reading 'gulps' as a definition.

21 across OFFSHORE ACCOUNT

A less taxing sailors’ yarn? (8,7)

Definition: A less taxing sailors’ yarn? · cryptic definition

A less taxing sailors’ yarn? → OFFSHORE ACCOUNT: an account is a yarn, and offshore is both out at sea and safely out of the taxman's reach.

  • A less taxing → the surface promises something easier going; the other reading is literal, money parked where the taxman cannot reach it
  • sailors’ yarn? → a yarn is a story, and the story a sailor tells is told out at sea, well away from the shore

An old salt spins a tall tale that is easier going than most of his.

'Taxing' swings between two meanings: demanding, and to do with tax. Once it lands on the second, the sailors stop being storytellers and start meaning anything out beyond the coast.

22 across SHIRTY

Ticked off – as top? (6)

Definition: Ticked off · double definition

Ticked off → SHIRTY: it is the annoyed sense, and 'as top?' reads the same word as shirt-like, which is where the adjective came from.

  • Ticked off → the slang sense: annoyed, needled, in a huff about something
  • as top? → like a particular kind of top — the one with a collar and cuffs — with a -y stuck on the end

A telling-off, followed by a question about somebody's choice of top.

'Ticked off' has three senses — crossed off a list, told off, annoyed — and only the last one fits. The second half is the setter building an adjective out of a garment.

25 across SHOCKING

Odious pipe, with joint to inhale (8)

Definition: Odious · container

Odious → SHOCKING: something odious gives offence, and shocking is that offence at full strength, as in 'shocking behaviour'.

  • pipe → SING to pipe up is to sing out; four letters about to be opened
  • joint → HOCK the hock is the joint in an animal's hind leg, and a hock of ham is the cut around it
  • to inhale — Inhaling draws something right inside you, so the piece named goes into the middle of the other one.

A revolting pipe is passed round along with a joint to smoke.

The surface is all smoking — a pipe, a joint, inhaling — and not one of those senses is wanted. The joint comes from a butcher, and to pipe is to sing.

26 across APSE

Recess, politicians on vacation in Adelaide spewing guts (4)

Definition: Recess · container + outer letters

Recess → APSE: an apse is the vaulted recess at the east end of a church, so this is the architectural sense of the word.

  • Adelaide spewing guts → AE the Australian city keeps only its first and last letters once the middle is gone
  • politicians on vacation → PS the word politicians emptied of everything between its first and last letters
  • on vacation — A word on vacation is emptied out like a house for the summer, keeping only the letters at either end.
  • in — Being in a place is being inside it, so that pair of letters sits within the pair that follows.
  • spewing guts — Spewing your guts is bringing up everything inside, so the middle of the word goes and the outsides stay.

Politicians on holiday in Adelaide are being noisily sick.

Two words are hollowed out by the same trick, and the clue says it twice in different language: one goes on holiday, the other is sick. Both keep nothing but their outsides.

27 across SPARKIEST

Short continental break where plonk guzzled – and most bubbly? (9)

Definition: most bubbly? · container + deletion

most bubbly? → SPARKIEST: a sparky person fizzes with energy, and the superlative picks out the one who bubbles most of all.

  • Short continental break → SIEST the afternoon nap taken all over southern Europe, with its final letter cut off
  • plonk guzzled → PARK cheap wine gets drunk out of doors, and the public park is where that happens
  • Short — Short means cut off before the end, so the word beside it arrives one letter light.
  • where — It marks the spot: the place named next sits inside the word that has just been shortened.

A quick holiday in Europe where the cheap wine goes down fast.

'Continental break' reads as a weekend in Europe, and the break wanted is the one Spain takes after lunch. 'Most' belongs to the definition, supplying its superlative ending.

1 down FRILL

Flounce and kick down Whitechapel Road? (5)

Definition: Flounce · homophone

Flounce → FRILL: a flounce is the gathered strip of material sewn round a skirt, which is exactly what a frill is.

  • kick → THRILL a kick is a thrill, the jolt of excitement you get from something
  • down Whitechapel Road? → FRILL say that word in the East End and the 'th' becomes an 'f', which is spelt differently
  • down Whitechapel Road? — Whitechapel Road runs through London's East End, so the word is being said in a Cockney accent, and an opening 'th' comes out of a Cockney mouth as an 'f'.

Someone storms off, aiming a kick as they go down an East End street.

'Flounce' reads as the verb, somebody stomping away in a huff, and it is the noun: a strip of gathered fabric. Whitechapel Road names an accent, not a place in the answer.

3 down TRUTH SOCIAL

Leader on laptop behind it, our chats fallacious? (5,6)

Definition: Leader on laptop behind it, our chats fallacious? · anagram + first letter + &lit

Read straight, the clue describes Donald Trump's own network: he is behind it, the posts there are notoriously untrue, and the name says the opposite.

  • it, our chats → ITOURCHATS ten letters of fodder, taken from three ordinary words
  • Leader on laptop → L the letter laptop leads with, dropped in at the back of the queue
  • Leader — The leader of a word is the letter it starts on, so laptop hands over its opening letter and nothing else.
  • behind — Whatever is behind the rest comes last, so that single letter waits at the end of the assembly.
  • fallacious — What is fallacious cannot be trusted, and letters you cannot trust are not standing in the order they were handed to you.

A politician sits behind a laptop while the conversations the rest of us have there turn out to be untrue.

Every word does two jobs: read straight the sentence describes the site, and read as instructions 'Leader' stops being a politician and becomes the letter laptop starts on.

4 down NOT A BIT

Hardly what conductor may seemingly have done with it (3,1,3)

Definition: Hardly · anagram

Hardly → NOT A BIT: 'did you mind? Not a bit' is a flat denial, the same as saying you hardly minded at all.

  • what conductor → BATON the slim stick a conductor holds; the clue wants the thing, not the man
  • it → IT the clue's own two letters join the fodder as they stand
  • may seemingly have done with — A conductor's stick spends the whole concert being waved around, so whatever he seemingly did with it leaves those letters in no fixed order.

A conductor appears to have done something peculiar with his baton.

The word to shuffle is never written down: 'conductor' asks you to supply the stick yourself before there are any letters to work with. 'Hardly' is the definition, tucked in at the front.

5 down DETONATED

Triggered by old boarding school? On the contrary (9)

Definition: Triggered · container

Triggered → DETONATED: a charge that has been triggered has gone off, the literal explosive sense sitting underneath today's figurative one.

  • old → DATED a dated jacket is an old one; five letters that open up to let something in
  • school → ETON Eton, the boarding school near Windsor, is the school crosswords reach for first
  • boarding — Boarding a train is climbing inside it, so one piece goes in among the letters of another.
  • On the contrary — It tells you the two pieces swap roles: what looked like the passenger turns out to be the vehicle.

Somebody is set off by memories of the boarding school they were sent to years ago.

Read as it stands, the old thing climbs into the school; 'On the contrary' turns that round, so the school goes inside the old one. Being 'triggered' is the modern sense of a very old word.

6 down THIN AIR

Upbringing happy, good day to hide – nowhere to disappear? (4,3)

Definition: nowhere to disappear? · reversal + container

nowhere to disappear? → THIN AIR: things that vanish are said to vanish into thin air, the idiom's name for the nowhere they go to.

  • happy → TNAIR riant is a word for cheerful, borrowed from the French for laughing, and here it is read upwards
  • good day → HI the greeting you give someone, two letters, tucked into the middle
  • Upbringing — Bringing something up a down clue writes it from the bottom towards the top, so those letters arrive back to front.
  • to hide — Hiding a thing puts it out of sight inside something bigger, and the letters already assembled are the hiding place.

Someone remembers a happy childhood, then decides today is a good day to keep out of sight.

'Upbringing' reads as a noun about childhood and is really an instruction: in a down clue, bringing letters up means writing them from the bottom. Riant is rare enough to be worth keeping.

7 down ELIMINATE

Clear I am at home when opening lift (9)

Definition: Clear · container

Clear → ELIMINATE: clearing a room of people is getting rid of them, and getting rid of something altogether is what the verb means.

  • lift → ELATE to lift someone's spirits is to elate them; five letters about to be prised apart
  • I am → IM I am shortened to I'm, with the apostrophe dropped
  • at home → IN someone at home is in, the crossword's most-used two letters
  • opening — To open something is to make a gap in it, and the gap is where the other letters are pushed in.

Someone announces that they are indoors just as the lift doors open.

'Lift' wants to be the elevator the surface is standing in, and it is the verb: to lift someone is to elate them. 'At home' for IN is worth learning once and keeping.

13 down TIN OF SOUP

Subject of pop artist put on, if so surrealistic? (3,2,4)

Definition: Subject of pop artist · anagram

Subject of pop artist → TIN OF SOUP: Warhol's Campbell's Soup Cans made a supermarket tin the most painted object of the movement.

  • put on, if so → PUTONIFSO nine letters of fodder, and the enumeration breaks them three, two and four
  • surrealistic — A surrealist painting puts familiar things in impossible arrangements, which is what has happened to these letters.

A gallery-goer wonders whether the pop artist's chosen subject, once hung, counts as surreal.

Pop art's most famous subject is Andy Warhol's Campbell's cans, silkscreened over and over. Surrealism is a different movement altogether, which is why the word passes as description.

14 down CANOODLES

Vessel requiring a load gets it on (9)

Definition: gets it on · charade

gets it on → CANOODLES: to canoodle is to cuddle and kiss, the affectionate end of what 'getting it on' covers.

  • Vessel → CAN a tin is a vessel, three letters for the thing the beans come in
  • a load → OODLES oodles of something is loads of it

A ship that needs cargo aboard before it can get going.

'Vessel' is placed to look like a ship waiting for its load, and the tin on the shelf is the one wanted. The definition is the modern phrase for a couple getting amorous.

17 down RESPITE

Breather touching on venom (7)

Definition: Breather · charade

Breather → RESPITE: a breather is a short pause to get your breath back, and respite is that same pause seen as relief.

  • touching on → RE re means on the subject of, the word at the head of a memo
  • venom → SPITE spite is venom in the figurative sense, the poison in what somebody says

A short break in an argument that keeps circling back to something poisonous.

'Breather' looks like the thing doing the breathing — a creature, a diver — and it is the pause you take. The venom is not a snake's but the human kind.

18 down DEEP-SEA

A shipmate’s last race having capsized on the ocean (4-3)

Definition: on the ocean · charade + last letter + reversal

on the ocean → DEEP-SEA: deep-sea fishing and deep-sea diving both happen far out on the open ocean rather than off the shore.

  • A → A the clue's first word, one letter taken as it stands
  • shipmate’s last → E the letter shipmate ends on
  • race → SPEED to race along is to speed; these five join the two above before the whole lot goes over
  • last — The last of a word is the letter it ends on, so shipmate gives up its final letter alone.
  • having capsized — A capsized boat has turned right over, and in a down clue that means these letters are read from the bottom up.

A sailor's final race ends with the boat going over in open water.

The whole sentence reads as a sailing report, which is how 'capsized' passes for narrative rather than the instruction it is. The answer is the adjective, as in deep-sea fishing.

20 down TWINKLE TOES

Skeleton with less foot to shake about, scintillating dancer? (11)

Definition: scintillating dancer? · anagram + deletion

scintillating dancer? → TWINKLE TOES: twinkletoes is what you call someone light and quick on their feet, and to twinkle is to scintillate.

  • Skeleton → SKELETON eight letters of fodder, taken whole
  • with less foot → WIT the word WITH loses the letter at its foot, leaving three to join the pile
  • less foot — The foot of a word in a down clue is the letter at the bottom of it, and 'less' throws that letter away.
  • to shake about — Shake a thing about and nothing stays where it started, so the letters are rebuilt in any order you like.

A skeleton missing a foot gets rattled about, and out comes a dazzling dancer.

'Foot' does two jobs: the bone the skeleton has lost in the picture, and the bottom letter of a word in the instruction. Down clues can talk about tops and feet because they run vertically.

23 down TENET

Principle, if flipping the same? (5)

Definition: Principle · palindrome

Principle → TENET: a tenet is a principle held as an article of belief, the sort a party or a faith is built on.

  • Principle → the definition end of the clue: a belief held as a rule to live or work by
  • if flipping the same? → the wordplay end: five letters reading the same from either direction, which is all the help on offer
  • flipping the same — A word that is the same when flipped reads identically from either end, so there is nothing to turn round — only the symmetry to spot.

Five letters that read the same both ways is the whole of the wordplay. Christopher Nolan named a film after this word for exactly that symmetry.
