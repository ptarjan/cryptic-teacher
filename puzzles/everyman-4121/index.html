Observer Everyman Crossword No 4,121

Across

1 across RHEA

'Not fully overhead?' I'm never overhead! (4)

Definition: I'm never overhead! · hidden word

  • 'Not fully overhead?' → RHEA four consecutive letters sit in the middle of the quoted question's last word, running from its fourth letter on

The same word does both jobs: the question quotes it for its letters, the shout uses it for its meaning. A bird that has never once left the ground is entitled to sound touchy about it.

4 across OHMS

Resistance units reported in East End residences (4)

Definition: Resistance units · deletion + homophone

  • residences → HOMES residences are homes, the step before anything is said aloud
  • East End → OMES the East End is working-class London, and Cockney speech there drops the aitch off the front
  • East End residences → OHMS said out loud, that aitchless word is written with the aitch put back in the middle

The joke is one sound with two spellings: a Londoner's word for houses, and the unit named after the physicist Georg Ohm.

8 across HOPE AGAINST HOPE

Bob vs. his uncle, pray (4,7,4)

Definition: pray · charade

  • Bob → HOPE Bob Hope, the comedian who entertained the troops for decades, gives his surname
  • vs. → AGAINST vs. is short for versus, Latin for against
  • his uncle → HOPE Bob's your uncle is the British catchphrase for and there you are, so the uncle is another Bob and hands over the same surname again

Two catchphrases in one line, and both Bobs turn out to be the same comedian. The family quarrel of the surface never happened.

11 across THRIFTY

(60% of 3 + 80% of 50) ... careful! (7)

Definition: careful · charade + deletion

  • 60% of 3 → THR write the number out as a word and keep three of its five letters
  • 80% of 50 → IFTY the larger number spelled out, with the first of its five letters shed

Numbers spelled out as words are the trick, and the percentages measure letters rather than value. Three keeps its front, fifty loses its front.

12 across BAFFLED

After degree, fellow ran off, perplexed (7)

Definition: perplexed · charade

  • degree → BA a BA, bachelor of arts, is the standard British first degree
  • fellow → F F is the abbreviation for fellow, as in the FRS after a scientist's name
  • ran off → FLED to run off is to flee, and the past tense keeps the surface story going

Two abbreviations a solver meets every week, then an everyday past tense. The absconding academic of the surface is pure invention.

13 across ODOURLESS

Not to be sniffed at? (9)

Definition: Not to be sniffed at? · cryptic definition

  • Not to be sniffed at? → the reading the surface pushes: the idiom for something well worth taking seriously, not to be dismissed
  • sniffed → the reading the setter meant, taken flat: put a nose to it and there is nothing there to detect

Nothing here comes apart into letters. One phrase carries a figurative meaning and a literal one at once, and the question mark is the setter admitting it.

14 across SAVOY

Somewhat inconspicuous, a voyeur in hotel (5)

Definition: hotel · hidden word

  • inconspicuous, a voyeur → SAVOY five letters run on without a break from the end of one word through the two that follow, comma and all

The definition is one famous building rather than the general word, which is fair because the Strand's grandest address is known well beyond London.

15 across RESAT

Took another exam in Weaponry From The East (5)

Definition: Took another exam · reversal

  • Weaponry → TASER a stun gun firing barbed darts, standing in here for weaponry in general

Capital letters in the middle of a clue are decoration and not significance: they make the last four words look like the title of something.

16 across MINNESOTA

Nominates Liberal for Midwestern state (9)

Definition: Midwestern state · anagram

  • Nominates → NOMINATES nine letters given whole, and they are exactly the nine the answer needs

The capital L is the trap. It looks like the political party about to donate its usual abbreviation, and it is really the instruction to shuffle.

19 across STABLER

Relative, crazy; looking back, not so crazy (7)

Definition: not so crazy · charade + reversal

  • Relative → REL rel. is the everyday short form of relative
  • crazy → BATS bats is informal British for crazy, from having bats in the belfry

'Crazy' is written twice and does two different jobs: once it hands over a four-letter synonym, and once, negated, it supplies the meaning.

21 across GRASSES

Rats in pasturage (7)

Definition: Rats · double definition

  • Rats → to rat on someone is to inform on them, and British underworld slang has a second word for doing exactly that
  • pasturage → the plants that make up grazing land, taken as a class

Both halves are definitions and neither is the everyday one. The informing sense is British slang, still common in police dramas.

22 across MONEY FOR OLD ROPE

Employer of Rod on break - getting paid while idle (5,3,3,4)

Definition: getting paid while idle · anagram

  • Employer of Rod on → EMPLOYEROFRODON fifteen letters given up whole, matching the four-word enumeration

'Employer' opens the clue like a definition and is really the front of the letter pile. Rod is nobody in particular, which is the giveaway.

23 across BRIE

Brother, that is mild cheese! (4)

Definition: mild cheese · charade

  • Brother → BR Br. is how a monk's title is written short
  • that is → IE i.e. stands for the Latin id est, which is what that is means

Two of the shortest conventions in the game laid end to end — a religious title and a Latin tag — with the exclamation mark marking the setter's pleasure.

24 across IDEA

Hypothesis: Everyman cherished ...? Not entirely (4)

Definition: Hypothesis · charade + deletion

  • Everyman → I the setter signs these puzzles Everyman, so when a clue names him he is speaking of himself
  • cherished → DEA something cherished is dear, and here it is written one letter short

Setters sign their own puzzles, and a clue that names the signature usually means the first person singular — one letter, and a habit worth learning early.

Down

2 down HEAD-FIRST

Far-sighted? Not good: reckless, reckless (4-5)

Definition: reckless · deletion + anagram

  • Far-sighted → FARSIGHTED ten letters given up whole, the hyphen counting for nothing
  • good → G G is the standard abbreviation for good, and it is the letter that leaves the pile

The word is printed twice on purpose: the first is the instruction, the second is the meaning. 'Far-sighted' looks like a definition and is really the bag of letters.

3 down ANALYSE

Wanting volume, navy seal overturned study (7)

Definition: study · deletion + anagram

  • navy seal → NAVYSEAL eight letters given whole, an elite American sailor-commando on the surface
  • volume → V V is the standard abbreviation for volume, and that is the letter going missing

A navy seal is a person on the surface and a bag of letters underneath. The study wanted is the verb, not the room you do it in.

4 down OMNIBUS

Books a vehicle in Clapham (7)

Definition: Books · double definition

  • Books → several works reprinted together in one fat volume
  • a vehicle in Clapham → English law measures behaviour against the ordinary reasonable person, always pictured on the bus through Clapham in south London

Two meanings and no letter mechanics. The trap is reading 'Books' as a verb, as though somebody were booking a taxi in south London.

5 down MOTIF

Car exam: providing figure (5)

Definition: figure · charade

  • Car exam → MOT the MOT is the annual British roadworthiness test every car over three years old has to pass
  • providing → IF providing that something happens means if it happens

Two staples worth keeping: the British car test, and 'providing' standing in for the two-letter conditional. The surface sounds like a driving school and means nothing.

6 down WHAT HO

'Hullo, I see no weeding tool', you say? (4,2)

Definition: Hullo · homophone

  • weeding tool → HOE the long-handled blade you chop weeds out of a bed with
  • I see no weeding tool → WHAT HO seeing none of it, the question you ask is which one — and that question said aloud loses the tool's final letter

The greeting is Bertie Wooster's, hailed across a drawing room in Wodehouse. The joke rests on a garden tool and a hearty exclamation sounding identical.

7 down HEYDAY

Excuse me, Doris - boom! (6)

Definition: boom · charade

  • Excuse me → HEY the shout you use to stop someone and get their attention
  • Doris → DAY Doris Day, the American singer and film star of the 1950s, supplies her surname

A first name in a clue is very often there only to fetch the famous surname standing beside it, and this is one of the most-used pairs in the game.

9 down PORT-OF-SPAIN

Spooner's report of rugby somewhere in Trinidad (4-2-5)

Definition: somewhere in Trinidad · spoonerism

  • rugby → PORT rugby is a sport, and this word gives up its opening S to the one at the far end
  • report → SPAIN the report on that game is that it hurts, and this word collects the S coming back the other way

Say the plain verdict on the game first, then trade the two opening sounds. The middle word sits still while the outer two swap, which is what makes the capital city appear.

10 down OBLIVIOUS TO

Unaware of Victor - too bilious, seething (9,2)

Definition: Unaware of · anagram

  • Victor → V Victor is the NATO phonetic alphabet's word for the letter V
  • too bilious → TOOBILIOUS ten more letters handed over whole

The NATO alphabet repays learning in one go — Victor, Oscar, Charlie, Echo and the rest each hand a clue a single letter, and setters lean on it constantly.

14 down SKEDADDLE

Elks added to resort: run! (9)

Definition: run! · anagram

  • Elks added → ELKSADDED nine letters handed over whole, which is exactly the count the enumeration asks for

'Resort' leads a double life and this is the crossword one. The elks are on the page only to look plausible next to a holiday destination.

15 down RESUME

Take back précis (6)

Definition: Take back · double definition

  • Take back → to take a thing back is to occupy or take it up once again — take back your seat, take back the throne
  • précis → a précis is a short summary of a longer text, and with its French accents it is also a CV

One spelling doing duty for two words: the plain English verb, and the French noun that normally keeps its accents. The italics are the setter's nod to the second.

16 down MARLOWE

Hurt and depressed, English poet (7)

Definition: poet · charade

  • Hurt → MAR to mar something is to damage or spoil it
  • depressed → LOW feeling low is feeling depressed
  • English → E E is the standard abbreviation for English

Christopher Marlowe wrote Doctor Faustus and was stabbed in a Deptford tavern at twenty-nine. Three plain pieces, the last of them a one-letter abbreviation seen constantly.

17 down NEGRONI

Drops of (not exclusively) gin, rosso, orange ... naturally, ice! (7)

Definition: Drops of (not exclusively) gin, rosso, orange ... naturally, ice! · &lit + first letters

  • (not exclusively) → NE the bracketed aside is fodder as well as apology: both its words give up an initial
  • gin, rosso, orange → GRO the three ingredients give their opening letters in the order they are listed
  • naturally, ice → NI the last two words supply the two initials that finish the run

Every word works twice. Read straight it is the recipe — gin, red vermouth, bitter orange, served over ice — and read again it is a list of initials, brackets included.

18 down AISLES

Regularly panics elders, displaying divisions in Church (6)

Definition: divisions in Church · alternate letters

  • panics → AIS the second, fourth and sixth letters of the word
  • elders → LES the same even-numbered count carries straight on into the next word

A clue that says 'regularly' is counting, not describing. The alarmed elders are there only to put the right letters in the even positions.

20 down LAYER

Blanket for quail in nest? (5)

Definition: Blanket · double definition

  • Blanket → a covering spread evenly over the whole of something, as in a blanket of snow or fog
  • quail in nest? → a bird sitting on eggs is named for the one job it is doing, and the question mark admits this is an example rather than the word

The question mark is doing real work. 'Blanket' also looks like an adjective describing the bird, and it is a noun: a covering.
