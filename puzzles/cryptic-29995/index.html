1 across STARBURST GALAXY

Out-of-this-world 23 coming together, system rapidly forming (9,6)

Definition: Out-of-this-world 23 · double definition

Starburst and Galaxy are both sweets named after space, and a starburst galaxy is one turning gas into stars at a furious rate.

  • Out-of-this-world 23 → the puzzle's theme, sweets sold under astronomical names, and this entry wants two of them side by side
  • system rapidly forming → astronomers' phrase for a star system making new stars far faster than the usual rate
  • coming together — Two things joined into one phrase: the pair of names named by the first half are laid end to end and read as a single answer.

Matter out in space clumping together as a new system takes shape.

23 down is CANDY, so 'out-of-this-world 23' asks for a sweet with a space name — here two of them, and the pair turns out to be a real term in astronomy.

6 across HASH

Weed in pig’s ear? (4)

Definition: Weed · double definition

Hash is cannabis resin, and a hash is also a botch — the same word British idiom pairs with a pig's ear.

  • Weed → the smokeable kind, cannabis resin pressed into blocks
  • pig’s ear? → to make a pig's ear of a job is British for botching it completely

Weed growing in a pig's ear.

Drug slang and kitchen slang sit a preposition apart: the smoker's block of resin, and the mess you make of a job — make a pig's ear of it, make a hash of it.

8 across MILKY WAY

Use unknown technique for out-of-this-world 23 (5,3)

Definition: out-of-this-world 23 · charade

A Milky Way is a chocolate bar — a candy, which is 23 down — and it is named for the galaxy we live in.

  • Use → MILK to milk a situation is to use it for all it is worth
  • unknown → Y algebra's unknown quantities are X, Y and Z
  • technique → WAY a way of doing something is the technique you use

Applying a technique nobody has heard of.

'Use' is the verb meaning exploit rather than employ. The bar takes its name from the galaxy overhead, which is what earns it a place in this puzzle.

9 across TONGAN

Recovered, trouble far from islander (6)

Definition: islander · charade + reversal

A Tongan is a native of Tonga, an island kingdom in the South Pacific, so 'islander' defines by example.

  • trouble → NAG to nag is to trouble somebody on and on
  • far from → NOT 'far from ready' means not ready, so the phrase stands in for a plain negative
  • Recovered — To recover a thing is to get it back, and 'back' is the instruction — the pieces are read from their far end. A loose signal, and the looseness is the setter's.

An islander back on their feet and no trouble to anybody.

'Far from' hides a whole word — it means 'not', which is half the wordplay. Tonga is a kingdom of some 170 islands in the South Pacific.

11 across ICED OVER

Divorcee, after breaking up, froze (4,4)

Definition: froze · anagram

Water that has iced over has frozen at the surface, so 'froze' is the same event in the same tense.

  • Divorcee → DIVORCEE anagram fodder, eight letters — exactly the enumeration
  • after breaking up — A marriage breaking up scatters what was joined, and these letters do the same — they will not stay in the order given.

A divorcee turning cold on the world once the marriage has ended.

The clue is one sad little story, and that is what stops the first word from looking like eight loose letters waiting to be shuffled.

12 across TARDIS

Vicious swine sent back in time machine (6)

Definition: time machine · charade + reversal

The Tardis is the blue police box that carries the Doctor through time, so 'time machine' names it by exactly what it does.

  • Vicious → SID Sid Vicious played bass for the Sex Pistols, and the surname is the half printed here
  • swine → RAT call somebody a rat and you have called them a swine
  • sent back — Something sent back travels the way it came, so in an across entry the assembled pieces are read from right to left.

A savage pig posted back through the centuries.

'Vicious' looks like an adjective describing the swine and is really a surname. The Tardis is the police box the Doctor travels in on Doctor Who.

15 across GREAT AUK

Dreary lecture, reportedly – that would never take off (5,3)

Definition: that would never take off · charade + homophone

The great auk was flightless, so a description of something that could never leave the ground names the bird exactly.

  • Dreary → GREY a dreary day is a grey one, the weather sense
  • lecture → TALK a lecture is a talk delivered to an audience
  • Dreary lecture, reportedly → GREATAUK said aloud, those two words run together and the break between them shifts one letter to the left
  • reportedly — A report passes on what was said, so what follows is written from the sound of the words rather than from their spelling.

A lecture so dull, by all accounts, that it was never going anywhere.

Where the gap falls is the whole joke: say the two words and the break slides, turning dull conversation into a bird. The great auk was a flightless North Atlantic seabird, extinct by 1852.

16 across PUMP ROOM

Question having caught cold: is spring here? (4,4)

Definition: is spring here? · charade + homophone

A pump room is the hall at a spa where water from the spring is pumped up and drunk, so the spring is indeed there.

  • Question → PUMP to pump somebody is to press them for information
  • having caught cold → ROOM rheum is the watery stuff a head cold produces, and it is said exactly like the four letters written here

Somebody streaming with a cold, questioned about whether spring has arrived.

'Caught cold' reads as the idiom for being taken by surprise and is really handing you a doctor's word for the sniffles, to be said aloud. Bath's Pump Room served water from the spring beneath it.

19 across RESITS

Extra tests: restrain oneself, turning tail (6)

Definition: Extra tests · reversal

A resit is the exam you take again after failing it, so 'Extra tests' matches it, plural for plural.

  • restrain oneself → RESIST to resist is to hold yourself back from something you want
  • turning tail — Turning tail is running away, and read literally it turns the tail of a word round — the last two letters change places.

Advice before more exams: keep a grip on yourself, then run.

Two letters move and no more, which is the smallest turn a clue can ask for and the easiest to miss when the word already looks nearly right.

21 across DEMPSTER

Old scandalmonger keeping politicians in check (8)

Definition: Old scandalmonger · container

Nigel Dempster was Fleet Street's best-known gossip columnist, trading in scandal, and 'Old' marks him as the late one.

  • in check → DETER to hold somebody in check is to deter them
  • politicians → MPS MPs are Members of Parliament, the elected members of the Commons
  • keeping — Keeping something means holding on to it inside, so the word beside it closes round the letters named next.

A veteran gossip columnist with the politicians under control.

Nigel Dempster wrote the Daily Mail's gossip column for a quarter of a century, and 'Old' is the clue's way of saying he is no longer with us.

22 across LAUNCH

Eat around a fire (6)

Definition: fire · container

To launch a rocket is to fire it, the sense of setting something off rather than sliding a boat into the water.

  • Eat → LUNCH to lunch is to eat, the verb of the midday meal
  • a → A the clue's own indefinite article, taken as a single letter
  • around — Around means encircling, so the word in front of it wraps itself about the letter that comes next.

Eating around a campfire.

Two ordinary words change job at once: 'fire' is a verb here and 'a' is a letter rather than an article.

24 across FLYING SAUCERS

Out-of-this-world 23, smashing stuff in the kitchen? (6,7)

Definition: Out-of-this-world 23 · double definition

Flying saucers are sherbet sweets named for the UFO, and saucers genuinely flying are smashing stuff — china in mid-air.

  • Out-of-this-world 23 → the sherbet-filled rice-paper discs from the corner shop, named after the UFO
  • smashing stuff in the kitchen? → crockery hurled across the room in a domestic row does not stay in one piece

Something unearthly in a kitchen where the china is already being thrown.

The question mark marks the second half as a joke rather than a dictionary sense: read one word at a time, it describes airborne crockery in a domestic row.

25 across BITTERNS

Some birds pecked others (8)

Definition: Some birds · charade

Bitterns are the booming brown herons of the reed beds, so 'Some birds' defines by example — a particular few of a large class.

  • pecked → BIT a bird that pecked you bit you
  • others → TERNS 'others' borrows the birds from the definition and asks for a different sort — the slim sea ones that hover and dive

A garden scuffle in which one lot of birds goes for the rest.

'Others' cannot stand alone: it reaches back into the definition for the word 'birds' and asks for another species, so one word serves two parts of the clue at once.

26 across BERG

Austrian maestro with an uber-Germanic piece (4)

Definition: Austrian maestro · hidden word

Alban Berg was the Viennese composer of Wozzeck and Lulu, so 'Austrian maestro' names him by nationality and trade.

  • uber-Germanic → BERG four letters in a row here, straddling the hyphen
  • piece — A piece of something is a fragment broken off it, so what is wanted is an unbroken run of letters out of the words beside it.

An Austrian conductor playing a very German piece of music.

'Piece' reads as a piece of music, which is exactly where the surface wants your mind. Alban Berg wrote Wozzeck and studied under Schoenberg in Vienna.

27 across SERIOUSLY

Very much for the love of God! (9)

Definition: Very much · double definition

One word covers both: the adverb meaning to a great degree, and the single-word groan of exasperation thrown at somebody trying your patience.

  • Very much → as in doubting a thing very much — the adverb meaning to a great degree
  • for the love of God! → the exasperated cry of somebody whose patience has just run out

Something done wholeheartedly out of love for God.

The clue reads as piety and means impatience: 'for the love of God!' is what people say at the end of their tether, and the answer is the one-word version of it.

1 down SHIVA

Say no more, narcissist has doffed cap for supreme Being (5)

Definition: supreme Being · charade + deletion

Shiva is not one god among many but the supreme being of Shaivism, the destroyer and renewer at the head of the Hindu pantheon.

  • Say no more → SH the noise you make telling somebody to be quiet, written down as two letters
  • narcissist → DIVA a diva is somebody entirely wrapped up in herself
  • has doffed cap — Doffing a cap is lifting it off your head, so the word it applies to loses the letter sitting on top of it.

A vain man baring his head, told to stop talking about himself.

The capital B on 'Being' is the only signal that a god is wanted rather than a person, and 'Say no more' is doing service as two letters instead of the idiom it looks like.

2 down AWKWARD

Difficult work, or not, opening present (7)

Definition: Difficult · container + deletion

Awkward means difficult to handle or deal with — an awkward customer is a difficult one, the same everyday sense.

  • present → AWARD an award is a present in the sense of something formally handed over
  • work, or not → WK WORK told to do without OR keeps only its first and last letters
  • or not — 'Or not' cancels the two letters it spells: the word in front of it has to manage without its O and its R.
  • opening — A present being opened has a gap in it, and that gap is where the letters named alongside are dropped.

Hard work, or maybe not, unwrapping a gift.

'Difficult work' reads as one phrase, which is what hides the seam: only the first word is the definition, and the second is about to lose two letters.

3 down BAWDY

Explicit case of wood in tree (5)

Definition: Explicit · container + outer letters

Bawdy means coarsely and openly sexual, so 'Explicit' matches it in the sense of leaving nothing decently unsaid.

  • tree → BAY the bay is an evergreen whose leaves go into a stew, and a crossword regular
  • case of wood → WD the case of WOOD is its shell: the first letter and the last, with the middle discarded
  • case of — A case is what encloses a thing, so what is wanted from the word after it is its outside — the letters at either end.
  • in — 'In' puts one piece within another: the letters just taken go inside the word that follows.

A crate of timber wedged up in a tree.

'Case of wood' sounds like a packing crate and is really an order to take two letters off the ends of a word. Everything else in the clue is one short synonym.

4 down RHYMING

Song about India in raga oddly similar in sound (7)

Definition: similar in sound · container + alternate letters

Rhyming words match in sound at their ends, so 'similar in sound' describes exactly the relation the word names.

  • Song → HYMN a hymn is a song, the churchgoing sort
  • India → I India is the letter I in the radio alphabet, alongside Alpha and Bravo
  • in raga oddly → RG the first and third letters of 'raga', the other two left out
  • about — Something placed about a thing is wrapped round it, so the word before goes outside the letter named after it.
  • oddly — Oddly points at the odd-numbered letters — take the first and the third and leave the rest behind.

A song about India that sounds oddly like a raga.

'Oddly' looks like a comment on how odd the resemblance is, and is really the instruction that thins 'raga' down to two letters. One container sits inside another here.

5 down TO THE REAR

Child, and corn she has on the backside? (2,3,4)

Definition: on the backside? · charade

To the rear means at the back of something, and 'on the backside' puts the same position in blunter, bodily terms.

  • Child → TOT a tot is a small child
  • she has → HER what she has is hers, and the possessive form of 'she' is these three letters
  • corn → EAR corn grows in ears, one to a stalk

A child, and a corn she has on her backside.

The three pieces straddle the gaps in the answer instead of sitting inside them, so nothing you build lines up with anything you write.

6 down HANG-OUT

For ‘haunt’, try entering an anagram of that! (4-3)

Definition: haunt · anagram + container

A hang-out is the place somebody is always to be found, which is precisely what a haunt is, in slangier clothes.

  • that → HAUNT 'that' points back at the word in quotation marks, five letters waiting to be shuffled
  • try → GO a go is an attempt at something
  • an anagram of — The setter names the device outright: the letters of the word he has just quoted are to be re-sorted before anything else happens.
  • entering — Somebody entering a building goes inside it, so the piece named before it is placed within the shuffled letters.

A setter daring you to solve it by rearranging the word he has just handed you.

The clue says exactly what to do and is still hard, because the letters being shuffled are the definition's own — meaning and material come out of the same five letters.

7 down SPACE DUST

Capacity remains for out-of-this-world 23 (5,4)

Definition: out-of-this-world 23 · charade

Space Dust is a brand of crackling sherbet, a candy — 23 down — whose name is literally out of this world.

  • Capacity → SPACE capacity is how much room there is, measured as an amount
  • remains → DUST what remains of a body in the end, as the funeral service has it

There is still room for something not of this earth.

Both wordplay words are nouns pretending to be a verb and its subject — capacity remains. 23 down is CANDY, and the theme is sweets named after space.

13 down AZURE BLUE

A river down below capital of Zimbabwe in shade (5,4)

Definition: shade · charade + container + first letter

Azure blue is the clear deep blue of a cloudless sky, so 'shade' names it as one particular tint among many.

  • A → A the clue's own first word, taken at face value
  • capital of Zimbabwe → Z not Harare but the letter the country's name starts with
  • river → URE the Ure runs through the Yorkshire dale named after it, and setters use it constantly
  • down → BLUE feeling down is feeling low, and there is a colour word for that mood
  • below — In a down entry, below means further down the column, so the piece it marks is parked at the foot of the answer.
  • capital of — A country's capital is its chief city, and the same joke makes it the chief letter of the country's name.
  • in — 'In' is the whole instruction: the letter it introduces is dropped inside the letters already assembled.

A river running below the Zimbabwean capital, deep in shadow.

'Capital of Zimbabwe' invites you to think of Harare and wants one letter; 'down' is a mood rather than a direction, though a direction is what the next word does with it.

14 down SHORTAGES

Rags, those sent abroad, insufficient amounts (9)

Definition: insufficient amounts · anagram

A shortage is a want of something needed, so 'insufficient amounts' spells out the same idea, plural for plural.

  • Rags, those → RAGSTHOSE anagram fodder, nine letters, matching the enumeration
  • sent abroad — Things sent abroad are scattered somewhere else entirely, and these letters land in an order of their own.

Old clothes shipped overseas, and never enough of them to go round.

'Those' reads as a pointing word rather than as five more letters, and it is the piece the whole anagram depends on.

17 down POPPING CANDY

Sweet thing, flower impressed by dancing when excited (7,5)

Definition: Sweet thing · container + anagram

Popping candy is the sherbet that crackles on the tongue, so 'Sweet thing' names it by the confectionery sense of sweet.

  • flower → POPPY the red field flower of the cornfields and the war graves
  • dancing → INGCAND the seven letters of 'dancing' in a fresh order
  • impressed by — To impress is to press one thing into another, so what follows is pressed into the middle of the letters that came before.
  • when excited — Excited means agitated, jumping about, and letters in that state will not stay in the order the clue gives them.

A charming girl, and a flower won over by an excited dance.

The flower is split almost at its end, which is why it stays out of sight: only its last letter is left over on the far side of the seven shuffled ones.

18 down MARS BAR

Definition: Out-of-this-world 23 · charade

A Mars bar is a candy — 23 down — and it carries the name of a planet, which makes it out of this world.

  • warps → MARS to mar a thing is to spoil it, which is what warping does to a shape

Something unearthly bending a beam out of true.

'Warps beam' reads as one action with its object and is two separate synonyms. The bar shares its name with the planet, which is all the theme asks of it.

22 down LOTTO

Definition: Chance to win big · deletion

Lotto is the numbers draw people play for a jackpot, so 'Chance to win big' describes the game by what it offers.

  • wasted → BLOTTO blotto is British slang for hopelessly drunk
  • lead → B the lead of a word is the letter at its head, and the drunken word above starts with this one
