Guardian Cryptic Crossword No 29,974

Across

1 across DAR ES SALAAM

A girl turning over in a dream when disturbed in city (3,2,6)

Definition: city · anagram + reversal + container

Dar es Salaam is Tanzania's largest city and its former capital, so the bare word names the answer by its class.

  • a dream → DAREAM six letters resettled into a new order, then parted to make room
  • A girl → SSALA a girl is A LASS, and these are its five letters set down the other way round
  • turning over — A sleeper turning over finishes the other way round, and so do the letters this phrase is applied to.
  • when disturbed — Something disturbed has been knocked out of its settled arrangement, so these letters will not stay in the order they are given.
  • in — Being in a thing means being surrounded by it, so the piece named first is planted among the shuffled letters.

A girl tossing and turning through a troubled dream somewhere in a city.

'A girl' reads as the opening of the anagram fodder and is a separate five-letter piece; only 'a dream' gets shuffled. The answer is the Indian Ocean port that was Tanzania's capital.

9 across IMPASTO

This writer’s quiet, a holy person revealed by old painting (7)

Definition: painting · charade

Impasto is paint laid on thick enough to keep the brush marks, so it names a manner of painting rather than a picture.

  • This writer’s → IM a setter writing about himself says I'm, which is two letters
  • quiet → P p is piano on a musical score, the mark for quiet
  • a → A the clue's own word, one letter taken as it stands
  • holy person → ST ST is the standing abbreviation for saint
  • old → O O is the usual abbreviation for old

A modest writer letting slip that a saint turned up in an old picture.

'This writer's' is the setter talking about himself, and it is worth owning: it means I'M in every puzzle you will ever meet, not only this one.

10 across SEMINAL

Pioneering conceptual artist in short trade event (7)

Definition: Pioneering · container + deletion

A seminal work is one whose ideas seed everything that follows, which is what pioneering means of a piece of work.

  • trade event → SAL a sale is a trade event, and it arrives here one letter short
  • conceptual artist → EMIN Tracey Emin is the British conceptual artist of the unmade bed
  • in — Putting one thing in another leaves it surrounded, so the artist's letters sit inside the letters named after.
  • short — Cutting a word short takes something off its end, so the trade event turns up one letter down.

A ground-breaking conceptual artist showing up at a brief trade fair.

'Conceptual artist' reads as a description and is really a surname. Tracey Emin's unmade bed, My Bed, was shortlisted for the Turner Prize in 1999.

11 across PROLACTIN

Expert not to have enough money, it’s said, as milk producer’s helper (9)

Definition: milk producer’s helper · charade + homophone

Prolactin is the hormone that switches milk production on after a birth, so it is what helps the milk producer do the job.

  • Expert → PRO a pro is an expert, short for professional
  • not to have enough money → LACTIN tin is old slang for money, so to lack tin is to be short of it; run together the sound is spelt differently
  • it’s said — Saying how a thing is said points at sound rather than spelling, so write down what the phrase sounds like and not how it looks.

An expert who is permanently broke, according to what people are saying.

'Tin' for money is Victorian slang that survives mainly in crosswords, and hearing the two words run together as one is what makes a hormone appear.

12 across NYALA

US state in the role of wild animal (5)

Definition: wild animal · charade

The nyala is a spiral-horned antelope of southern Africa's thickets, so 'wild animal' names it by the broadest class available.

  • US state → NY NY is the postal abbreviation for New York
  • in the role of → ALA à la is French for 'in the manner of', used in English of doing something in somebody's style

An American state playing the part of some wild creature.

'In the role of' is a stretched way of reaching à la, which properly means 'in the manner of' — the setter is trading on how close playing a part sits to imitating a style.

13 across RIAL

Bit of cash made by heartless opponent (4)

Definition: Bit of cash · deletion

The rial is the currency of Iran, so a single one is a bit of cash in the plainest possible sense.

  • opponent → RIVAL an opponent is a rival, and the V at its centre is the letter that goes
  • heartless — A heartless thing has had its middle taken out, so the word loses the single letter sitting at its centre.

Small change handed over by a rival with no heart.

Setters lean on the world's currencies whenever a short entry needs filling, and this one — Iran's, and Oman's too — is among the regulars.

14 across APPLE SAUCE

Supply a cup please for stewed fruit (5,5)

Definition: stewed fruit · anagram

Apple sauce is apples cooked down to a pulp, so 'stewed fruit' describes the dish literally rather than by any figurative sense.

  • a cup please → ACUPPLEASE anagram fodder, ten letters
  • Supply — Read as the adverb from supple — SUP-lee, meaning flexibly — it tells you the letters beside it bend into another shape.

Somebody politely asking for a cup to be brought out with the stewed fruit.

'Supply' is not the verb it looks like: said SUP-lee it is the adverb of 'supple', meaning flexibly. Pasquale puts it in the first word, before you are suspicious of anything.

16 across BRIGANDAGE

Group capturing apparatus, taking time plundering (10)

Definition: plundering · container + charade

Brigandage is robbery carried out by roving bands of armed men, which is plundering under an older and more formal name.

  • Group → BAND a band is a group, and it parts in the middle to take something in
  • apparatus → RIG a rig is the apparatus set up for a job — drilling, lighting, sailing
  • time → AGE an age is a stretch of time
  • capturing — To capture a thing is to take it and hold it inside, so the group closes around the letters named next.

A gang seizing equipment and taking their time over it.

'Taking time plundering' reads as one leisurely phrase and it breaks across the clue's seam: 'time' is the last of the wordplay and 'plundering' is the definition.

19 across SHOO

Go away and make no noise with love over (4)

Definition: Go away · charade

Shoo is what you say to drive a cat or a bird off, so it is 'go away' spoken as an instruction.

  • make no noise → SH sh! is the sound you make telling somebody to be quiet
  • love → O love is nil in tennis, written as the O-shaped zero
  • over → O O is the scorer's abbreviation for a cricket over, six deliveries from one end

Being told to leave quietly now that the love affair is over.

'Love over' reads as a finished romance and is two separate scoreboards, one from tennis and one from cricket, both writing the same round letter.

21 across MOCHA

One drink and second drink that’s different (5)

Definition: One drink · charade

A mocha is coffee made with chocolate, so 'one drink' reaches the answer as an instance of the class rather than as a synonym.

  • second → MO a mo is a moment — 'hang on a mo' — which is a second of time
  • drink that’s different → CHA cha is tea, a different beverage and a different word for drink

Somebody ordering a drink and then a second one of quite a different sort.

'Drink' appears twice on purpose and means two different things: once it is the definition and once it is wordplay, and 'that's different' is Pasquale saying so.

22 across SNICKERED

Son sent to prison, one ultimately angry-looking, expressed a little amusement (9)

Definition: expressed a little amusement · charade + last letter

To snicker is to let out a half-smothered laugh, which is amusement expressed in a small and slightly unkind way.

  • Son → S S is the standard abbreviation for son
  • sent to prison → NICK in British slang the nick is prison, the place an offender is sent to
  • one ultimately → E the last letter of onE
  • angry-looking → RED a face gone red is a face gone angry
  • ultimately — What comes ultimately comes last of all, so only the final letter of the word beside it is taken.

A man's son locked up, and somebody with a furious face finding it funny.

'The nick' is British slang for prison and 'nicked' for arrested; the word turns up constantly in these puzzles and almost nowhere outside Britain.

24 across SORT OUT

Discipline soldiers sunk in alcoholic drink (4,3)

Definition: Discipline · container

To sort somebody out is to deal with them firmly — the British sense of putting a person straight, which is disciplining them.

  • alcoholic drink → STOUT stout is the dark, heavy beer, and it opens up to take something in
  • soldiers → OR OR is the army's abbreviation for other ranks, the soldiers who are not officers
  • sunk in — Whatever is sunk in something finishes below the surface and surrounded by it, so those letters go inside the drink.

Troops being disciplined after sinking rather too much of a dark beer.

'I'll sort him out' is British and unmistakably physical, and that threatening sense is how a phrase which ordinarily means tidying reaches 'discipline'.

25 across PUDDING

Food theologian favoured being fed to dog (7)

Definition: Food · container + charade

A pudding is the sweet course at the end of a British meal, so 'food' names it by the broadest class it belongs to.

  • dog → PUG a pug is a small flat-faced dog
  • theologian → DD DD is Doctor of Divinity, the degree a theologian holds
  • favoured → IN what is in is what is favoured or fashionable
  • being fed to — What is fed to an animal goes inside it, so the letters named before this phrase are swallowed by the dog.

A theologian who is well thought of being handed to a dog as its dinner.

'Being fed to' reads as part of the picture and is the instruction that puts one word inside another. A Doctor of Divinity is a real degree, which is how a theologian shrinks to two letters.

26 across STRIPTEASER

Starter and pies wrong for entertainer who needs good figure? (11)

Definition: entertainer who needs good figure? · anagram

A stripteaser undresses slowly for an audience, so a good figure is a working requirement rather than a synonym for the word.

  • Starter and pies → STARTERPIES anagram fodder, eleven letters
  • wrong — Something wrong is not as it should be, and letters not as they should be are letters out of their proper order.

The starter and the pies coming out wrong for a performer's dinner.

The question mark is doing real work: it flags a definition that describes the job rather than naming it, which is the setter's licence for the joke.

Down

1 down DIPLOMATIC CORPS

Awful mod politics – crap group representing country (10,5)

Definition: group representing country · anagram

The diplomatic corps is the whole body of ambassadors and their staff posted abroad to speak for their country.

  • mod politics → MODPOLITICS eleven of the fifteen letters, taken exactly as they stand
  • crap → CRAP the remaining four letters of the fodder
  • Awful — Calling a thing awful says it has come out badly, and letters that have come out badly are letters no longer in order.

Mod-scene politics at its worst, and a hopeless band being sent abroad to represent the nation.

'Crap group' reads as one insult about a band, and its two words fall on opposite sides of the clue: one is fodder, the other opens the definition.

2 down RASTA

A prominent Russian upset adherent of African religion (5)

Definition: adherent of African religion · charade + reversal

A Rasta follows Rastafari, the Afrocentric faith built around Ethiopia's emperor Haile Selassie, which is what the clue calls an African religion.

  • prominent Russian → RAST the tsar was Russia's emperor, his four letters read from the bottom up
  • A → A the clue's own opening letter, taken as it stands
  • upset — Upset means overturned, and an overturned answer running down the grid reads its letters from the bottom upwards.

A well-known Russian upsetting a believer in some African faith.

'Upset' reads as the surface's verb, the Russian annoying somebody, and it is an instruction instead. Tsars have been gone since 1917 but remain a setter's most dependable Russian.

3 down SHOOT UP

With drink around, sound is to increase dramatically (5,2)

Definition: to increase dramatically · container

Prices or numbers that shoot up climb suddenly and steeply, which is increasing dramatically rather than merely rising.

  • drink → SUP to sup is to drink, and the word parts in the middle to make room
  • sound → HOOT a hoot is the sound an owl or a car horn makes
  • around — To hold a thing around something is to wrap right round it, so the drink's letters open up and close over the piece named next.

With drinks going round, the noise in the room climbs sharply.

'Sound is' reads as one grammatical joint and it is where the clue splits: 'sound' ends the wordplay and 'is' is the setter's equals sign.

4 down ARSENAL

Ways academy may be set up for club (7)

Definition: club · charade + reversal

Arsenal is a north London football club, so 'club' names the answer as one example of the species rather than defining it.

  • academy → AR the Royal Academy is RA, here written from the bottom up
  • Ways → SENAL ways are lanes, and these are its letters read upwards
  • may be set up — Something set up stands on end, and a down answer stood on end reads its letters from the bottom of the grid upwards.

The paths around a school being laid out to suit a club that uses it.

Reading 'ways academy' as one thing — a school of methods — is what stalls this clue; they are two separate pieces, and the club at the end is a football one.

5 down ADMONISH

A fellow hugging maiden is husband getting reproach (8)

Definition: reproach · charade + container

To admonish is to tell somebody off firmly but not furiously, which is what reproach means as a verb.

  • A → A the clue's own first letter, taken as it stands
  • fellow → DON a don is a fellow of an Oxford or Cambridge college
  • maiden → M M is the scorer's mark for a maiden over
  • is → IS the word itself, its two letters going straight in
  • husband → H H is the standard abbreviation for husband
  • hugging — A hug closes right round whatever is being hugged, so the word doing it opens up and takes the next letter inside.

A man with his arm round a young woman turns out to be her husband.

'Maiden' is cricket here, not romance: an over from which no runs are scored is written M on the scorecard, and it is one of the most reliable single letters a setter has.

6 down MONTAGU'S HARRIER

Bird, exceptionally smart, hung o’er air (8,7)

Definition: Bird · anagram

Montagu's harrier is a slender bird of prey that quarters open ground, named after the naturalist George Montagu.

  • smart, hung o’er air → SMARTHUNGOERAIR anagram fodder, fifteen letters
  • exceptionally — What is exceptional departs from the ordinary arrangement, so these letters are to be taken out of the order they arrive in.

A strikingly handsome bird holding itself motionless above open ground.

'O'er' is poetry's clipped 'over', and its three letters do the same job as any other three in the fodder — the apostrophe carries no instruction at all.

7 down KIPPER

Captain, not opener – one’s smoked before meal (6)

Definition: one’s smoked before meal · deletion

A kipper is a herring split and smoked before it ever reaches the breakfast table, so the definition names the fish by what is done to it.

  • Captain → SKIPPER the captain of a boat or a team is the skipper, and its leading S is the letter that goes
  • not opener — An opener is whatever comes first, so saying it is not there strikes the leading letter off the word in front of it.

A ship's captain who does not open the batting, and something smoked served before dinner.

'Opener' is cricket's word for the batter who goes in first, which is what lets it stand for the first letter of a word without ever looking like an instruction.

8 down CLEAVE

Stick around, then go (6)

Definition: Stick · charade

To cleave to something is to stick fast to it, the old sense kept alive in 'cleave to your principles'.

  • around → C c. is short for circa, the 'around' put in front of an approximate date
  • go → LEAVE to leave is to go

Being told to hang about for a while and then head off.

'Stick around' is the trap: it reads as one phrase meaning wait, and its two words belong to opposite halves of the clue. The answer is its own opposite, since it also means to split.

15 down PARAMOUR

Lover in the morning in room losing heart (8)

Definition: Lover · container + deletion

A paramour is a lover, and specifically the illicit kind — the one somebody is seeing outside a marriage.

  • room → PARLOUR a parlour is a room, and the L at its centre is the letter that goes
  • the morning → AM AM marks the hours before noon
  • in — Being in a room means being surrounded by it, so the letters named first end up inside the ones named after.
  • losing heart — Losing heart is losing whatever sits at the centre, so the room's middle letter drops out before anything is put in.

A lover sitting in a room early in the day, quietly losing heart.

'Losing heart' carries the surface's emotional note and a deletion instruction at the same time, which is why it never reads as one; 'in the morning' hides AM the same way.

16 down BEMUSE

Honour to have value producing puzzle (6)

Definition: puzzle · charade

To bemuse somebody is to leave them bewildered, which is what 'puzzle' means as a verb rather than as a crossword.

  • Honour → BEM the British Empire Medal, BEM, is a state honour for civil or military service
  • value → USE the use of a thing is its value — 'what use is it?'

An award that turns out to be worth having, and the confusion that follows.

British honours supply setters with a whole family of short letter groups — OBE, MBE, CBE, DSO — and this is the one that non-British solvers meet least often.

17 down DESKTOP

Some French kit I discarded before work in computer location (7)

Definition: computer location · charade + deletion

The desktop is the screen a computer shows when nothing is open, the place its files and shortcuts are kept.

  • Some French → DES 'des' is the French for 'some', as in 'des amis'
  • kit I discarded → KT KIT with its I thrown out
  • work → OP op. is short for opus, a composer's numbered work
  • discarded — What is discarded is thrown away, so the letter named just in front of it leaves the word it was sitting in.
  • before — One thing before another comes first in the reading, so this piece is laid down ahead of the one named after it.

Somebody in France throwing out a bit of kit before settling down to work.

'Some French' asks for the French word for 'some', not a piece of the word 'French' — a Guardian staple that also yields UN, UNE, LES and DU.

18 down GLIMPSE

Quickly observe little devils in endless merriment (7)

Definition: Quickly observe · container + deletion

To glimpse something is to see it briefly and by chance, which is exactly what observing it quickly amounts to.

  • merriment → GLE merriment is glee, arriving here without its last letter
  • little devils → IMPS an imp is a little devil
  • in — Something in a thing is inside it, so the devils named first are planted within the letters named after.
  • endless — An endless word has had its final letter taken off, so the merriment turns up one letter shorter.

Naughty children spotted in the middle of a party that never quite ends.

'Endless merriment' is a phrase anybody would use about a good party, and here it names a word and then trims it.

20 down OLD AGE

Told a gentleman to conceal senior citizenship (3,3)

Definition: senior citizenship · hidden word

Old age is the stage of life a senior citizen has reached, so the state and the status name the same stretch of years.

  • Told a gentleman → OLDAGE six consecutive letters run from inside the first word, straight across the second and into the third
  • to conceal — Concealing a thing means it is there but not showing, so these letters are already written out and only need spotting.

Somebody being asked to keep a gentleman's pensioner status quiet.

'Told' looks like an instruction to listen for a sound and is only the first word of the hiding place. Spanning three words is what keeps the letters out of sight.

23 down KUDOS

So getting duck not century brings reverse of commendation needed (5)

Definition: commendation · charade + deletion + reversal

Kudos is the credit and praise that an achievement earns, which is commendation taken as an uncountable noun.

  • duck not century → KUD in cricket a duck is a score of nought and a century a hundred; DUCK loses the C, and the three letters left are read the other way up
  • So → OS the clue's own opening word, its two letters turned back to front
  • needed → surface only: it rounds the sentence off and hands over no letters
  • not — Saying a letter is not there is saying it comes out, so the century's C leaves the word standing in front of it.
  • reverse of — The wordplay is built to read the opposite way round from the definition, so what it spells has to be turned end for end.

A cricketer scoring nothing instead of a hundred, and the praise that does not follow.

'Reverse of' sits on the definition's side of the clue rather than the wordplay's, so the letters you assemble come out spelling the answer backwards.
