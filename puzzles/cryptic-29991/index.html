Across

1 across COMELY

Pretty shy hugging me at rear of school (6)

Definition: Pretty · container + last letter

Comely is the old-fashioned adjective for good-looking, so 'Pretty' swaps straight in wherever it stands.

  • shy → COY somebody shy about being looked at is coy
  • me → ME the setter's own 'me' goes in as its two letters, unchanged
  • school → L the letter at the back of 'school'
  • hugging — To hug somebody is to get your arms right round them, so the word in front closes over the letters named next.
  • at rear of — The rear of a word is the letter it ends on, and that single letter is all this takes.

A bashful admirer clinging to the speaker round the back of the school.

'Pretty shy' reads as one phrase, a fairly timid person, and the split falls between the two words: the first is the definition and the second is material.

4 across CROSSBOW

Get over arch, and one may make a bolt for it (8)

Definition: one may make a bolt for it · charade

A crossbow fires bolts, so the description is literal: the bolt is its ammunition rather than an escape.

  • Get over → CROSS to get over a road or a river is to cross it
  • arch → BOW a bow is an arch: a bow window bulges out in the same curve

Somebody climbing over an arch, where anyone might suddenly run for it.

'Make a bolt for it' is the idiom for dashing away, and the bolt the setter means is the short heavy arrow the weapon shoots.

9 across GUSTO

Relish oxygen brought by sudden wind (5)

Definition: Relish · charade

Gusto is the relish you do something with: 'ate with gusto' and 'ate with relish' ask for the same appetite.

  • sudden wind → GUST a gust is a sudden rush of wind
  • oxygen → O o is the chemist's symbol for oxygen
  • brought by — The wind is what carries the other piece along, so whatever it brings is set down after it.

A sudden squall of wind blowing fresh air about.

'Relish' looks like the verb — to relish a challenge — and the noun is wanted, the zest somebody does a thing with.

10 across LEVELLERS

Old Parliamentary radicals who told it to you straight? (9)

Definition: Old Parliamentary radicals · double definition

The Levellers were English radicals of the 1640s, and a leveller is also anyone who levels with you — plain speaking, no flattery.

  • Old Parliamentary radicals → the movement that pressed the Civil War Parliament of the 1640s for equal rights and a vote for ordinary men
  • who told it to you straight → to level with somebody is to tell them the truth with nothing dressed up, and these are the people who do it

Politicians of the past who never dodged a question.

The question mark marks the second half as a joke rather than a dictionary sense, and the capital P sells the first half as history.

11 across BARGAINER

Deal-maker in pub, one taking a profit (9)

Definition: Deal-maker · charade

A bargainer is somebody who haggles over terms until a deal is struck, so 'Deal-maker' names them by what the haggling produces.

  • pub → BAR the bar is where a pub serves its drinks, and stands for the place itself
  • one taking a profit → GAINER somebody who gains is a gainer — a share that rises is one of the day's gainers

A dealmaker propping up a pub bar, out to take his cut.

'In' looks like it is putting one thing inside another, which is what that word usually does in a clue; here it only joins the definition to the wordplay.

12 across TASTE

A brief experience of discrimination (5)

Definition: A brief experience · double definition

A taste of something is a brief sample of it, and a person of taste discriminates between good and bad — two senses of the noun.

  • A brief experience → a short sample of something — a few days of it, enough to know what it is like
  • discrimination → the older sense of the word: the judgement that tells the good from the shoddy

A short spell of being discriminated against.

'Discrimination' pulls hard towards prejudice, because that is the only sense in daily use now, and the setter wants the faculty of telling fine from cheap.

13 across ROOM AT THE TOP

Lower tier seats filled first for this film? (4,2,3,3)

Definition: this film · cryptic definition

If the lower tiers fill first the space left is literally up above, and that phrase is the title of the 1959 film.

  • Lower tier seats filled first → the surface picture is an auditorium filling from the bottom up, the cheap seats downstairs going before the rest
  • for this film? → the question mark says a title is wanted, so what you must name is the state the upper tier has been left in

An usher reporting that the cheap seats downstairs always go before any of the others.

Room at the Top is the 1959 British film of John Braine's novel about a young man clawing his way up, and the phrase itself means scope for promotion.

17 across BOY-MEETS-GIRL

In isle grey tomb crumbling, typically romantic (3-5-4)

Definition: typically romantic · anagram

The phrase names the standard romance storyline, and it is used as an adjective — a boy-meets-girl story — which is what the hyphens signal.

  • isle grey tomb → ISLEGREYTOMB anagram fodder, twelve letters
  • crumbling → anagram indicator
  • crumbling — A tomb crumbling falls into pieces that no longer sit where they were, and the letters come apart the same way.

A grey tomb falling apart on an island, which somebody finds terribly romantic.

The hyphens in the enumeration are the giveaway that a set phrase is coming rather than a word, and the definition wants a plot rather than a mood.

20 across AGREE

Have no argument with an Athenian amputee (5)

Definition: Have no argument · charade + deletion

To agree is to have no argument: once you agree there is nothing left to dispute, so the definition states it from the negative side.

  • an → A the article itself, dropped in as one letter
  • Athenian → GREEK an Athenian is a Greek, Athens being the capital of Greece
  • amputee → the amputation takes the final K off the word before it
  • amputee — An amputee has had a limb taken off, so the word beside it loses the letter at its end.

Getting on peaceably with an Athenian who has lost a limb.

'Have no argument with' reads as one phrase and the 'with' is only joinery, so the definition stops a word earlier than the ear expects.

21 across NARROWEST

Missile landing in sniper’s lair: this tightest of squeaks? (9)

Definition: tightest · container

Narrowest is tightest of margins: a narrow squeak and a tight squeeze both mean getting through with almost nothing to spare.

  • sniper’s lair → NEST a sniper shoots from a nest, the hidden position he settles into
  • Missile → ARROW an arrow is a missile in the older sense, anything shot or thrown
  • of squeaks? → surface only: it completes the idiom for a close escape, the sort you only just get through
  • landing in — A thing that lands in a place comes to rest inside it, so the missile's letters end up within the lair's.

A missile coming down on a sniper's hideout, about as close a call as anyone could have.

'Squeaks' sounds like mice in the lair; the idiom being aimed at is a narrow squeak, a hair's-breadth escape.

23 across IN TRANSIT

On journey, train isn’t moving (2,7)

Definition: On journey · anagram

Goods in transit are on their way from one place to another, so 'On journey' is the same state in plainer words.

  • train isn’t → TRAINISNT anagram fodder, nine letters
  • moving → anagram indicator
  • moving — Letters that are moving are not staying where they were put, so the ones just named shift into a new order.

A train that has stopped somewhere and is going nowhere.

'Isn't moving' reads as one phrase about a stationary train, and the two words belong to different jobs: one is material and the other is the instruction.

24 across TRACE

Track down a very tiny amount (5)

Definition: Track down · double definition

To trace somebody is to track them down, and a trace of a substance is the smallest detectable amount of it — one word, verb and noun.

  • Track down → to follow something to where it is now, as police do with a missing person
  • a very tiny amount → the barely detectable quantity a chemist reports, too small to measure properly

Hunting down the last tiny scrap of something.

The two definitions run together as one sentence about a search, and 'down' belongs to the first of them rather than to the amount.

25 across ST ANDREW

Kubrick briefly portrayed Scottish patron (2,6)

Definition: Scottish patron · charade + deletion

St Andrew is the patron saint of Scotland, whose diagonal cross flies as the saltire on the Scottish flag.

  • Kubrick → STANLEY the film director Kubrick's first name was Stanley
  • briefly → the name is cut down to the familiar short form, losing its LEY
  • portrayed → DREW to portray somebody is to draw them, and this is the past tense
  • briefly — Asking for a name briefly asks for its everyday short form, so the back end of it is left off.

Kubrick giving a short performance as Scotland's patron saint.

'Kubrick briefly portrayed' reads as one clause about a director playing a part, and 'portrayed' has been borrowed from that clause for the second half of the build.

26 across UNEASY

Not simple, so restive (6)

Definition: restive · charade

Restive means restless and hard to settle, which is how somebody uneasy feels — the word is about disquiet, not about difficulty.

  • Not → UN un- is the prefix that negates a word, as in unfair or unkind
  • simple → EASY an easy task is a simple one

Something that is not straightforward and so leaves everybody restless.

'Not simple' reads as one phrase meaning complicated, and it splits into a prefix and the word that prefix negates.

1 down CAGE-BIRD

Budgie perhaps free in big cedar (4-4)

Definition: Budgie perhaps · anagram

A budgie is one instance of the class, a bird kept in a cage, so the definition names the whole group by a single member.

  • big cedar → BIGCEDAR anagram fodder, eight letters
  • free → anagram indicator
  • free — Something set free is no longer held where it was put, and letters let loose like that can settle in any order.

A pet bird let loose among the branches of a big cedar tree.

'Perhaps' is the setter's flag for definition by example: a budgerigar is one of these rather than the word for all of them.

2 down MUSHROOM

Swell breakfast item (8)

Definition: Swell · double definition

To mushroom is to swell — costs mushroom, meaning they balloon — and the noun is a fry-up staple.

  • Swell → the verb for growing fast and wide, the way demand or a town does
  • breakfast item → one of the things in the pan beside the bacon in a British cooked breakfast

Somebody calling a breakfast dish absolutely swell.

'Swell' reads as the American adjective for excellent, praising whatever is on the plate, and the sense wanted is the verb for growing rapidly.

3 down LOOFA

An idiot picked up sponge (5)

Definition: sponge · reversal

A loofa is the dried gourd sold as a bath sponge, so 'sponge' names it by the job it does in the bathroom.

  • An → A the indefinite article, taken as its own single letter
  • idiot → FOOL a fool is an idiot
  • picked up — Picking a thing up lifts it, and in a down clue lifting means reading the letters back up the column from bottom to top.

An idiot bending down to pick a sponge up.

'An idiot' looks like the person doing the picking up, and it is the material, article included — 'An' is as much a part of the letters as the noun.

5 down REVERSE FERRET

Brazen volte-face about poem, ‘Polecat’ (7,6)

Definition: Brazen volte-face · charade

A reverse ferret is Fleet Street slang for a newspaper suddenly and shamelessly changing its line, which is a brazen volte-face.

  • about → RE re means about or concerning, the way a memo heading is written
  • poem → VERSE verse is poetry, so a poem is a piece of it
  • ‘Polecat’ → FERRET the domesticated form of the polecat, which is why the setter puts the word in quotes rather than claiming it outright

A shameless U-turn, and a poem called 'Polecat'.

'About' looks like an instruction to turn something round, and the volte-face beside it encourages that reading; it is only the two-letter word for concerning.

6 down SOLUTIONS

People searching for these here but they lack concentration (9)

Definition: People searching for these here · double definition

Solutions are what a solver hunts for in a grid, and in chemistry the same word names a dissolved mixture, weak when the concentration is low.

  • People searching for these here → 'here' is the puzzle in front of you, and everybody filling in a grid is hunting for these
  • they lack concentration → the chemist's sense, reached sideways through a property: a weak mixture is one with very little dissolved in it

A complaint that the people hunting for something here cannot keep their minds on the job.

Chemistry supplies the second sense: liquid with something dissolved in it, and a dilute one has hardly any concentration in it at all.

7 down BREAST

Reach the top of chest (6)

Definition: Reach the top of · double definition

To breast a slope is to reach its top, and the breast is the chest, so one word covers the climb and the body.

  • Reach the top of → the climber's verb: you do this to a hill when you come over its crest
  • chest → the front of the torso, from collarbone down to ribs

Climbing up to the top of a chest.

The two definitions run together as one instruction about a piece of furniture, and 'of' is the join: a runner breasts the tape and a walker breasts the hill.

8 down WESSEX

Hardy folk lived here in primarily wild county (6)

Definition: Hardy folk lived here · charade + first letter

Wessex is the old Saxon kingdom Hardy revived as the setting of his novels, so 'here' points at that fictional south-west.

  • wild → W the letter 'wild' starts with
  • county → ESSEX Essex is an English county, on the coast just east of London
  • primarily — What comes primarily is what comes first, so only the opening letter of the word after it is taken.

Tough country people once living in a rough and untamed county.

'Hardy' reads as an adjective for those tough folk, and it is Thomas Hardy, who set his novels in a half-invented corner of south-west England.

10 down LONG TIME NO SEE

Hello again, rector: still not made a bishop? (4,4,2,3)

Definition: Hello again · charade

It is what you say to somebody you have not run into for ages, which is a greeting on meeting again rather than a first hello.

  • rector: still → LONGTIME a rector who is still only a rector has been one for ages
  • not made a bishop → NOSEE a bishop is given a see, the district he presides over, so a clergyman passed over has none

Running into a long-serving rector and asking whether he has been passed over for promotion again.

A bishop's see is his diocese — the word survives in the Holy See — so a priest never promoted to one genuinely has no see to his name.

14 down AFORESAID

Ideas for a reform mentioned earlier (9)

Definition: mentioned earlier · anagram

Aforesaid is the legal drafter's adjective for something already named in the document, which is exactly what was mentioned earlier.

  • Ideas for a → IDEASFORA anagram fodder, nine letters
  • reform → anagram indicator
  • reform — To reform a thing is to shape it again, so the letters just before it are given a new arrangement.

Proposals for a reform that somebody has already brought up.

'Reform' reads as the thing the ideas are for, part of the same noun phrase, and it is the instruction acting on the words in front of it.

15 down CINERAMA

New American widescreen system (8)

Definition: widescreen system · anagram

Cinerama was the 1950s process that threw three projectors onto one deeply curved screen, so 'widescreen system' describes it plainly.

  • American → AMERICAN anagram fodder, eight letters
  • New → anagram indicator
  • New — Making a thing new is making it over from what was already there, and the letters that follow are made over the same way.

A brand-new American way of showing films on a very wide screen.

'New American' reads as one phrase about a modern invention, so nothing looks like an instruction; the process really was American, which is the setter's wink.

16 down FLATTERY

Cook breakfast, including coffee, getting insincere praise? (8)

Definition: insincere praise · container

Flattery is praise laid on to please rather than because it is true, so the question mark marks it as the sly half of the clue.

  • Cook breakfast → FRY to fry is how a breakfast gets cooked, bacon and eggs in the pan
  • coffee → LATTE a latte is a coffee, espresso lengthened with hot milk
  • including — To include a thing is to have it inside you, so the coffee named next sits within the word before it.

Cooking a fry-up with a coffee alongside, and being buttered up for it.

A fry-up is the British cooked breakfast, which is why 'Cook breakfast' points at one particular verb rather than at a whole menu.

18 down RADIUS

Spoke of what one has in each arms (6)

Definition: Spoke · double definition

A radius is a spoke, the line from a wheel's centre to its rim, and it is also the forearm bone paired with the ulna in each arm.

  • Spoke → one of the bars running from a wheel's hub out to its rim
  • what one has in each arms → the shorter of the two bones in the forearm, and everybody has one on each side

Somebody talking about what a person is carrying in each arm.

'Spoke' looks like the past tense of speak, which is the only way the surface sentence works, and the setter wants the noun from a wheel.

19 down PROTEA

Shrub and vegetable penetrated by decay (6)

Definition: Shrub · container

A protea is a South African flowering shrub, the king protea being that country's national flower, so 'Shrub' names its kind plainly.

  • vegetable → PEA a pea is a vegetable
  • decay → ROT rot is what decay does to fallen fruit
  • penetrated by — To penetrate a thing is to go right into it, so the letters named afterwards sit inside the word before.

A shrub and a vegetable going rotten in the garden.

'And' looks like it is joining two things in a list, so the shrub and the vegetable seem to be a pair; only one of them is being defined.

22 down OFTEN

Many times relent, though not at first (5)

Definition: Many times · deletion

Often means on many occasions, so 'Many times' is the plain adverbial swap: it happens often, it happens many times.

  • relent → SOFTEN to soften is to relent: you soften your line when you give ground
  • not at first → the S at the front of that word is what goes
  • not at first — This takes away the letter standing at the front, so the word has to start one place later than it did.

Somebody who gives way plenty of times, just never straight away.

'Though not at first' reads as a concession about timing — he relents in the end — and it is an instruction about the front of a word.
