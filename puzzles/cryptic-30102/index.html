Guardian Cryptic Crossword No 30,102

Across

7 across JOHN CENA

Wrestler’s trick with bad acne (4,4)

Definition: Wrestler · charade + anagram

John Cena is a WWE wrestler turned film actor, so the definition names the answer by the trade he made his name in.

  • trick → JOHN in the slang of sex work a client is a trick, and the same client is a john
  • bad acne → CENA the four letters of ACNE, returned in a different order
  • bad — 'bad' here means spoiled, gone off, and letters that have gone off no longer sit in the order they were given.

The surface is a teenager's skin complaint, and nothing in it points at a person until 'trick' is heard as a noun from another world entirely.

9 across EYELET

Small opening in Gogglebox you once rejected (6)

Definition: Small opening · charade + reversal

An eyelet is the small ringed hole punched in a shoe or a sail for a lace or a rope to pass through.

  • Gogglebox → TELE the gogglebox is British slang for the television set, the tele
  • you once → YE 'ye' is the old form of 'you', the one Chaucer's readers used
  • rejected — To reject something is to throw it back, and a word thrown back is read the other way round.

The italics make Gogglebox the television programme, people filmed watching television, while the letters need the older slang for the set in the corner.

10 across PAUL

Sample from Lulu apparently repulsed my colleague (4)

Definition: my colleague · hidden word + reversal

Paul is one of the Guardian's regular cryptic setters, so 'my colleague' identifies the answer as a name on the same rota as Brockwell.

  • Lulu apparently → LUAP four consecutive letters straddling the gap between the two words
  • Sample from — A sample is a piece taken out of something larger, so the letters are lifted from within the words that follow.
  • repulsed — To repulse is to drive back, so the letters picked out run in the opposite direction.

'Sample from Lulu' reads as a record credit, which is where the eye goes. 'My colleague' means a fellow Guardian setter, so the definition wants a person rather than a job.

11 across THE BEATLES

Musicians performing late in ancient city (3,7)

Definition: Musicians · anagram + container

'Musicians' is a plain category label, and the plural covers a four-man group whose name carries its own definite article.

  • ancient city → THEBES Thebes, the great city of ancient Egypt on the Nile
  • late → ATLE the four letters of LATE, shuffled before they go in
  • performing — To perform a piece is to give your own arrangement of it, so the word beside this comes out arranged differently.
  • in — 'in' is doing literal work: the rearranged letters go inside the city's name, not beside it.

'Musicians performing late' is a complete picture of a band on after midnight, and every word of it turns out to be doing a different job.

12 across GEORGE

Stuff clinging to the back of Pete Best? (6)

Definition: Best? · container

George Best was Manchester United's Northern Irish winger, so 'Best?' reaches a first name through one famous bearer of the surname.

  • Stuff → GORGE to stuff yourself and to gorge are the same greedy verb
  • the back of Pete → E the last letter of PetE
  • clinging to — Something clinging to an object has its arms all the way round it, so one word closes over the letter that follows.

Pete Best reads as one name, the drummer the band let go, and the clue takes him apart: Pete supplies a letter, and Best is the definition — George Best the footballer.

14 across COLORADO

Militant going into business with John Denver here (8)

Definition: Denver here · container

Denver is Colorado's capital, so 'Denver here' asks for the state that city stands in — a place named by what it contains.

  • business → CO co. for company, as in the name over a shopfront
  • John → LOO the john and the loo are American and British for the same small room
  • Militant → RAD a rad is a radical, someone at the militant end of a cause
  • going into — To go into a thing is to end up inside it, so these letters are planted within the other pieces rather than laid after them.

John Denver reads as one name, and the clue takes it in two: the first half is bathroom slang, and only the second half is defining anything.

15 across SPIDER

Ducks once more coming back for rest (6)

Definition: rest · reversal

A spider is the long-legged cue rest that arches above a ball in snooker, so 'rest' is being used in its billiard-hall sense.

  • Ducks once more → REDIPS to duck is to dip, and the re- on the front is 'once more'
  • coming back — Something coming back retraces its path, and in an across entry that means reading the letters from the right.

In snooker a spider is the arched rest you stand over the ball for a shot you cannot stretch to, so 'rest' is the table's word and not the sofa's.

17 across GRINGO

German band love Foreigner, but not in a nice way (6)

Definition: Foreigner, but not in a nice way · charade

Gringo is what Spanish-speaking Latin America calls an English-speaking outsider, and it is rarely a compliment — which the definition's second half owns up to.

  • German → G the standard single letter, as on a language label
  • band → RING a band is a ring — the thing on a finger, not the group on stage
  • love → O love is nil in tennis, and nil is written as a nought

'German band' and 'Foreigner' build a rock-music surface, and neither has anything to do with the letters: the bands here are a ring and a nought.

20 across MINOTAUR

Child cuddling cross monster (8)

Definition: monster · container

The Minotaur is the bull-headed man kept in the Cretan labyrinth, so 'monster' is the plain category the myth files him under.

  • Child → MINOR in law a minor is a child, anybody under eighteen
  • cross → TAU the tau cross is the T-shaped one, named for the Greek letter it copies
  • cuddling — To cuddle something is to hold it with your arms right round it, which is one word wrapped about another.

'Cross' is the trap: it reads as an adjective describing the child's mood, and it is a noun — one particular shape of cross with a Greek name.

22 across PAST IT

Every year songbirds going west over the hill (4,2)

Definition: over the hill · charade + reversal

To be past it is to be too old to do the thing any more, which is precisely what 'over the hill' says — two idioms for one decline.

  • Every year → PA pa is per annum, the Latin tag printed beside a salary
  • songbirds → TITS tits are the small British garden songbirds — blue tits, great tits, coal tits
  • going west — West is leftwards on the page, so an across answer going west has its letters read from the right.

The surface is birdwatching, an annual migration heading west, and the definition sits at the end in plain sight, disguised as more scenery.

23 across DAVE SEXTON

Ground doesn’t vex a Manchester United manager (4,6)

Definition: a Manchester United manager · anagram

Dave Sexton managed Manchester United from 1977 to 1981, so the definition names him as one holder of the job rather than the only one.

  • doesn’t vex a → DOESNTVEXA ten letters lifted straight off the clue, ready to be rearranged
  • Ground — Ground is the past tense of grind: whatever has been ground is broken up small and comes back in no particular order.

'Ground' reads as the stadium, which is what the football surface is for. It is the past tense of grind, and the definition runs from there to the end.

24 across STAG

Put on endless party (4)

Definition: party · deletion

A stag is short for a stag party or stag night, the men-only send-off held before a British wedding.

  • Put on → STAGE to put on a play is to stage it
  • endless → the final E is the letter that goes
  • endless — Endless means with its end removed, so the word it applies to arrives one letter short of itself.

The clue reads as a party that never stops, and the instruction is the opposite of that: something has to be cut short.

25 across MELONI

Prime minister’s note opened by billionaire (6)

Definition: Prime minister · container

Giorgia Meloni has led Italy as prime minister since 2022, so the definition names the answer by the office rather than the country.

  • note → MI mi is the third note of the sol-fa scale: doh, ray, mi
  • billionaire → ELON Elon Musk is the billionaire everyone knows by the first name alone
  • opened by — Something opened by a visitor is split to let them in, so the shorter piece is prised apart and the longer one goes inside.

'Note' looks like the leaked memo the surface promises. It is a note of music, the sort you sing, and it is only two letters long.

26 across CARDINAL

Number 19 nailing 501 (8)

Definition: Number · container

A cardinal number is a counting number — one, two, three — as against an ordinal, so 'Number' names the answer by its own grammatical class.

  • 19 → CARNAL 19 down's answer is a synonym for carnal, of the flesh
  • 501 → DI Roman numerals: D is 500 and I is one
  • nailing — To nail something is to fix it in place, and what is fixed in place here is held inside the other word.

'Number 19' reads as a shirt or a front door, and both numbers turn out to be doing something else: one points at another clue, the other is Roman.

Down

1 down DOWAGERS

Imposing women arrange bets (8)

Definition: Imposing women · charade

A dowager is a widow who keeps her late husband's title, and the word carries exactly the grand, formidable bearing 'imposing' describes.

  • arrange → DO to do a room, or someone's hair, is to arrange it
  • bets → WAGERS a wager is a bet, the older and grander word for it

'Imposing' looks like an instruction — to impose is to place one thing upon another — and it is nothing of the kind. It is half the definition, doing plain descriptive work.

2 down ANAL

Particular canine abandoned by waterway (4)

Definition: Particular · deletion

'Anal' is short for anal-retentive, so it means obsessively fussy over detail — the pointed sense of 'particular' rather than the neutral one.

  • waterway → CANAL a canal is a waterway cut by hand rather than one a river made
  • canine → C a dentist marks the canine tooth C on a chart, and that C is what walks out
  • abandoned by — To be abandoned by someone is to be left without them, so the longer word has to be read minus that letter.

'Canine' puts a dog on the towpath, which is the whole misdirection: it is the tooth, and the tooth is written as a single letter.

3 down BEETLE

Car rentals regularly in competition (6)

Definition: Car · container + alternate letters

The VW Beetle was named for its domed, insect-shaped shell, so 'Car' reaches the answer through one very famous model.

  • competition → BEE a bee is a competition — a spelling bee, a quilting bee
  • rentals regularly → ETL every other letter of rEnTaLs: the second, fourth and sixth
  • regularly — At regular intervals means every second letter of the word beside it, not the whole of it.
  • in — 'in' is literal placement: the letters it introduces land inside the following word rather than in front of it.

'Car rentals' reads as one phrase at the hire desk, which is where the misdirection lives: the definition stops dead after the first word.

4 down JEWELLER

Derisive remark about my Picasso? (8)

Definition: Picasso? · container

Paloma Picasso has designed jewellery for Tiffany for decades, so the surname names a trade through one practitioner — which the question mark admits.

  • Derisive remark → JEER a jeer is a taunt shouted at somebody
  • my → WELL 'my!' and 'well!' are the same mild exclamation of surprise
  • about — A remark made about something goes round its subject, and these letters do the same — one word closes over the other.

The surface is somebody sneering at a painting, so Pablo is the Picasso you reach for. The clue wants his daughter, who works in a different material.

5 down NECTARINES

Fruit recipe stopping tennis ace going bananas (10)

Definition: Fruit · anagram + container

A nectarine is a smooth-skinned variety of peach, so the definition is simply the plain category the answer sits in.

  • tennis ace going bananas → NECTAINES the nine letters of those two words, handed back in another order
  • recipe → R R is the short form of recipe, the word that once headed a prescription
  • stopping — To stop a gap is to plug it, so this letter is wedged into the middle rather than added at either end.
  • going bananas — Anything going bananas has lost all order, and letters that lose their order come back in a new one.

Half the vocabulary here belongs in a fruit salad, and that is the joke: 'bananas' is the one fruit word that turns out to be an instruction.

6 down BEHEAD

Top ambassador interrupting ball (6)

Definition: Top · container

To top something is to cut its top off — the gardener's word and the executioner's — which is exactly the operation the answer names.

  • ball → BEAD a bead is a small ball, the kind threaded on a string
  • ambassador → HE an ambassador is His or Her Excellency, HE in diplomatic address
  • interrupting — An interruption breaks into something already under way, so these letters land in the middle of the word rather than after it.

'Top ambassador' reads as one phrase about a senior diplomat. 'Top' is standing alone, and it is a verb.

8 down AGENCY

Means business (6)

Definition: Means · double definition

One sense is the power to act, the means by which something happens; the other is the firm that acts on a client's behalf.

  • Means → the philosopher's sense: the capacity to act, the power by which a thing gets done
  • business → a firm that acts for its clients — an advertising one, an estate one, a recruitment one

Two words and no wordplay whatever. 'Means business' is an idiom about determination, and reading it as one phrase is exactly what stops you splitting it.

13 down RHINOCEROS

One who may charge money around statue (10)

Definition: One who may charge · charade

A rhinoceros charges — lowers its horn and runs at you — while the billing sense of 'charge' belongs entirely to the surface.

  • money → RHINO rhino is old British slang for cash
  • around → C c. for circa, the letter written in front of an approximate date
  • statue → EROS the winged archer above Piccadilly Circus, London's best-known statue

The trap is 'around': it looks like an instruction to wrap one piece about another, and it pays out a single letter instead.

16 down ELATERID

3 characters in hotel ate ridiculously (8)

Definition: 3 · hidden word

An elaterid is a click beetle, the family that snaps itself upright when flipped, so it is one instance of what 3 down spells.

  • hotel ate ridiculously → ELATERID eight unbroken letters running across all three words, starting inside the first
  • characters in — Characters are letters, and the ones wanted are those standing inside the words that follow.

'Ridiculously' reads as an anagram signal, and that is the trap — it is there to finish the run of letters. The definition is the number, pointing at another entry.

18 down OKINAWAN

Islander’s fine home about to get washed out (8)

Definition: Islander · charade

An Okinawan is a person from Okinawa, so the definition is the plain -an form English builds for the inhabitant of a place.

  • fine → OK fine and OK are the same shrug of approval
  • home → IN somebody at home is in, the way a caller finds them
  • about → A Chambers lists 'a' as a worn-down form of 'about', which is where a setter finds a spare A
  • washed out → WAN a wan face is pale and drained, washed out

'About to get washed out' reads as a weather forecast, and 'about' is really standing on its own. Okinawa is the largest of the islands strung south-west of mainland Japan.

19 down EROTIC

Blue Ivy Carter initially supports Beyonce’s latest nonsense (6)

Definition: Blue · charade + first letters + last letter

'Blue' has meant indecent since the Victorian music hall, the sense that survives in blue jokes and blue films, and that is the register wanted here.

  • Beyonce’s latest → E the final letter of the singer's name
  • nonsense → ROT rot is nonsense — 'what utter rot'
  • Ivy Carter initially → IC the opening letters of the two names, in the order given
  • initially — Initially means at the start, so only the opening letter of each of those two names is wanted.
  • supports — A thing that supports sits underneath what it holds up, and in a down entry underneath means further towards the bottom.
  • latest — The latest of anything is whatever comes last, so this is the final letter of the name in front of it.

Blue Ivy Carter is Beyoncé's daughter, so the whole clue reads as one celebrity paragraph — and 'Blue', which looks like the front of the child's name, is the definition.

21 down IMAGES

Console gutted Modigliani over pictures (6)

Definition: pictures · reversal + outer letters

An image is a picture in the widest sense — a photograph, a painting, a reflection — so the two words swap in and out of each other freely.

  • Console → SEGA Sega built the Mega Drive, so a console can be clued by its maker
  • gutted Modigliani → MI M and I are the first and last letters of the painter's name
  • gutted — A gutted building keeps only its shell, so the name here loses everything between its first and last letters.
  • over — 'over' is turned over: the letters assembled in front of it are then read the opposite way.

'Console' wants to be a verb about comforting a hard-up painter, which is what makes the surface work. It is the games machine under the television.

22 down PINERY

Long track in evergreen forest (6)

Definition: evergreen forest · charade

A pinery is a plantation or wood of pines, and pines keep their needles all year, which is what makes such a wood evergreen.

  • Long → PINE to pine for something is to long for it
  • track → RY ry is the map-maker's short form of railway

'Long track' looks like a description of a railway line stretching away. 'Long' is a verb standing on its own, and the track is worth only two letters.

24 down SOIL

The Sun ringing Brockwell for dirt (4)

Definition: dirt · container

Soil is the earth in a garden or a field, and 'dirt' is the everyday word for exactly that; the scandal sense belongs only to the surface.

  • The Sun → SOL Sol is the Latin name for the sun; the italics are there to make you read the newspaper instead
  • Brockwell → I a setter in their own clue is I or ME, so the byline is worth one letter
  • ringing — To ring something is to draw a circle round it, so one piece encloses the other instead of following it.

The italics turn the sun into the tabloid, so the surface is a reporter chasing gossip — 'dirt' in the sense the clue is very happy for you to take.
