Across

1 across TAMPER-PROOF

Resistant to changing it, bar I am an expert in (6-5)

Definition: Resistant to changing · charade + deletion

Resistant to changing → TAMPER-PROOF: made so nobody can interfere with it, using the -proof sense of impervious that waterproof and bulletproof carry.

  • it, bar I → T IT with the I excepted leaves a single letter
  • am → AM the verb comes across as it stands
  • an → PER 'an' does the job of 'per' — sixty miles an hour is sixty miles per hour
  • expert → PRO a pro is the expert, the professional as against the amateur
  • in → OF the setter swaps one linking preposition for the other, the way an expert in a subject is a professor of it
  • bar — 'bar' is the preposition meaning except — all present bar one — so it throws the I out of the word beside it.

The comma is the trap: you read 'changing it' as one phrase when the wordplay wants IT with its I barred. Five small pieces, and they arrive in clue order.

9 across ICE-FREE

Cleared by air defence after skirmish, and OK to travel? (3-4)

Definition: OK to travel · anagram + deletion

OK to travel → ICE-FREE: said of a port or sea lane that winter has not frozen shut, so shipping can still get through.

  • air defence → AIRDEFENCE the fodder, before the deletion
  • and → A, N and D come out of it — the little word is doing wordplay, not joining two thoughts
  • Cleared — clearing a space means carrying away what stands in it, so the letters named are taken out before anything else happens.
  • after skirmish — a skirmish leaves whatever it touches scattered, so the surviving letters come out in a new order.

The word to delete is sitting in the clue looking like plain grammar. Once 'and' has gone, ten letters are seven, and the raid on the surface has nothing to do with it.

10 across GUERDON

One drug troublingly Tennyson’s reward? (7)

Definition: Tennyson’s reward · anagram

Tennyson’s reward → GUERDON: an archaic, poetic word for recompense, kept alive by Victorian verse, so the poet's name flags its vintage.

  • One drug → ONEDRUG the seven letters of the fodder, before they are stirred
  • troublingly — water that is troubled is stirred up and unsettled, so the letters beside the word will not stay as they were given.

Naming a Victorian poet is how a setter warns you that the word is an old one — GUERDON is the poets' term, and no modern sense is coming to rescue you.

11 across EPAULETTE

Essential for dress colours on outside of uniform? (9)

Definition: Essential for dress colours on outside of uniform? · container + middle letter + &lit

The whole clue defines it: the shoulder piece a dress uniform is not complete without, showing rank and regimental colours on the outside.

  • dress → E the middle letter of the five in 'dress'
  • colours → PALETTE a palette is the board a painter lays the colours out on; it opens after two letters
  • uniform → U U is uniform in the phonetic alphabet
  • Essential — the essence of a word is its middle, so 'Essential' asks for the letter at the very heart of the one beside it.
  • on outside of — what sits on the outside of a thing goes round it, so the painter's board wraps the single letter.

Every word does two jobs at once: the thing really is essential to a dress uniform and really does carry the colours on the outside of one. That double duty is what &lit means.

12 across THREE

In Exodus, perhaps, you’ll have run through a crowd (5)

Definition: a crowd · container

a crowd → THREE: straight out of the proverb 'two's company, three's a crowd', where the number is exactly what makes a gathering one too many.

  • In Exodus, perhaps, you → THEE the Authorised Version's word for 'you', which is the English the surface's Exodus is written in
  • run → R R is the cricket scorer's abbreviation for a run
  • through — a thing driven through another finishes up inside it, which is where the single letter lands.

The proverb does the defining — two's company, three's a crowd — and 'In Exodus, perhaps' is the setter's polite way of saying: use the Bible's word for you.

13 across ORBY

Gold in reserve not often spherical (4)

Definition: spherical · charade

spherical → ORBY: the adjective built from orb, a literary word for round or globe-shaped that survives now mainly in verse and in crosswords.

  • Gold → OR OR is gold in heraldry, and the crossword's usual gold
  • in reserve → BY money put by is money kept in reserve
  • not often → no letters in this one: it is the setter warning you that the word he wants is a rare one

'Not often' earns its place by managing expectations — ORBY is poets' English, orb plus an adjective ending, and without the warning you would never trust so short a charade.

14 across GARDEN FLAT

Frantically rang about empty domicile, having been let down – one such? (6,4)

Definition: one such? · anagram + outer letters + charade

one such? → GARDEN FLAT: the pointer looks back at the clue's own 'domicile', and a garden flat is one — the lower-ground dwelling with the yard behind.

  • rang about empty domicile → GARDEN RANG with the D and E that an emptied 'domicile' leaves, shaken together
  • having been let down → FLAT a tyre that has been let down is flat
  • Frantically — someone acting frantically does nothing in the order they meant to, and neither do these letters.
  • empty — an emptied word keeps only its walls, the first and last letters, and loses everything in between.

'One such?' points back at 'domicile', a word the wordplay has already stripped for its outer letters — and 'let down' is the tyre, not the disappointment the surface offers.

16 across EGOMANIACS

Who say, in fancy dress: ‘Can I go as me?’ (10)

Definition: Who say, in fancy dress: ‘Can I go as me?’ · anagram + &lit

The whole clue defines them: people so taken with themselves that they would come to a costume party as themselves, and their question supplies the letters.

  • Can I go as me → CANIGOASME the ten letters of the fodder, quoted as if they were speech
  • fancy dress — at a fancy-dress party nobody turns up as themselves, so the letters arrive in another guise too.

The joke is the definition: the only guest who would ask to attend a costume party as themselves is the very thing the letters of that request spell out.

19 across RUNG

Identified with a circle, called, spoke (4)

Definition: called · double definition

called → RUNG: the past participle of ring, as of a bell or a phone; spoke → RUNG: the crosspiece of a ladder, and Chambers' word for a wheel's spoke.

  • Identified with a circle → a circle is a ring, and the answer is that verb in another tense — the clue names the word before defining it twice
  • called → the past participle of 'ring' in the telephone sense: you ring someone, and later report that you have
  • spoke → past tense of speak on the surface; underneath, the bar of a wheel, which Chambers gives this word for too

Three jobs for four letters: a bell that has been rung, the rung of a wheel or ladder, and 'ring' the circle-drawing verb standing behind the whole clue.

20 across HEGEL

Absolute idealist turned on by Man (5)

Definition: Absolute idealist · charade + reversal

Absolute idealist → HEGEL: the German philosopher whose system is called absolute idealism, so the definition names him by the doctrine he founded.

  • Man → HE the pronoun a man answers to
  • on → GEL in cricket the on side is the leg side, and those three letters are turned round
  • turned — something turned faces the other way, so the cricketing word beside it is written from its far end.

'Turned on by Man' is deliberately suggestive, and the piece that matters is the cricket sense of 'on'. Absolute idealism is the name Hegel's system goes by.

21 across MEANS TEST

What might be refused by statesmen? (5,4)

Definition: What might be refused by statesmen? · anagram + &lit

The whole clue defines it: the check on income that politicians might vote down, and the very people who would refuse it supply the letters.

  • statesmen → STATESMEN the nine letters of the fodder
  • refused — read it as re-fused, melted down and run together again, and the letters beside it are free to come out in any order.

Two readings of 'refused' at once: politicians turning a policy down, and the letters of 'statesmen' fused together again into something new. The sentence defines as well as builds.

23 across NOTATOR

Controversial claim about Glastonbury? One puts the music down (7)

Definition: One puts the music down · charade

One puts the music down → NOTATOR: the person who writes music into notation, setting on paper what has so far only been played or sung.

  • Controversial claim → NOT A the contentious assertion is a flat denial: the words you would use to say the famous hill is nothing of the kind
  • Glastonbury → TOR the Somerset town's landmark is its Tor, the hill with the tower on top

Standing on Glastonbury Tor and announcing it is not a tor would indeed start an argument — and the definition is the other half of the joke, since putting music down means writing it out.

24 across TESTUDO

Tortoises originally used to supply cover for Roman soldiers (7)

Definition: cover for Roman soldiers · anagram + first letter

cover for Roman soldiers → TESTUDO: the roof of overlapping shields a legion locked overhead when advancing, named from the Latin for tortoise.

  • Tortoises originally → T the first letter of the first word
  • used to → ESTUDO the six letters of 'used to', bent into a new order
  • originally — 'originally' asks for a word at its origin, which is the single letter it begins with.
  • supply — not the verb but the adverb from supple — pliantly — so the letters beside it will bend into another shape.

'Supply' as an adverb is one of the language's best traps. The tortoises are not decoration either: the Latin for tortoise is what the legions named this shell of locked shields after.

25 across NECKERCHIEF

Cashier’s wound dressed with fine designer cravat? (11)

Definition: designer cravat · anagram

designer cravat → NECKERCHIEF: a square of cloth knotted at the throat, the working cousin of the cravat, which the question mark dresses up as designer wear.

  • Cashier’s → CHECKER the one at the till who checks your goods through is a checker
  • fine → FINE the four letters join the shuffle exactly as they stand
  • wound — not an injury but the past tense of wind: something wound round has been twisted out of the shape it started in.
  • dressed — a wound is dressed by being bound up with something else, which is how the two sets of letters come together here.

The first-aid scene is a lie in every word: 'wound' is the winding sort, 'dressed' is the assembling of letters, and the cashier is at a till rather than bleeding.

2 down MORAL

Lesson in speaking presided over by master (5)

Definition: Lesson · charade

Lesson → MORAL: the point a fable leaves you with, as in 'the moral of the story' — a lesson drawn from a tale rather than taught in class.

  • master → M M is the standard abbreviation for master
  • in speaking → ORAL an oral exam is the one done in speaking rather than in writing
  • presided over by — to preside is to sit above the gathering, so in a down entry the presiding piece is written at the top.

The schoolmaster of the surface is misdirection twice over: he supplies one letter, and the lesson wanted is the tag at the end of a fable, not an hour on a timetable.

3 down ELECTRA

Upcoming party to start celebrating Agamemnon’s daughter (7)

Definition: Agamemnon’s daughter · hidden word + reversal

Agamemnon’s daughter → ELECTRA: the Mycenaean princess of Greek tragedy, subject of plays by Sophocles and Euripides and of Strauss's opera.

  • start celebrating → ELECTRA the seven letters run unbroken backwards across the gap, beginning at the third letter of the first word
  • Upcoming — this entry runs down the grid, so 'upcoming' means the letters are read the other way, climbing back up the column.
  • party to — to be party to something is to be inside it, taking part in it — so the answer is buried in the words that follow.

Nothing in the surface admits to hiding anything, which is the whole art of it — and the Greek princess who avenged her father sits inside a sentence about a party.

4 down PIG FEED

Babe’s grub mixed up? Tucking in, girlfriend beginning to eructate (7)

Definition: Babe’s grub · container + first letter

Babe’s grub → PIG FEED: Babe is the pig of the film, so the definition names his dinner by example — what is put in the trough.

  • mixed up? → PIED pied means mottled, mixed in colour — the printer's word for type in a jumble; it opens after two letters
  • girlfriend → GF GF is how a girlfriend is abbreviated in a text message
  • eructate → E the beginning of the doctor's word for belching
  • Tucking in — tucking something in is pushing it inside and folding the edges back over it, which is where the two new pieces end up.
  • beginning to — 'beginning to' points at the very start of the word behind it, so only its first letter is taken.

Babe is the film's sheep-herding pig, so the grub is swill. 'Mixed up?' looks like an anagram signal and is really a word in its own right, PIED, waiting to be opened.

5 down OVERTONE

Open a suggestion (8)

Definition: suggestion · charade

suggestion → OVERTONE: not a proposal but a hint, the faint extra sense a remark carries — borrowed from the higher notes sounding above a struck string.

  • Open → OVERT overt means done in the open, not concealed
  • a → ONE a and one are the same number written two ways

Three words with no indicator anywhere: the whole difficulty is seeing that 'Open a' is not a phrase but two separate pieces pushed together.

6 down FEDERAL BUREAU OF INVESTIGATION

It’s mobilised for deviation and alien subterfuge around America (7,6,2,13)

Definition: It’s mobilised for deviation and alien subterfuge around America · anagram + &lit

The whole clue reads straight: the federal body mobilised over subversion and foreign espionage inside America, while those same words supply its name.

  • for deviation and alien subterfuge → FORDEVIATIONALIENSUBTERFUGE the fodder — and 'and' is only the joint between the two halves, contributing nothing
  • America → A A is the crossword's abbreviation for America, the twenty-eighth letter needed
  • mobilised — troops mobilised are called up and marched out of their usual places, which is what happens to every letter here.
  • around — 'around' says the single letter standing for the country ends up inside the assembly rather than at either end.

Twenty-eight letters, and they only add up when you notice that 'and' joins the fodder without belonging to it while 'for' does belong to it. The whole sentence defines as well.

7 down LIKE SOMETHING THE CAT BROUGHT IN

Fancy item of jazz memorabilia on Antiques Roadshow, maybe not particularly presentable? (4,9,3,3,7,2)

Definition: not particularly presentable · charade

not particularly presentable → LIKE SOMETHING THE CAT BROUGHT IN: the idiom for a bedraggled arrival, from the half-dead mouse a cat deposits proudly on the mat.

  • Fancy → LIKE to fancy something is to like it
  • item → SOMETHING an unspecified item is a something
  • of jazz → THE CAT in jazz slang a cat is a player; the phrase brings its own 'the' with it
  • memorabilia on Antiques Roadshow, maybe → BROUGHT IN people bring their keepsakes in to the programme to have them valued

A five-piece charade whose pieces are all ordinary English, which is why it hides so well: the jazz cat really would be bringing something in to be valued.

15 down BALLETIC

With Grace to summon up, ducking delivery? (8)

Definition: With Grace · charade + reversal

With Grace → BALLETIC: describing movement with a dancer's poise and control, the adjective English reaches for whenever an ordinary action looks choreographed.

  • delivery → BALL a bowler's delivery is a ball
  • to summon → ETIC to summon someone to court is to cite them, and CITE is written climbing
  • up — the entry runs down the grid, so 'up' sends the letters of the word before it back the way they came.
  • ducking — to duck is to drop below something, so the turned-round word is written underneath the other piece.

The surface is W. G. Grace ducking a bouncer, and every cricketing word is doing something else: Grace is elegance, summoning is a court's business, the delivery is only a ball.

17 down IMMERSE

Slimmers enjoy taking dip (7)

Definition: dip · hidden word

dip → IMMERSE: to dip a thing is to plunge it right under the surface, the verb sense of dip rather than the swim or the thing on a cracker.

  • Slimmers enjoy → IMMERSE the letters run unbroken across the join, starting two letters into the first word
  • taking — a word that takes something is holding it, so the two words in front are carrying the answer between them.

Read 'taking dip' as the slimmers' swim and the instruction goes past you: 'dip' is the definition, and the two words before it are the hiding place.

18 down CHAOTIC

Such parts this catastrophic? (7)

Definition: Such parts this catastrophic? · deletion + anagram + &lit

The whole clue reads straight: a catastrophe leaves everything in disorder, and these letters are left in exactly that state once the named ones have departed.

  • this catastrophic → CATASTROPHIC the twelve letters the wordplay works on
  • parts → P, A, R, T and S go — the word names the letters that leave and says that they are leaving
  • parts — 'parts' is a verb as well as a noun: to part is to leave, so the five letters it names walk out of the long word.

Take PARTS out of CATASTROPHIC and the seven letters left over will not sit still: they spell CHAOTIC, which is also what a catastrophe leaves behind it.

22 down SUSHI

Selection of exotic cakes along the way gets us high (5)

Definition: Selection of exotic cakes · hidden word

Selection of exotic cakes → SUSHI: a platter of the Japanese vinegared-rice cakes, exotic to a British solver and served as an assortment rather than singly.

  • gets us high → SUSHI the five letters run unbroken from the end of the first word into the middle of the last
  • along the way — the letters lie along the words that follow, picked up in order as you travel through them.

Nothing in the surface owns up to concealing anything: 'gets us high' reads as what the cakes do, and is really the place the answer has been sitting all along.
