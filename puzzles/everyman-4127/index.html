Observer Everyman Crossword No 4,127

Setter: Everyman · Published: Sunday 23 November 2025 · Difficulty: Moderate (harder than 20% of the puzzles here) · Grid: 15×15

Across

1 across ORAL

Otherwise lying about the French exam (4)

Definition: exam · charade + reversal

exam → ORAL: the spoken paper in a language exam, a noun in its own right rather than the adjective meaning by mouth.

  • Otherwise → OR or carries the sense of otherwise — do this, or else that
  • the French → AL the French word for 'the' is la, and it arrives the other way round
  • lying about — Something left lying about has been turned round from the way it was put down, so the letters it points at are written in the opposite order.

'Otherwise lying' looks like an anagram waiting to happen. The shuffle never comes: 'or' is quietly doing duty as a word meaning otherwise.

4 across PUBS

Round houses? (4)

Definition: Round houses? · cryptic definition

Round houses? → PUBS: a pub is a public house, and a round of drinks is what it houses, so both halves of the phrase define it at once.

  • Round houses → the surface offers circular buildings, or the engine shed where locomotives were turned
  • houses → read as a verb instead: what these places do with a round of drinks, each of them being a public house itself

Nothing is spelled out here — the clue is one sly description, and the question mark is the setter admitting he has bent 'round' from the drinks sense into an adjective.

8 across MAGNIFYING GLASS

Detective's helper having gang sign; family's worried (10,5)

Definition: Detective's helper · anagram

Detective's helper → MAGNIFYING GLASS: not a sidekick but the lens of the Sherlock Holmes cliché, the thing that helps a detective see.

  • gang sign; family's → MAGNIFYING GLASS fifteen letters handed over, and every one of them is used
  • worried — Worried in the sense a dog worries a sheep, shaking it about — and letters shaken about will not stay in the order they were given.

Count before you commit: fifteen letters of fodder for a fifteen-letter answer. The semicolon sitting in the middle of them is punctuation the setter is entitled to ignore.

11 across SIROCCO

Gentleman, wanting company, reflected: It brings warmth (7)

Definition: It brings warmth · charade + reversal

It brings warmth → SIROCCO: the wind is named for the heat it carries north from Africa, so the definition points at it by its effect.

  • Gentleman → SIR the polite form of address for a man
  • company → OCCO company is CO on a firm's letterhead, and set against its own mirror image it runs OC then CO
  • reflected — A reflection shows a thing beside its own mirror image, so those two letters appear once backwards and then once forwards.

A sirocco is the hot wind that blows off the Sahara into southern Europe, so 'it brings warmth' is meteorology rather than affection.

12 across OFFENCE

Crime of stolen goods' receiver (7)

Definition: Crime · charade

Crime → OFFENCE: the legal word for a breach of the law, singular and general, so it swaps straight into 'a serious crime'.

  • of → OF the preposition taken at face value, two letters straight off the page
  • stolen goods' receiver → FENCE a fence is the dealer who buys stolen property and passes it on

'Crime of...' reads as a single phrase, which is the whole disguise: the 'of' belongs to the wordplay, not to the definition.

13 across SAMARITAN

Airman sat about: he was once good, they say (9)

Definition: he was once good, they say · anagram

he was once good, they say → SAMARITAN: the definition is the parable itself, the passer-by who helped when everyone respectable walked on.

  • Airman sat → SAMARITAN nine letters offered up, and every one of them used
  • about — Here it means turned round and moved out of place, not concerning — letters sent about will land in a new order.

The good one of the parable is proverbial: a stranger who stopped to help a beaten traveller. 'They say' is the story being told, not a signal about how anything sounds.

14 across NEPAL

High state and somewhat saturnine pallor (5)

Definition: High state · hidden word

High state → NEPAL: state in the sense of a sovereign country, and the highest ground on earth runs along its northern border.

  • saturnine pallor → NEPAL five consecutive letters straddle the gap between the two words
  • somewhat — Somewhat means partly, and part of the words that follow is exactly what is wanted — a run of their letters, not all of them.

The surface describes an unhealthy complexion, and 'high' is the giveaway: this country sits in the Himalayas, so height is geography rather than mood.

15 across AMASS

Collect collection of glam assistants (5)

Definition: Collect · hidden word

Collect → AMASS: both are verbs for gathering things up in quantity, though amass leans towards wealth piling up over time.

  • glam assistants → AMASS five letters in a row, spanning the space between the two words
  • collection of — A collection is a part gathered out of something larger, so what follows gives up some of its letters rather than all of them.

The definition and the indicator sit side by side and mean nearly the same thing, which is what makes the seam between them so hard to see.

16 across GLOSS OVER

Explain away, before stint of bowling, England's third defeat (5,4)

Definition: Explain away · charade + third letter

Explain away → GLOSS OVER: to gloss over a fault is to smooth past it with a comfortable account of it, both being verbs you can swap.

  • England's → G count three letters into the country's name
  • defeat → LOSS a defeat is a loss, on the field as anywhere else
  • stint of bowling → OVER in cricket a bowler's stint is an over, six balls delivered from one end
  • before — A positional instruction rather than a description: what the clue names next is written in front of the piece it has just named.
  • third — It counts a position instead of describing anything — walk three letters into the word beside it and keep the one you land on.

Everyman is fond of cricket and the surface is an England collapse — but 'England's third' is a letter count, not the third wicket.

19 across SHAMPOO

Scratching backsides of stain, low-quality bathroom product (7)

Definition: bathroom product · charade + deletion

bathroom product → SHAMPOO: the everyday word for the hair-washing liquid, defined by where the bottle stands rather than by what it does.

  • stain → SHAM a stain on a reputation is a shame, docked at the end
  • low-quality → POO low-quality is poor, and it gives up its final letter too
  • Scratching backsides of — To scratch something is to strike it out, and a backside is the end of a word, so both words named lose their final letter.

The lavatory humour of 'backsides' is a joke aimed at the second piece, and 'stain' is meant abstractly — a blot on a name, not on a shirt.

21 across AT A LOSS

Confronted with a puzzle - for no monetary gain (2,1,4)

Definition: Confronted with a puzzle · double definition

One sense is being baffled with nothing to go on; the other is trading below cost, where what is lost is money rather than wits.

  • Confronted with a puzzle → the state of being baffled, with no idea what to do next
  • for no monetary gain → selling something for less than it cost you

The dash is the entire join. Two idioms share a phrase for quite different reasons, one about bafflement and one about the books.

22 across SNAKE IN THE GRASS

Small, almost bald, with dignity regularly stripped, that man: tell-tale treacherer (5,2,3,5)

Definition: treacherer · charade + deletion + alternate letters

treacherer → SNAKE IN THE GRASS: the idiom for a betrayer hidden among friends, which is what a rare noun for one who commits treachery names.

  • Small → S S is small on a clothing label
  • bald → NAKE a bald head is a naked one, docked of its final letter
  • dignity → INT the second, fourth and sixth letters, the odd ones discarded
  • that man → HE the pronoun standing in for a man
  • tell-tale → GRASS to grass is British slang for informing on someone, and a grass is the informer
  • almost — Almost a word is not quite all of it, so the last letter is left off.
  • regularly stripped — Stripped at regular intervals: every other letter is taken away and what survives is read in order.

The definition is the last word on its own: 'treacherer' is a rare form of traitor. 'Tell-tale' belongs to the wordplay, though it reads like part of the definition.

23 across WEEP

Small - minimal amount of money - sob! (4)

Definition: sob · charade

sob → WEEP: both are verbs for crying, weep being the quieter and more literary of the two.

  • Small → WEE wee is the Scottish word for small, long since at home across Britain
  • minimal amount of money → P a penny is the smallest unit of British money and is written p

Both halves are British in their own way: 'wee' came south from Scotland, and 'p' is how a penny is written and said aloud — 'fifty p'.

24 across MEAN

Stingy Everyman starts to audit nibbles (4)

Definition: Stingy · charade + first letters

Stingy → MEAN: the British sense of unwilling to spend rather than the American sense of nasty, both being adjectives for a person.

  • Everyman → ME the setter of these puzzles signs himself Everyman, and a setter speaking of himself is me
  • audit nibbles → AN the opening letter of each word, taken in order
  • starts to — A start is the opening letter, and the plural is carried by the two words that follow — one letter from each.

The surface is an accountant picking at his lunch. Nobody in it is named, which is the tell that 'Everyman' is the byline at the top of the puzzle.

Down

2 down RAILCARDS

Train-users' conveniences: bars containing source of merriment (9)

Definition: Train-users' conveniences · container

Train-users' conveniences → RAILCARDS: the discount cards British passengers buy, convenient in the plain sense of handy rather than the lavatory sense.

  • bars → RAILS bars in the sense of rails — the metal bars of a fence, or of a track
  • source of merriment → CARD a card is an amusing person, the joker who keeps a table laughing
  • containing — To contain is to hold something inside, so the piece named first opens up and the piece named next goes in.

'Source of' almost always asks for a first letter, and here it does not: the source of merriment is a whole person, not an M.

3 down LAYS OUT

Exhibits floors (4,3)

Definition: Exhibits · double definition

Exhibits puts goods on display; floors knocks a person to the ground — one phrase covering a curator and a boxer.

  • Exhibits → to exhibit is to spread things out where people can look at them
  • floors → floors as a verb, the boxing sense: knocking a man flat

Two words and nothing between them, which is the shape of a double definition. The surface reads as one phrase about a gallery's flooring, and that is the whole disguise.

4 down PONTOON

Boat 21 (7)

Definition: Boat · double definition

One sense is the flat-bottomed boat that floats a bridge; the other is the card game British players name after its winning score.

  • Boat → a flat-bottomed float, the kind lashed together to carry a temporary bridge
  • 21 → the British name for the card game called blackjack elsewhere, twenty-one being the score to hit

A two-word clue has no room for an indicator, so both halves have to be definitions — and the numeral is a whole definition rather than a number to use.

5 down BOGOF

Acronymic bargain offering, gratis, other fare? (5)

Definition: Acronymic bargain offering, gratis, other fare? · &lit + first letters

Read straight, the whole clue describes a shop's acronymic deal in which the second item costs nothing — which is what the initials spell.

  • bargain offering, gratis, other fare → BOGOF five words, five opening letters, in the order they are printed
  • Acronymic — An acronym is built out of the opening letters of a phrase, so the instruction is to take the initial of each word that follows.

BOGOF is British supermarket shorthand for buy one get one free, and the clue is its own definition: the words that describe the deal also spell it.

6 down AMUSES

Australian engages in deep thought; causes hilarity (6)

Definition: causes hilarity · charade

causes hilarity → AMUSES: the everyday sense of making people laugh, and the third-person verb matches the 'causes' of the definition.

  • Australian → A A is the standing abbreviation for Australian, as on a nationality plate
  • engages in deep thought → MUSES to muse is to ponder something at length

'Engages in' reads like a container instruction — something going inside deep thought — but nothing is inserted here. The two pieces simply sit end to end.

7 down AS WELL

Besides a balloon (2,4)

Definition: Besides · charade

Besides → AS WELL: both are adverbs meaning in addition, filling the same slot in 'he came besides' and 'he came as well'.

  • a → A the article taken at face value, one letter
  • balloon → SWELL to balloon is to swell — both verbs for puffing outwards

'Balloon' looks like the noun in a clue about a party, and the whole trick is hearing it as a verb instead.

9 down GARAM MASALA

Spicy mix: Frenchmen embraced by Australians: sadly, tabloid up for describing them? (5,6)

Definition: Spicy mix · container + reversal

Spicy mix → GARAM MASALA: the northern Indian blend of ground spices, so 'mix' is literal, a mixture, and not an instruction to shuffle.

  • tabloid → GAR a rag is a tabloid newspaper, climbing the light here
  • Australians → AA A is Australian, and the plural asks for a second one
  • Frenchmen → MM M is monsieur and MM is its plural, the standard French abbreviation
  • sadly → SALA sadly is alas, and it climbs the light too
  • embraced by — An embrace closes around whatever it holds, so the letters named first end up inside the ones named next.
  • up — The entry runs down the grid, so a piece written up the light is read back the way it came.
  • describing — To describe a circle is to draw one around something, which is what this outer piece does to everything gathered so far.

Two envelopes, one inside the other, and colons doing the work of commas — Everyman's punctuation is decoration and never part of the parse.

10 down ANNA PAVLOVA

Dancer crashed a van twice; Opel (not electric) (4,7)

Definition: Dancer · anagram + deletion

Dancer → ANNA PAVLOVA: a definition by example, pointing at one celebrated ballerina rather than at dancers in general.

  • a van twice → AVANAVAN four letters counted out, then counted out again
  • Opel → OPL the German car marque, minus the letter the bracket takes off it
  • crashed — A crash leaves its pieces in a heap, so the letters beside it are to be rebuilt in some other order.
  • twice — It doubles what has just been named: those four letters go into the heap a second time.
  • not electric — Electric is E on a plug or a car badge, and 'not' takes that single letter away before anything is rebuilt.

The dessert and the dancer share a name: Anna Pavlova was the Russian ballerina, and the meringue pudding was invented in her honour while she was on tour.

14 down NOSTALGIA

Daft analogist's rose-tinted view (9)

Definition: rose-tinted view · anagram

rose-tinted view → NOSTALGIA: looking back on the past as warmer than it really was, which is what the rose-tinted spectacles idiom describes.

  • analogist → NOSTALGIA nine letters, all of them used; an analogist is someone who reasons by analogy
  • Daft — Daft is silly, not right in the head, and letters that are not right will not stay in the order they were printed.

An unusual word in a smooth surface is nearly always fodder — nobody reaches for 'analogist' unless what they want is its letters.

15 down ASSIST

Back up donkey in pole position? (6)

Definition: Back up · charade

Back up → ASSIST: to back someone up is to help them out, and both are verbs that fit 'will you back me up'.

  • donkey → ASS the plain word for a donkey
  • in pole position? → IST pole position is the front of a motor-racing grid, first place — and first is written 1st

'Back up' looks like an instruction to reverse something, and in a down clue that is exactly what it would normally do. Here it is the definition.

16 down GROWN-UP

Adult joke, curt oath, good to recall (5-2)

Definition: Adult · charade + deletion + reversal

Adult → GROWN-UP: the noun for a fully grown person, the word children use for one, so it swaps into 'ask an adult' unchanged.

  • joke → PUN a pun is the joke that turns on a word having two meanings
  • oath → WOR an oath is a word given — 'my word!' — and it comes here with its last letter cut off
  • good → G G is the standing abbreviation for good, as on a school report
  • curt — Curt means cut short, so the word it describes arrives without its final letter.
  • to recall — To recall something is to call it back, and a piece called back is written in the opposite direction.

Build the three pieces in clue order and then read them from the bottom of the entry upwards. 'Oath' is the 'word' of 'my word!', not a curse.

17 down ON A WHIM

For fun, circle that guy that's eaten pasty up (2,1,4)

Definition: For fun · container + reversal

For fun → ON A WHIM: doing a thing on a whim is doing it for the fun of it, on a sudden fancy with no reason behind it.

  • circle → O the letter is drawn as a circle, which is why O also does duty for nothing, love and a ring
  • pasty → NAW pasty means pale and sickly — wan — climbing the light
  • that guy → HIM the pronoun for a man already mentioned
  • that's eaten — Something eaten has gone inside, so the letters it names are swallowed by the ones already assembled.
  • up — The entry runs down the grid, so a piece written up the light comes out the other way round.

'Pasty' wants to be the Cornish pie, especially with 'eaten' alongside it. It is the adjective for a pale face instead.

18 down RESIST

Confront second attempt at exam about Sweden (6)

Definition: Confront · container

Confront → RESIST: both are verbs for standing against something, though resist is holding out where confront is going out to meet it.

  • second attempt at exam → RESIT a resit is the exam a British student takes again after failing it
  • Sweden → S S is Sweden's international vehicle code, the letter on the back of its cars
  • about — About means around here rather than concerning: the piece before it closes around the letter that follows.

Everyman likes country codes from car plates — S, D, F, E — and they are worth learning, because they turn up wherever a single letter is wanted.

20 down PIECE

Heard last word of Silent Night: sidearm? (5)

Definition: sidearm · homophone

sidearm → PIECE: slang for a handgun, the sense in 'carrying a piece', which is why the setter flags it with a question mark.

  • last word of Silent Night → PIECE the carol closes on 'sleep in heavenly peace', and that word said aloud is written another way
  • Heard — It asks for the sound of a word rather than its spelling, so what goes into the grid is a different word that says the same thing.

A sidearm is a handgun, and a Christmas carol and a pistol have nothing whatever to do with each other — which is where the joke lives.
