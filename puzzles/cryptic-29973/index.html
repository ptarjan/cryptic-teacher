Across

7 across CONTRACT

Make short counterargument with pamphlet (8)

Definition: Make short · charade

To contract something is to draw it in so that it takes up less room, which is making it short as a verb.

  • counterargument → CON the con is the case against, the other half of the pros and cons
  • pamphlet → TRACT a tract is a short printed pamphlet, usually arguing a religious or political case

Boiling down an objection and issuing it as a pamphlet.

'Make short counterargument' reads as one instruction — abridge the objection — and that is the trap: the definition stops dead after two words.

9 across BRIDGE

Part of ship that crosses river, often (6)

Definition: Part of ship · double definition

A ship's bridge is the raised platform the vessel is steered from; the other kind carries a road or railway from bank to bank.

  • Part of ship → the raised deck with the wheel and the windows, where the officer of the watch stands
  • that crosses river, often → the structure that carries a road or a railway over water — though plenty of them span roads, valleys and railways instead, which is what 'often' admits

One part of a ship, described as the bit that usually gets you over the water.

Read 'Part of ship that crosses river' as a single phrase and you are hunting for a hull or a ferry. The clue is two definitions laid end to end, and the join is invisible.

10 across CAPE

Cold primate in sleeveless cloak (4)

Definition: sleeveless cloak · charade

A cape is the short cloak that hangs from the shoulders with no sleeves at all, so the definition describes the garment exactly.

  • Cold → C C is the letter on the cold tap and on a weather chart
  • primate → APE the zoological order, and its best-known member

A shivering monkey wrapped up in a cloak.

'Primate' is the setter's favourite double meaning: an archbishop is a primate too, and an ecclesiastical garment is exactly what the definition sounds like it is heading for.

11 across GOOSEBERRY

Kind of fool whose company isn’t wanted? (10)

Definition: Kind of fool · double definition

Gooseberry fool is a real pudding, so 'kind of fool' defines by example; and to play gooseberry is to be the unwanted third with a couple.

  • Kind of fool → a fool is the pudding of crushed fruit folded into cream, and this is the sharp green fruit that makes the classic one
  • whose company isn’t wanted → the third person trailing after a courting couple who would rather be alone, an idiom every British solver grows up with

An idiot nobody can bear to spend an evening with.

'Fool' is the trap and it is a good one: it reads as an insult and it is a dessert, so the whole first half of the clue means something other than it seems.

12 across SIMPLE

In some clues, I’m pleasantly straightforward (6)

Definition: straightforward · hidden word

Something simple has nothing complicated about it, which is the everyday sense of straightforward applied to a task or an explanation.

  • clues, I’m pleasantly → SIMPLE six consecutive letters run from the end of the first word, across the second and into the third
  • In some — Saying you need only some of what follows, and that it is in there, points at a run of letters already written out rather than at a whole word.

The setter promising that at least some of today's clues will be kind to you.

Brendan is talking about his own puzzle, which is why the sentence reads so naturally that you never count its letters. The apostrophe carries no instruction at all.

14 across INTEREST

Present setter’s anagram to intrigue (8)

Definition: intrigue · anagram

To interest somebody is to catch and hold their curiosity, which is what intrigue means as a verb.

  • Present → IN someone present is in, the word a register or a receptionist uses
  • setter’s → SETTER six more letters of the fodder, the possessive doing nothing but joining them on
  • anagram — The word names the device outright: whatever letters stand beside it are to be re-sorted, and here it hides by being something a setter would genuinely be presenting.

The setter offering up an anagram in the hope of tempting you in.

Naming the device out loud is the disguise. A setter presenting an anagram is an ordinary thing to say, so the instruction reads as scenery instead of an order.

15 across MILTON

Poet’s one line in masculine style (6)

Definition: Poet · charade + container

Milton wrote Paradise Lost, so 'Poet' names the answer as one instance of the trade rather than defining the word.

  • masculine → M M is the standard abbreviation for masculine
  • one → I I is one in Roman numerals
  • line → L L is the usual short form of line
  • style → TON ton, borrowed from the French, is style or fashionable society — the Regency's word for the smart set
  • in — In puts what comes before it inside what comes after, so the two single letters are planted between the pieces named next.

A poet managing a single line in a thoroughly manly manner.

Three of the four pieces are single letters, so almost nothing here is a word: the clue is assembled letter by letter and only the last chunk is worth learning.

17 across KEYNES

Economist making crucial point inconclusively (6)

Definition: Economist · charade + deletion

John Maynard Keynes is the economist who argued that governments should spend their way out of a slump, so the bare word names him by his trade.

  • crucial → KEY the key point in an argument is the crucial one
  • point → NES a ness is a headland, the point of land that runs out into the sea, as in Sheerness — and its last letter never arrives
  • inconclusively — What is done inconclusively stops short of the end, so the word it applies to never reaches its final letter.

An economist making a vital point and not quite clinching it.

'Point' in a crossword is usually a compass direction, N or S or E or W, and that expectation is what hides the coastal sense the clue actually wants.

20 across INTERIOR

Private put in grave situation backed foreign ruler (8)

Definition: Private · charade + reversal

Interior means inner, and a person's interior life is their private one, so the adjective substitutes directly.

  • put in grave situation → INTER to inter is to put a body in a grave; 'situation' fills out the picture without adding a letter
  • foreign ruler → IOR roi is French for king, and his three letters are set down right to left
  • backed — Something backed has been turned to face the other way, so an across piece is written from its last letter to its first.

A private soldier in deep trouble who came out in support of a foreign king.

'Private' is the rank the whole surface is built on, and a crossword usually turns that into PTE or GI. Here it is not wordplay at all — it is the definition.

22 across DESIGN

Reluctantly agree about son’s intention (6)

Definition: intention · container

To have designs on something is to intend to get it, so design is a plan or purpose rather than a drawing.

  • Reluctantly agree → DEIGN to deign is to agree while making plain you are lowering yourself, and the word parts after its second letter
  • son → S S is the standard abbreviation for son
  • about — About means round, so the letters of the piece named first open up and close again around what comes next.

A parent grudgingly going along with what a son means to do.

The definition is the sense in 'by design' or 'have designs on' — a purpose, not a pattern — and the surface's family argument is what keeps the drawing sense in your head.

23 across ARITHMETIC

Thing reversed in chart I revised as piece of mathematics (10)

Definition: piece of mathematics · anagram + reversal + container

Arithmetic is the branch of mathematics that deals with numbers and the four operations, so it is one part of the wider subject.

  • chart I → ARITHC six letters resettled into a new order, then parted to make room
  • Thing → METI a thing is an item, and these are its four letters read the other way round
  • reversed — Reversed says the piece is turned end for end, so an across entry takes those letters from the last to the first.
  • in — In means inside, so the turned-round piece is planted among the shuffled letters rather than set beside them.
  • revised — Revising something means going back over it and altering it, so these letters will not stay in the order they are given.

Something turned the wrong way round in a chart that had to be revised.

'Chart I revised' reads as the setter talking about his own diagram, and the 'I' is nothing of the kind: it is one more letter of the fodder.

24 across MEAN

Humble soldiers protecting area (4)

Definition: Humble · container

A man of mean birth is one of humble birth — the old sense of low in rank, not the modern unkind or average.

  • soldiers → MEN the men are the ranks as against the officers, and the word parts after its second letter
  • area → A A is the standard abbreviation for area
  • protecting — Troops protecting something close round it on all sides, so the letters of the first piece open and take the next inside.

A guard of soldiers thrown round a patch of ground.

The definition wants a sense that has nearly died out — low in rank, as in mean streets or mean birth — while the two everyday senses, unkind and average, lead nowhere.

25 across DOUBLE

Stand-in to make score of ten, say (6)

Definition: Stand-in · double definition

A double is the stand-in who takes an actor's place, and to double ten is to turn it into twenty, which is a score.

  • Stand-in → the actor who replaces the star for the dangerous shots and the distant ones
  • make score of ten, say → a score is twenty, so making a score out of ten means multiplying it by two — and 'say' admits that any other number would do as well

A substitute sent on with instructions to knock up ten.

'Score' looks like a total in a match, which is what a stand-in on the field suggests. It is the old counting word for twenty, as in four score and seven years ago.

26 across SAUCEPAN

Something used by cooks in USA – pecan nuts (8)

Definition: Something used by cooks · anagram

A saucepan is the deep pan with a long handle that a cook boils and simmers in, so the definition names it by who reaches for it.

  • USA – pecan → USAPECAN anagram fodder, eight letters
  • nuts — Calling something nuts says it has gone haywire, and letters gone haywire are not in the order they arrived in.

American pecans going into whatever the cook has on the hob.

'Nuts' has to be read as mad rather than as the pecans it is sitting beside, and the dash does nothing but keep the surface readable.

Down

1 down VOCALISM

It’s uttered very briefly on weird social media, initially (8)

Definition: It’s uttered · charade + anagram + first letter

Vocalism is sound made with the voice, the exercise of the vocal organs, so the definition names a thing the voice produces.

  • very briefly → V V is the everyday shortening of very, as in a school report's 'v good'
  • social → OCALIS six letters resettled into a new order
  • media → M the opening letter of the word, taken on its own
  • briefly — Briefly asks for the short form of the word beside it, and the shortest form a word can have is its recognised single-letter abbreviation.
  • weird — Weird means out of the ordinary shape, so the letters beside it will not stand in the ordinary order they arrive in.
  • initially — Initially points at the start of a word, so only the letter that opens it is taken.

A rumour going round briefly on some strange corner of social media.

'Social media' is one modern phrase and the clue splits it in two, taking one word for its letters and the other for its initial.

2 down STYE

Eye problem from middle of last year (4)

Definition: Eye problem · middle letters

A stye is the small painful swelling that comes up on an eyelid, which is a problem of the eye rather than of sight.

  • last year → STYE eight letters run together, and the two at each end are dropped
  • middle of — The middle of a written phrase is what survives when you bite an equal amount off each end, so the phrase is measured rather than read.

An eye complaint carried over from halfway through last year.

'Middle of last year' reads as a date, somewhere around June, and that is the whole disguise: it points at the centre of a phrase, not at a month.

3 down TANGLE

Seaweed gourmet ultimately put on fish (6)

Definition: Seaweed · charade + last letter

Tangle is the name coastal Britain gives to the broad-fronded kelp that washes up on beaches, so the definition is the plant's plain name.

  • gourmet → T the last letter of the word, taken on its own
  • fish → ANGLE to angle is to fish with rod and line
  • ultimately — Ultimately means at the very end, so what is taken from the word beside it is the letter that finishes it.
  • put on — Putting one thing on another rests it on top, and in a down entry the top is where the letters begin.

A food writer's last word on seaweed, laid over a fish course.

'Fish' is the verb, not the animal, which is where the clue is hardest: everything around it is edible, so you read it as a thing on a plate.

4 down ABSENTEE

Problem pupil stimulated in a communal meeting (8)

Definition: Problem pupil · container

An absentee is the one who is not there, so in a school register the definition names the pupil by the trouble the empty seat causes.

  • a communal meeting → ABEE a bee is a gathering of neighbours for a shared job — a quilting bee, a spelling bee — and with the clue's own 'a' in front the letters part to make room
  • stimulated → SENT to be sent is to be carried away with excitement, as in music that really sends you
  • in — In means inside, so the piece named before it is planted within the letters that follow rather than laid beside them.

A troublemaker at school getting worked up at some village get-together.

'Sent' for thrilled is jazz slang and is the step most solvers stall on. The surface's problem pupil is present and misbehaving, which is exactly the opposite of the answer.

5 down FIREBRANDS

Radicals assuming rising changes image (10)

Definition: Radicals · charade + reversal

A firebrand is an agitator who stirs people up, so the plural sits straight in for radicals in the political sense.

  • assuming → FI assuming a thing is supposing it, the job the little word 'if' does, and its two letters are read from the bottom up
  • changes image → REBRANDS a company that changes its public image rebrands
  • rising — What rises travels upwards, and a down answer read upwards takes the letters of that piece from the bottom of the grid to the top.

Radicals reckoning that an uprising will change how they are seen.

'Assuming rising' reads as one idea, revolutionaries counting on an uprising, and it is really two instructions: a three-letter conjunction and an about-turn.

6 down AGORAS

Wide open spaces since found, after a journey over river (6)

Definition: Wide open spaces · charade

The agora was the public square of a Greek city, the market and meeting ground, so the plural names open spaces at the heart of a town.

  • a → A the clue's own word, taken as it stands
  • journey → GO a go is a turn or spell at something, as in having a go
  • river → R R is the map-maker's short form for river
  • since → AS as means since in the causal sense — as it was raining, we stayed in
  • found, after — Something found after a thing comes along behind it, so this piece is parked at the end of everything the rest has built.
  • over — A piece written over another stands above it, and in a down entry standing above means coming first.

Broad empty country discovered at the end of a trip across a river.

Every piece here is two letters or fewer, so there is nothing solid to grip: the clue is assembled from four scraps in a different order from the one it reads in.

8 down TROPIC

Subject embracing monarch in global circle (6)

Definition: global circle · container

Cancer and Capricorn are each a line of latitude drawn right round the earth, so the definition names one of them by its shape on the globe.

  • Subject → TOPIC the subject under discussion is the topic, and the word parts after its first letter
  • monarch → R R is rex or regina, the letter a monarch signs after the name
  • embracing — An embrace closes right round whatever is held, so the word doing it opens up and takes the next letter inside.

A subject of the Crown who holds his monarch dear the world over.

'Subject' is the word being talked about, not a citizen, and the surface leans hard the other way by putting a monarch next to it.

13 down POLLEN TUBE

What helps reproduce stock, say? Pet bull one redeployed (6,4)

Definition: What helps reproduce stock, say · anagram

A pollen tube grows down from a grain to deliver the male cells that fertilise a flower, so it is what lets a plant like stock breed.

  • Pet bull one → PETBULLONE anagram fodder, ten letters
  • redeployed — Redeploying troops sends them to new positions, so these letters take up different places from the ones they start in.

A farmer moving his prize bull about to get the herd in calf.

'Stock' is the scented garden flower here, not livestock, and 'say' marks it as one example of a plant — which is what makes the cattle-breeding surface so convincing.

16 down OPIUM DEN

Supplier of drug mostly impounded, destroyed (5,3)

Definition: Supplier of drug · anagram + deletion

An opium den is the establishment where the drug was sold and smoked, so 'supplier' names a place rather than a person.

  • impounded → IMPOUNDE the word gives up its final letter, leaving eight to be broken up
  • mostly — Mostly means all but a little, and the little you leave off a written word is the letter at the end of it.
  • destroyed — Something destroyed has been broken up, so its letters do not survive in the order they were given.

Most of a dealer's stock seized by the police and then burnt.

'Supplier of drug' reads as a dealer, a person, and the answer is a room. The surface's seizure keeps you looking for someone to arrest.

18 down SIGN AWAY

Relinquish or keep subscribing? (4,4)

Definition: Relinquish · double definition

To sign away a right is to surrender it by putting your name to a document; and since 'away' can mean incessantly, it also reads as going on signing.

  • Relinquish → what you do to a right or a property by putting your name at the bottom of a form
  • keep subscribing → to subscribe is literally to write your name underneath, and keeping at anything is doing it away

A choice between giving something up and carrying on paying for it.

'Away' does two entirely different jobs: in the first sense it carries the rights off for good, and in the second it means without pause, as in work away or chatter away.

19 down GRATIS

Free traitor imprisoned by US troops (6)

Definition: Free · container

Gratis means at no charge, so 'Free' is the price sense of the word rather than the liberty the surface is pushing.

  • US troops → GIS American soldiers are GIs, from the government issue stamped on their kit, and the word opens after its first letter
  • traitor → RAT a rat is the informer who sells his own side
  • imprisoned by — Being imprisoned by somebody puts you inside their walls, so the piece named first is shut in by the letters that follow.

A turncoat locked up by the Americans and then let go.

'Free' opens the clue looking like a verb the surface needs — release the prisoner — and it is an adjective about money instead.

21 down NARROW

New missile limited in scope (6)

Definition: limited in scope · charade

Narrow interests or a narrow outlook cover very little ground, which is what being limited in scope means of a person or a subject.

  • New → N N is the standard abbreviation for new
  • missile → ARROW a missile is anything shot or thrown, not just the modern sort

A new weapon with a disappointingly short range.

'Missile' sounds like hardware from a defence budget and covers the oldest projectile there is, which is the whole distance the clue asks you to travel.

22 down DICTUM

Richard’s pronouncement on corporation, statement of principle (6)

Definition: statement of principle · homophone + charade

A dictum is a short formal saying that lays down a principle, the sort of line a judge or a critic is quoted for.

  • Richard’s pronouncement → DIC Richard's familiar short form is Dick, and only the sound of it is wanted, so the hard closing letter is never written
  • corporation → TUM a corporation is old comic English for a pot belly — a tum
  • pronouncement — A pronouncement is something spoken, so the name in front of it is wanted for the noise it makes rather than the way it is spelt.

Richard delivering his verdict on some large company.

'Corporation' is the joke: not a company at all but the Victorian word for a paunch. Say the two halves one after the other and you are already saying the answer.

24 down MEET

These days, rarely fit for sports event (4)

Definition: fit · double definition

Meet is an old adjective for fitting or proper, and a meet is also the fixture at which athletes or swimmers compete.

  • These days, rarely fit → fit in the old sense of proper or seemly, a use now confined to prayers and Shakespeare — which is what 'these days, rarely' is warning you about
  • sports event → an athletics or swimming fixture, where several clubs come together to compete

A complaint that hardly anybody is in shape for athletics nowadays.

'These days, rarely' is not an instruction but a usage label, telling you the first sense is archaic. The surface reads it as a remark about national fitness.
