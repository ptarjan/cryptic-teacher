Observer Everyman Crossword No 4,143

Setter: Everyman · Published: Sunday 15 March 2026 · Difficulty: Gentle (harder than 4% of the puzzles here) · Grid: 15×15

Across

1 across ICARUS

A little vicar used to be high-flier (for a while) (6)

Definition: high-flier (for a while) · hidden word

  • vicar used → ICARUS six letters running consecutively from the second letter of the first word, straight across the gap into the second

The bracket is the joke: the Greek boy's wax wings held him up until the sun got at them. A parish clergyman is the disguise, and none of him is wordplay.

5 across ASSUME

Take as read: Everyman twit first and foremost; secondarily, buffoon (6)

Definition: Take as read · charade + second letter

  • twit → ASS calling someone an ass is calling them a fool, which is what a twit is
  • buffoon → U the letter standing in position two of that word
  • Everyman → ME the setter signs every one of these puzzles with that name, so when he writes it he means himself

The semicolon separates two instructions that read as one long insult. Everyman's own byline is a piece of the wordplay, which is a habit worth expecting in this puzzle.

8 across ARCTIC OCEAN

Much water wanting vigorous aeration, carbon thrice (6,5)

Definition: Much water · anagram

  • aeration → AERATION eight letters of fodder, handed over whole
  • carbon thrice → CCC C is the chemical symbol for carbon, and it is wanted three times over

The surface is a fish tank in need of a pump. Counting eleven letters of fodder against the (6,5) enumeration is what confirms the shuffle before any answer suggests itself.

11 across WHITE FLAG

Ashen prisoner accepting fine, showing sign of submission (5,4)

Definition: sign of submission · charade

  • Ashen → WHITE a face drained of all its colour is described as ashen
  • fine → F F is the grade stamped on a pencil for fine, the harder, thinner lead
  • prisoner → LAG an old lag is British slang for a convict who has served plenty of time

The surface is a convict taking his punishment on the chin. F for fine is the pencil grade, nothing to do with money, and it is the smallest of the three pieces.

12 across SEIZE

Take control of waters, as dictated (5)

Definition: Take control of · homophone

  • waters → SEIZE the world's great bodies of water are the seas, and that single sound has a second spelling in English

One sound, two spellings, and the naval surface — taking control of the waters — is smooth enough that the whole device is easy to walk straight past.

13 across MOISTEN

Perhaps lick fourth of stamps: it's one that's damaged (7)

Definition: Perhaps lick · anagram + fourth letter

  • stamps → M count four letters into the word and stop there
  • it's one → ITSONE six letters of fodder, taken exactly as they are written, apostrophe ignored

The colon makes the clue read as a note to self — lick the stamps — and the two halves of that phrase are doing quite different jobs, one defining and one surrendering a letter.

14 across NON-STOP

Steadily, at pool table, sinks last of seven ... on ... on a roll (3-4)

Definition: Steadily · charade + reversal

  • at pool table, sinks → POTS to sink a ball on a pool table is to pot it
  • last of seven → N the closing letter of that number written out
  • on → ON the little word taken at face value

Everyman strings the sentence out with ellipses so that 'on ... on' reads as a chant from the crowd. Only the first of the two is a piece; the second belongs to the instruction.

15 across REDACTS

Censors in Communist legislation (7)

Definition: Censors · charade

  • Communist → RED a red has meant a communist ever since the colour of the revolutionary flag
  • legislation → ACTS laws that have passed through parliament are acts

The clue reads as a headline about state control of the press, and that is the whole disguise — the two halves are separate pieces laid end to end.

17 across BUCKETS

Receptacles for pelts (7)

Definition: Receptacles · double definition

  • Receptacles → the plain sense: the things you carry water in
  • pelts → when rain pelts down it comes in torrents — a verb about heavy weather, not the animal skins the surface offers

'Receptacles for pelts' conjures a tannery, and that is the entire misdirection: 'pelts' is what rain does, so both halves are straight definitions.

20 across WOUND

Tightened injury (5)

Definition: Tightened · double definition

  • Tightened → the past tense of wind: a clock spring that has been turned tight
  • injury → a cut or a gash, the noun a nurse dresses and bandages

Two words side by side and nothing else to go on. They share five letters and no sound at all: one rhymes with found, the other with tuned.

21 across GOLF CLUBS

Where Woods would use woods - or woods? (4,5)

Definition: Where Woods would use woods · double definition

  • Where Woods would use woods → Tiger Woods is the golfer, and the place he would swing a driver is a venue with a course and a clubhouse
  • woods → a wood is the club with the big rounded head, once carved from persimmon and now made of metal

Three woods in one sentence and not a tree among them: a surname, a set of clubs, then the same set again. The capital W is the only signpost on offer.

22 across TALENT SCOUT

I'm seeking that special someone in foreign consulate, time and time again (6,5)

Definition: I'm seeking that special someone · anagram

  • consulate → CONSULATE nine letters of fodder, a country's office in a foreign city
  • time and time again → TT T is the standard abbreviation for time, and the phrase asks for it twice over

'Time and time again' reads as an idiom for endless repetition and is really an instruction to write one letter down twice. The definition speaks in the answer's own voice.

23 across ERRATA

Mistakes rare at resorts (6)

Definition: Mistakes · anagram

  • rare at → ERRATA six letters of fodder, which is the whole length of the entry

The surface makes a claim about holiday hotels, and the holiday word is the instruction. Watch for it: 'resorts' is a favourite because it hides a hyphen.

24 across ROSTRA

Extract of intro's transcribed as lecturers talk here (6)

Definition: lecturers talk here · hidden word

  • intro's transcribed → ROSTRA six letters in a row, starting three into the first word and crossing both the apostrophe and the space

The apostrophe is the only real obstacle, and it is not one: a hidden run ignores punctuation and the gaps between words and reads straight on through.

2 down CLARIFIED BUTTER

Explained: goat maybe ingredient for frying (9,6)

Definition: ingredient for frying · charade

  • Explained → CLARIFIED to clarify a point is to explain it, making it clear
  • goat maybe → BUTTER a goat butts with its horns, so it is one that butts, and 'maybe' flags it as only one example of such a creature

The colon dresses 'Explained' up as a heading introducing the rest of the sentence, when it is really the first half of a two-part build.

3 down RICHEST

Most intense potpourri chests will contain (7)

Definition: Most intense · hidden word

  • potpourri chests → RICHEST seven consecutive letters beginning near the end of the first word and running on into the second

Potpourri and chests belong to a surface picture of a scented drawer. Neither is being defined — they are only the wrapping the letters travel in.

4 down SKI-PLANES

Avoid narrow streets in cold-weather vehicles (3-6)

Definition: cold-weather vehicles · charade

  • Avoid → SKIP to skip a meeting is to avoid going to it
  • narrow streets → LANES a lane is the narrowest kind of street, wide enough for a single line of traffic

'Avoid narrow streets' reads as advice to a driver, and that is the whole trap: the two words are clued separately, with the hyphen in the enumeration marking the join.

5 down ALONG

By the side of a pine (5)

Definition: By the side of · charade

  • a → A the single letter taken straight off the clue
  • pine → LONG to pine for someone is to long for them

'a pine' offers you a tree, and the tree is a noun. The clue wants the verb, and five letters is far too few for a species anyway.

6 down STEPSON

Relative in cowboy hat having change of heart (4-3)

Definition: Relative · substitution

  • cowboy hat → STETSON the tall, broad-brimmed felt hat of the American West, named after the hatter John B Stetson
  • having change of heart → P the fourth of those seven letters steps aside and P takes the empty seat

In the surface a change of heart is a softening of feeling towards a relative. Here it is strictly arithmetic: one letter, dead centre, replaced by another.

7 down MIND IN THE GUTTER

Rude intent might - possibly - give you this? (4,2,3,6)

Definition: Rude intent might - possibly - give you this? · &lit + anagram

  • Rude intent might → MIND IN THE GUTTER fifteen letters of fodder, exactly the count the enumeration asks for

The dashes make 'possibly' look like an aside from the setter. Read straight, the sentence describes a state of mind; read again, its first three words are the raw material for it.

9 down SWIM

Small worm, exhausted, clenching - first to crawl? (4)

Definition: crawl · charade + outer letters + container

  • Small → S S is the size printed on a clothing label
  • worm, exhausted → WM the first and last letters of the creature, with its insides emptied out
  • first → I first is number one, and the Roman numeral for one is I — it goes between the two letters above

'first to crawl' paints a baby on the carpet, and the crawl in question is the swimming stroke. The dash is doing no work at all except keeping that picture alive.

10 down KEEP

Tower: look up (4)

Definition: Tower · reversal

  • look → KEEP a quick look is a peek, and here it is written climbing the grid instead of descending it

The colon does all the misdirection. 'Tower: look up' reads as an instruction to raise your eyes, when the second half is simply a word to be turned round.

14 down NEBULISER

Spray blue rinse all over the place (9)

Definition: Spray · anagram

  • blue rinse → NEBULISER nine letters of fodder, the same count the enumeration asks for

A blue rinse is the pale tint an old-fashioned hairdresser puts on grey hair, which is why the phrase sits so comfortably next to a spray. It is material, not description.

15 down ROWS

Disputes files (4)

Definition: Disputes · double definition

  • Disputes → the noisy-quarrel sense, the one that rhymes with cows
  • files → soldiers drawn up in ranks and files are standing in lines, and a line of things is the other reading

Two words and no other wordplay, and the sly part is that the two are not even said the same way: one rhymes with cows, the other with nose.

16 down CEDILLA

Provençal character? (7)

Definition: Provençal character? · cryptic definition

  • Provençal → the surface offers a person from Provence; the setter is holding the word itself up for inspection, hook and all
  • character? → not a personality but a written mark, the sense in which a keyboard carries a hundred of them

The clue is its own example: the mark being described is printed right there under the c of Provençal, and 'character' quietly trades a person for a piece of type.

18 down CYCLOPS

Monster, one I only mentioned? (7)

Definition: Monster, one I only mentioned? · cryptic definition

  • Monster → the straight half — a giant out of Homer, the shepherd Odysseus blinded with a burning stake
  • one I only mentioned? → on the page that is the pronoun, but in the mouth it is the organ this giant has exactly one of

The joke lives entirely in the ear: written down it is a pronoun, spoken it is an eye, and a single eye is the one thing everybody knows about the creature.

19 down SESH

Primarily slangy expression signifying hangin'? (4)

Definition: Primarily slangy expression signifying hangin'? · &lit + first letters

  • slangy expression signifying hangin' → SESH the four initials, read straight off the four words in the order they stand

The apostrophe on 'hangin'' does double duty: it marks the dropped g of casual speech, and casual speech is exactly the register of the word being spelled out.

21 down GENOA

Are you familiar with that woman delivered in Italian port? (5)

Definition: Italian port · homophone

  • Are you familiar with that woman → GENOA the question collapses in speech to 'd'ye know 'er?', with the aitch dropped and the three words run together

The sounded phrase is a whole question rather than a single word, and it only works at conversational speed — the sort of run-together English nobody ever writes down.
