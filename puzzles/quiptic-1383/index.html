1 across PROVIDE

Supply one paid to get a lot of footage (7)

Definition: Supply · charade + deletion

'Supply' and the answer are the same verb: to furnish someone with what they need, interchangeable in almost any sentence.

  • one paid → PRO someone paid to do what others do for love is a pro
  • footage → VIDE footage is VIDEO, and only most of that word is wanted, so the final letter drops off
  • a lot of — 'A lot of' something is most of it and not the whole of it, so the word it points at arrives with its tail missing.

Money handed over to buy a decent stretch of film.

'Supply' reads as the money in the sentence and is really the definition, standing alone before any wordplay starts. The paid one is the professional of sport and music, not an employee.

5 across BAGHDAD

Start to bring a good husband and father in Middle East capital (7)

Definition: Middle East capital · charade + first letter

The answer is the capital city of Iraq, so the definition places it by region and status rather than offering any synonym.

  • Start to bring → B the opening letter of the verb, and nothing else of it
  • a → A the article stands for itself
  • good → G the standing abbreviation, as on a school report
  • husband → H the standing abbreviation, used on forms and family trees
  • father → DAD the familiar word for a father
  • Start to — 'Start to' points at where a word begins, so only the opening character of the word behind it is taken.

Someone introducing a decent husband and his father.

Four of the seven letters never look like letters at all: 'good' and 'husband' are standing one-letter abbreviations, and 'a' supplies itself, which is how a whole domestic sentence collapses into a city.

10 across AGOG

Very eager group among Chicago gangsters (4)

Definition: Very eager · hidden word

'Very eager' is the plain adjectival sense: all eyes and ears with excitement about what is coming.

  • Chicago gangsters → AGOG four letters in a row, starting late in the first word and finishing just inside the second
  • group among — A group sitting among other things is a run of letters sitting inside other words, unbroken and in the order given.

Prohibition-era Chicago and the gangs that ran it.

The capital C makes Chicago read as scene-setting rather than as raw material, and the run steps over the space between the two words. Agog is being wide-eyed with anticipation.

11 across PASSENGERS

Those on board given four directions in bleeping devices (10)

Definition: Those on board · container

'Those on board' names the people being carried rather than the crew carrying them, which is exactly the distinction the answer draws.

  • bleeping devices → PAGERS a pager is the pocket bleeper that tells a doctor to ring in
  • four directions → SSEN south, south, east and north, each written as its compass initial
  • in — The smallest word in the clue is the instruction: what stands before it is placed inside what follows it.

Directions being handed out among a heap of bleeping gadgets.

The clue says four directions, not four different ones, so south gets used twice — a fair count that still feels like a cheat the first time you meet it. 'Given' is doing no work but joining the halves.

12 across ALTERS

Modifies flat-topped places in church, we hear (6)

Definition: Modifies · homophone

'Modifies' is the third-person verb for making changes to something, matching the answer in person and number.

  • flat-topped places in church → ALTERS the flat-topped table at the east end of a church is an altar; say the plural aloud and the vowel gives you no clue how to spell it
  • we hear — 'We hear' says the wordplay describes a sound, so what the clue names is spelled one way and written down another.

Word going round that the flat-topped tables in a church are being changed.

Only one letter separates the two spellings, which is what makes this homophone nearly invisible — the middle vowel is the whole of the difference. 'In church' belongs to the sounded word, not to the definition.

13 across LANDFILL

Possibly find all behind lake making disposal of waste material (8)

Definition: disposal of waste material · anagram + charade

The answer is the practice of getting rid of rubbish by burying it in the ground, which is what 'disposal of waste material' describes.

  • lake → L the map abbreviation, as in L. Superior
  • find all → ANDFILL the seven letters of these two words in a new order
  • Possibly — 'Possibly' says the two words after it are only one possible arrangement of their letters, so another arrangement is wanted.
  • behind — What is behind something comes after it, so the reordered letters follow the single letter named next.

A search party told to look for everything downstream of a lake.

'Possibly' is the entire instruction, and it reads as ordinary hedging in the sentence rather than as an order to rearrange. The definition names a process, not a place: burying rubbish, rather than the tip it goes to.

14 across DRAMATIST

A dim start sadly for one creating scenes? (9)

Definition: one creating scenes · anagram

'One creating scenes' names the person who writes the scenes of a play, with the question mark acknowledging that it also describes someone making a fuss.

  • A dim start → DRAMATIST the nine letters of these three words in a new order
  • sadly — 'Sadly' reads as regret in the sentence, but an anagram indicator only has to say something is not as it should be, and letters in a sorry state need putting right.

A disappointing opening to somebody's venture.

'Creating scenes' is the theatre sense, not the tantrum sense, though the trade covers both. The fodder reads as a verdict on the clue itself, which is precisely what hides it.

16 across EVICT

Expel six occupying English court (5)

Definition: Expel · container + charade

To expel is to force someone out, and the answer is the legal species of that: removing an occupier from a property by court order.

  • English → E the standing abbreviation, as on a school timetable
  • six → VI six in Roman numerals
  • court → CT the short form used in street names and case citations
  • occupying — To occupy a space is to sit inside it, so the number takes up residence between the letters on either side.

Six people being thrown out of an English courtroom.

'Occupying' is the joke: the surface has people occupying a court and the wordplay has letters occupying letters. Roman numerals are worth owning as a set, because VI hides inside ordinary words better than any of the others.

17 across ASIDE

Assistant concealing second private comment (5)

Definition: private comment · container

The answer is the stage word for a remark meant to reach only some of those present, so 'private comment' is the theatre's own sense.

  • Assistant → AIDE an aide is an assistant to someone senior
  • second → S the standing abbreviation for the unit of time
  • concealing — Concealing something means keeping it out of sight inside, so the letter named next vanishes into the word before it.

An assistant keeping quiet about somebody's second confidential remark.

In the theatre this is the line an actor delivers to the audience while the other characters pretend not to hear, which is what makes 'private comment' an exact definition rather than a loose one.

19 across PRECISION

Copiers in work showing accuracy (9)

Definition: accuracy · anagram

'Accuracy' is the abstract noun for exactness, and the answer is its near synonym: exactness carried to a fine degree.

  • Copiers in → PRECISION the nine letters of these two words in a new order
  • work — To work a material is to knead and press it into a different shape, which is what the letters in front of it undergo.

Office photocopiers doing their job faithfully.

'In' looks like a preposition parking the copiers somewhere and is really two of the nine letters, which is why the fodder is so hard to see. 'Work' is the instruction, disguised as the place they stand.

23 across CAUTIOUS

A university in Connecticut facing financial debts making one wary (8)

Definition: wary · container + charade

'Wary' is the adjective for being on guard against risk, and the answer is its close synonym: careful because something might go wrong.

  • Connecticut → CT the two-letter postal code for the state
  • A → A the article stands for itself
  • university → U the standing abbreviation, as in a university's initials
  • financial debts → IOUS an IOU is a signed note admitting a debt, and several of them are IOUs
  • in — 'In' places what stands before it inside what follows, so two letters are dropped into the middle of the state's short form.
  • facing — To face something is to stand in front of it, so everything built so far is set before the letters named next.

An American student at a Connecticut university staring down a pile of debt.

The state's postal code splits open to take two letters, so 'in' is doing real work rather than describing where the university sits. IOU is 'I owe you' written as three letters, and it pluralises like a word.

24 across ANSWER

Doctor warns, describing beginning of emergency response (6)

Definition: response · anagram + container

'Response' is what you give when you are asked or addressed, and the answer is the everyday noun for exactly that.

  • warns → ANSWR the five letters of this word in a new order
  • beginning of emergency → E the opening letter of that word, and nothing more of it
  • Doctor — To doctor something is to tamper with it, so the word standing next to it comes out with its letters rearranged.
  • describing — In its geometric sense, to describe a circle round something is to draw it around, so the rearranged letters go outside what follows.
  • beginning of — 'Beginning of' asks for the opening letter of the word it points at and none of the rest.

A doctor issuing warnings about how an emergency should be handled.

'Doctor' is the instruction rather than the person, and the medical surface works hard to keep you from reading it that way. The single letter lands second from last, not at either end.

26 across CLOTH-EARED

A DC here upset about group that’s inattentive (5-5)

Definition: inattentive · anagram + container

'Inattentive' is the polite version of a British insult meaning unable or unwilling to hear, so it defines somebody who is simply not listening.

  • A DC here → CHEARED the seven letters of these three words in a new order
  • group → LOT a lot of people is a group of them
  • upset — Something upset has been knocked over and its contents scattered, so the letters before it no longer hold the order they were given.
  • about — Placed about something means wrapped round it, so the scattered letters close over the word that follows.

Somebody in Washington thoroughly annoyed about a particular crowd.

DC is the district Washington stands in, which is what makes the first three words read as a place rather than as seven letters. The insult is British and blunt: it says a person might as well have cloth over their ears.

27 across ARID

Help required around river getting dry (4)

Definition: dry · container

'Dry' in the rainfall sense — too little water for much to grow — is the sense the answer carries, not the opposite of wet to the touch.

  • Help required → AID help sent where it is needed is aid, and 'required' only holds the surface sentence upright
  • river → R the standing map abbreviation, as in R. Thames
  • around — Going around something means enclosing it, so the word before it opens up and the letter after it drops inside.

Relief supplies being brought in along a river.

'Getting dry' sounds like a towel after a swim and is the climate sense, the one that describes a desert. Aid is the disaster-relief word the surface leans on to make its picture.

28 across SPIDERY

Look about unusually dire characteristic of some writing? (7)

Definition: characteristic of some writing · anagram + container

The answer describes handwriting made of thin, sprawling strokes, so the definition points at how a script looks rather than at the creature it borrows its name from.

  • Look → SPY to spy something is to catch sight of it
  • dire → IDER the four letters of this word in a new order
  • about — Placed about something means wrapped round it, so the short word for looking opens to take what follows.
  • unusually — 'Unusually' says the word beside it is not in its usual order, which is all an instruction to reorder needs to say.

Taking a look at something unusually awful.

'Look about' reads as glancing round the room and is really two separate instructions inside three letters. Handwriting earns this description when the strokes are thin and straggling, like legs.

29 across CANTEEN

Drinks holder taken by adolescent in place to eat at work (7)

Definition: place to eat at work · charade

'Place to eat at work' is the works or barracks dining room — the institutional sense, not a restaurant anyone can walk into.

  • Drinks holder → CAN a can is what a drink comes in
  • adolescent → TEEN a teen is an adolescent

A teenager helping himself to somebody else's can of drink.

'Taken by' reads as theft and is only joinery holding the two pieces together. The same word names a soldier's water flask and a boxed set of cutlery, so the drinks holder in the surface is closer to the answer than it looks.

2 down REGULAR

Medium-sized person often at local? (7)

Definition: Medium-sized · double definition

'Medium-sized' is the everyday sizing sense of the word; 'person often at local' is the pub sense — one word, two ordinary meanings.

  • Medium-sized → the sizing sense — neither small nor large, the middle option on a menu board
  • person often at local → the noun: a pub's habitual customer, the one whose drink the bar staff already know

A man of average build who is in the neighbourhood pub most evenings.

Both halves define and neither hands over a letter. A 'local' in Britain is the pub nearest your home, and the people who drink there night after night are known by this very word.

3 down VAGUE

Virginia given estimate after leaving ship that’s unclear (5)

Definition: unclear · charade + deletion

'Unclear' is the adjective for something whose outline or meaning cannot be made out, and the answer is its everyday synonym.

  • Virginia → VA the two-letter postal code for the American state
  • estimate → GUESS an estimate is a guess
  • ship → SS SS stands for steamship and prefixes a vessel's name; these two letters are the ones that depart
  • after leaving — What is there after something leaves is the remainder, so the letters named last walk out of the word they were sitting in.

A rough valuation offered once the boat has sailed.

The deletion happens inside the word for estimate rather than at either end of it: the two ship letters sit together at the back and simply go. Three letters of the five come from a word about guessing.

4 down DEPOSIT

Drop thing needed by a UK election candidate (7)

Definition: Drop · double definition

'Drop' is the plain verb for setting something down; the candidate's is the noun for a stake paid over and held — both live senses of one word.

  • Drop → the verb: to set something down, or let it fall
  • thing needed by a UK election candidate → the noun: the sum a would-be MP hands over with the nomination papers

Something a hopeful parliamentary candidate must not lose on polling day.

Anyone standing for the UK Parliament must put down £500 with their nomination papers and gets it back only by taking 5% of the vote, which is why 'losing your deposit' is the standard humiliation on election night.

6 down AGEING

Getting on a horse captivates a German (6)

Definition: Getting on · charade + container

'Getting on' is the idiom for growing old — 'he's getting on a bit' — and it matches the answer as a present participle.

  • a → A the article stands for itself
  • horse → GG a gee-gee is the nursery and racecourse word for a horse, written as its two initials
  • a German → EIN 'a' in German is ein, and it goes between the pair of Gs
  • captivates — To captivate is to hold somebody spellbound, and holding is the job here: what comes next is held inside what came before.

Mounting a horse so gracefully that a German onlooker is enchanted.

'Getting on' wants to mean climbing into the saddle, which is the reading the horse plants; it is the British idiom for growing old. GG comes from 'gee-gee', the word children use for a horse.

7 down HIGH FIVES

Towering over court-based sport making triumphal gestures (4,5)

Definition: triumphal gestures · charade

'Triumphal gestures' names the raised-palm slap two people trade after a win, in the plural the answer needs.

  • Towering → HIGH a towering building is a high one
  • court-based sport → FIVES fives is a handball game struck against the walls of a court, still played at English public schools
  • over — In a down entry 'over' is a position rather than a preposition: the piece before it sits above the piece after it.

A tall figure looming above a game played in a walled court.

Fives is named for the five fingers you hit the ball with, so the sport and the celebration are both about a raised hand — the surface picture and the answer share a joke the letters never mention.

8 down ACRYLIC

Article Cyril tailored with cold, artificial material (7)

Definition: artificial material · charade + anagram

The answer names the synthetic fibre and resin made from acrylic acid, so 'artificial material' defines it by class: man-made rather than grown.

  • Article → A the grammatical article, which is itself a single letter
  • Cyril → CRYLI the five letters of the name, re-sewn into a new order
  • cold → C the standing abbreviation, as on a tap or a weather map
  • tailored — Tailoring cuts cloth apart and sews it back in a different shape, which is what happens to the name standing beside it.

A newspaper piece about a man named Cyril, cut to fit the page.

'Article' is the grammatical kind, not the newspaper kind the surface is selling, and it gives up one letter. The material is what jumpers and craft paints are made of.

9 down AS A LAST RESORT

Type in support of manipulating rates alas, when all else has failed (2,1,4,6)

Definition: when all else has failed · anagram + charade

'When all else has failed' is the situation the phrase names: the option you fall back on once every better one is gone.

  • rates alas → ASALASTRE the nine letters of these two words in a new order
  • Type → SORT a type of thing is a sort of thing
  • manipulating — Manipulating something is handling it until it takes the shape you want, and the two words behind it are handled letter by letter.
  • in support of — In a down entry a supporting piece props up what stands on it, so it goes underneath — at the end of the answer.

A trader lamenting the fixing of interest rates.

'Alas' sounds like a sigh at the rate-fixing and is really four letters of raw material. The shuffle stops in the middle of the final word, so the gaps you hear in the phrase are not the gaps the wordplay uses.

15 down MEDITATED

Contemplated model appearing in TV etc. with on-screen priest (9)

Definition: Contemplated · container + charade

'Contemplated' is the past tense of turning something over in the mind at length, and the answer is that same past tense.

  • TV etc. → MEDIA television, radio and the press together are the media
  • model → T Ford's Model T, the standing crossword sense of the word model
  • on-screen priest → TED Father Ted was the priest of the Channel 4 sitcom of the same name
  • appearing in — Appearing in something means showing up inside it, so the letter named before it is planted within the word that follows.

A fashion model turning up in the TV listings alongside a television priest.

'Model' is not a person here but Ford's Model T, an abbreviation setters lean on constantly. Father Ted, a 1990s comedy about priests marooned on a fictional Irish island, supplies the last three letters.

18 down SCALLOP

Concession about name for shellfish (7)

Definition: shellfish · container

The answer is the fan-shelled bivalve on a fishmonger's slab, so 'shellfish' defines it by naming the family it belongs to.

  • Concession → SOP a sop is a small concession thrown to somebody to keep them quiet
  • name → CALL to call something is to name it
  • about — Something placed about another thing is wrapped round it, so the first word's letters open up to admit the second's.

A minor concession made about someone's reputation.

'About' reads first as 'concerning', which is the whole disguise, and it is the instruction to wrap. A sop was originally bread soaked in liquid, thrown to Cerberus to get safely past him.

20 down CLAUDIA

Praise among US agents for woman (7)

Definition: woman · container

'Woman' defines by example: the answer is a woman's given name, so the definition names the class and the wordplay picks the member.

  • US agents → CIA the Central Intelligence Agency, America's foreign intelligence service
  • Praise → LAUD to laud someone is to praise them
  • among — To be among a group is to stand in the middle of it, so the praise slots between the letters of the agency.

Compliments circulating inside American intelligence.

'Woman' almost always signals a first name, and the surface gives no hint which one, so the letters have to settle it alone. LAUD survives mostly in 'laudable' and in hymns.

21 down ONE-TIME

Former individual with money put in competitive fixture (3-4)

Definition: Former · charade + container

'Former' is the adjective for something that held a role earlier and holds it no longer, which is exactly what the hyphenated answer means.

  • individual → ONE a single individual is one
  • competitive fixture → TIE a tie is a match in a knockout competition, as in a cup tie
  • money → M the standing abbreviation, as in a salary quoted at £1m
  • put in — 'Put in' is a plain instruction to insert: the letter it follows goes inside the word that comes after.

A solitary man who put his money on a big match.

'Former' looks like it wants a noun — a former of things — and it is the adjective, as in a one-time champion. A cup tie is a British knockout football fixture, drawn out of a hat rather than scheduled.

22 down POSEUR

Petty Officer rues agitating pretentious figure (6)

Definition: pretentious figure · charade + anagram

'Pretentious figure' describes someone who strikes attitudes to impress, which is the affectation the answer names rather than any single pose.

  • Petty Officer → PO the naval rank's standard initials
  • rues → SEUR the four letters of this word in a new order
  • agitating — Agitating a mixture stirs it until nothing is where it started, so the word beside it comes out reordered.

A naval officer grumbling and getting on everyone's nerves.

'Agitating' reads as what the officer is doing to the people around him, which is the surface's whole trick. Petty officer is the Royal Navy rank between able seaman and chief petty officer.

25 down SCANT

Barely sufficient stuff kept in Tuscan trattoria (5)

Definition: Barely sufficient · hidden word

'Barely sufficient' is the adjectival sense: an amount that is only just enough, the way the word is used of supplies or of regard.

  • Tuscan trattoria → SCANT five letters in a row, starting part way through the first word and finishing at the head of the second
  • stuff kept in — Stuff kept in something is stored out of sight inside it, and what is stored here is a run of letters rather than provisions.

Ingredients kept in the store cupboard of a Tuscan restaurant.

Two Italian-sounding words in a row make the letters look like scenery, and the run steps across the gap between them. 'Stuff kept in' is the only instruction in the whole clue.
