Across

1 across LAMBDA

Ultimately penned by innocent child, a letter on papyrus, perhaps? (6)

Definition: letter on papyrus, perhaps? · charade + last letter

LAMBDA is the eleventh letter of the Greek alphabet, and 'on papyrus' flags a character written in antiquity rather than a note in the post.

  • innocent child → LAMB a lamb is the byword for an innocent young thing, and 'child' asks for the young one rather than the grown sheep
  • Ultimately penned → D the closing letter of 'penned'
  • a → A used literally, lifted straight out of the surface
  • Ultimately — 'Ultimately' means at the very end, so only the closing letter of the word beside it is taken.

A letter written on papyrus, apparently by an innocent child, and signed off at the end.

Papyrus points at the ancient world, so 'letter' is a character from an alphabet and not something posted — the sheet the surface hands you is the wrong sort of writing altogether.

5 across CELIBACY

A parting bicycle travelling, getting away from relations (8)

Definition: getting away from relations · anagram

Celibacy is abstention from sexual relations, so 'relations' takes its other sense and the phrase names the state as a noun would.

  • A → A one letter taken literally from the farewell in the picture
  • bicycle → BICYCLE seven letters of raw material, handed over whole
  • parting — A parting separates things, and this one splits the shuffled letters so the single letter can drop into the gap.
  • travelling — Letters that are travelling do not stay at the address they were given, so they arrive in a different order.

A goodbye at the roadside as somebody pedals off into the distance.

'A parting' reads as the farewell, which is exactly why the A slips past unnoticed as a letter. 'Relations' means family on the surface and something quite different underneath.

9 across MISTAKEN

Ill-conceived, a plastic boyfriend on film (8)

Definition: Ill-conceived · charade

Something mistaken is wrongly judged or wrongly conceived, so 'ill-conceived' is the same idea in adjective form and swaps in without a wobble.

  • film → MIST a film is a thin layer, and a thin layer of water in the air is mist
  • a → A used literally
  • plastic boyfriend → KEN Barbie's boyfriend is a doll, so 'plastic' names what he is made of rather than insulting him
  • on — In an across row a piece sitting 'on' another is written after it, the way words lean rightwards along the line.

A film with a hopelessly artificial boyfriend in it, and a badly judged one at that.

'Plastic' does two jobs on the surface, insincere and literally moulded, and only the literal one leads anywhere. Ken has been Barbie's boyfriend since 1961.

10 across VERTEX

Partner once put on green top (6)

Definition: top · charade

A vertex is the highest point of a figure or a hill, so 'top' is the everyday noun for the same place.

  • green → VERT vert is the heraldic name for green, the colour as a herald would blazon it
  • Partner once → EX a former partner is an ex
  • put on — Put one piece on another along an across row and it goes after it, pushed along to the right.

An old flame turning up dressed in green.

Heraldry supplies 'vert' for green, one of a small set of blazon words — gules for red, sable for black — that setters reach for whenever a colour is needed.

11 across WEDDING DRESS

Train carriage required with this? (7,5)

Definition: Train carriage required with this? · cryptic definition

A wedding dress is the garment that comes with a train, the trailing fabric a bride carries behind her, so the question describes it exactly.

  • Train carriage → on the surface these are two railway words in a row, one of them the thing the other is made of
  • required with this? → read the other way, the sentence is asking what needs a train, and 'carriage' becomes the bearing of the person carrying one

A railway question: which sort of carriage does a train need?

There is nothing to take apart here — the whole sentence is the definition, with 'train' switching from rails to the length of fabric that trails behind a bride down the aisle.

14 across RAMSGATE

Megastar at sea port (8)

Definition: port · anagram

Ramsgate is a working harbour town on the Kent coast, so 'port' defines it by category and the anagram picks out which port is meant.

  • Megastar → MEGASTAR eight letters of raw material — the celebrity is there for the picture only
  • at sea — Being at sea is being lost with no bearings, and letters with no bearings do not stay in the order they arrived in.

A celebrity adrift somewhere off the coast.

Ramsgate is a Channel port in Kent, on the corner of England nearest the continent, and the kind of British place name a daily puzzle assumes you have ready.

17 across REARWARD

Bounty boarded by a mutineer finally, astern (8)

Definition: astern · container + last letter

Astern means towards the back of a ship and rearward means towards the rear of anything, so the definition is the nautical case of the answer's general sense.

  • Bounty → REWARD a bounty is a reward posted for something, and the capital letter belongs to the ship in the picture
  • a → A used literally
  • mutineer finally → R the last letter of 'mutineer'
  • boarded by — Boarders come over the side and end up within the ship, so the pieces that board go inside the word they board.
  • finally — 'Finally' points at the very last letter of the word in front of it and nothing else.

The Bounty being stormed, with the last of the mutineers climbing aboard at the back.

The mutiny is scenery: 'Bounty' is money here, the reward posted for information, and a clue's first word is capitalised anyway, so the ship costs the setter nothing.

18 across NINE

Baseball team certainly not heard of in Europe? (4)

Definition: Baseball team · homophone

A baseball team fields nine players, so the number is its standing nickname, the way a cricket team is an eleven.

  • certainly not → NINE the German for a flat refusal is nein, and said aloud that is an English number
  • heard of in Europe? — 'Heard' asks for a sound rather than a spelling, and 'in Europe' says which language to say it in — the German for no.

Baseball sides being something nobody in Europe has ever heard of.

German nein is a plain no, and 'certainly not' is that no with feeling — the small stretch the question mark is there to apologise for.

20 across ROSETTA STONE

Villa’s place secured in prize museum piece (7,5)

Definition: museum piece · container

The Rosetta Stone is the inscribed slab that unlocked hieroglyphs, and 'museum piece' names it by where it has sat since 1802, the British Museum.

  • prize → ROSETTE a rosette is the ribbon badge pinned on a winner at a show
  • Villa’s place → ASTON Aston Villa are a Birmingham football club, named after Aston, the district of the city they come from
  • secured in — Something secured in a place is fastened inside it, so those letters are locked within the word that follows.

A villa's site being fastened into some prize-winning exhibit.

Villa is the football club, so 'place' is the Birmingham district it takes its name from rather than a house in Tuscany, which is where the surface would rather you looked.

23 across AVATAR

Vishnu, leader lost in war following attacks, reportedly? (6)

Definition: Vishnu · homophone + deletion + charade

An avatar is a god's earthly incarnation, and Vishnu's ten are the famous set, so naming the god points at the form he comes down in.

  • attacks, reportedly → AVAT said out loud, 'attacks' is 'a tax' — and the tax every British shopper pays on their purchases is VAT, value added tax
  • leader lost in war → AR 'war' with its opening letter gone
  • reportedly — 'Reportedly' means as reported aloud, so what counts is the sound of the words and not the way they are spelt.
  • leader lost — The leader of a word is its opening letter, and losing it leaves the rest of the word to stand alone.
  • following — What follows comes after, so that piece is written to the right of the one it follows.

A god's commander reported missing in a war that followed a spate of raids.

The sound comes first and the spelling second: what you hear is a tax, and Britain's name for the tax on purchases supplies the letters you write down.

24 across ALCATRAZ

Prison, where racketeer has time inside (8)

Definition: Prison · container

Alcatraz is the island jail in San Francisco Bay, so 'Prison' defines by category and the most famous instance stands in for the class.

  • racketeer → ALCARAZ a racketeer here is somebody who wields a racket: Carlos Alcaraz, the Spanish tennis champion
  • time → T the standard one-letter shorthand for time
  • inside — Doing time inside is doing it in jail, and here the word is meant literally as well: that letter goes within the other piece.

The prison where a gangster is currently serving his sentence.

'Racketeer' is the joke, and it is not the gangster the surface promises but a man holding a tennis racket, whose surname is a single letter away from the island.

25 across USERNAME

Sequence logged by gentleman, resubmitted when typed the wrong way? (8)

Definition: Sequence · hidden word + reversal

A username is the fixed string of characters you register and type in each time, so 'sequence' names it by its form rather than its job.

  • gentleman, resubmitted → USERNAME the letters run consecutively across the join between these two words, travelling the other way
  • logged by — To log something is to write it down inside a record, so these are the words the letters are recorded in rather than a description of them.
  • typed the wrong way — Typed the wrong way round, a run of letters comes out back to front, which is how it is sitting in the clue.

A gentleman logging in, getting his details wrong and having to type them all over again.

The computing picture is doing all the disguising: 'logged', 'typed' and 'sequence' belong to the same world as the answer, so none of them looks like the instruction it is.

26 across ALMOND

A loch, out of which pulled the first old nut (6)

Definition: nut · deletion

An almond is the nut in marzipan and macaroons, so 'nut' is the plain category word and the wordplay settles which kind.

  • A loch → ALOMOND the A of the surface, then Lomond — Loch Lomond is the great loch north of Glasgow, and the name comes over whole
  • old → O o is the standard shorthand for old, and it names the letter being pulled out
  • out of which pulled — Pulling a letter out of a word is a deletion: it leaves, and the letters on either side close up behind it.
  • the first — There are two of that letter to choose from, and 'the first' settles which one goes — the earlier of the pair.

An old nut fished out of a Scottish loch.

Loch Lomond is the loch everybody reaches for first and the right one for once, so the work is in spotting that 'old' is a letter rather than part of the picture.

2 down ACID TEST

Critical evaluation, turning of tides indeed? (4,4)

Definition: Critical evaluation · container + anagram

An acid test is the trial a thing has to survive, from assaying gold with acid, so it stands for any critical evaluation.

  • indeed → ACT the DEED at the back of this word is an act, while the two letters in front of it are the other job the word is doing
  • tides → TIDES five letters of raw material, due to be re-ordered before they go anywhere
  • turning — Tides turn, and a set of letters told to turn does not come back in the order it left.
  • indeed — The IN at the front of this word is the instruction — one piece goes inside the other — and the DEED behind it is material.

Someone weighing up a claim and agreeing that the tide really has turned.

'Indeed' is two words pushed together, the IN that sends one piece inside and the DEED that supplies the outer letters, so a single word carries half the wordplay.

3 down BATHWATER

Someone loathing vampire, point driven through heart – might this cover the body? (9)

Definition: might this cover the body? · container

Run a bath and the water covers whoever gets in, so the definition works by describing the effect instead of offering a synonym.

  • Someone loathing vampire → BATHATER a vampire takes bat form, so someone loathing one is a bat hater — two words running together into eight letters
  • point → W west is a point of the compass, and its initial is the usual shorthand
  • driven through heart — A stake driven through the heart goes into the middle, so the letter it carries lands at the centre of the other piece.

Someone who hates vampires driving a stake clean through the creature's heart.

'Point' reads as the sharpened end of the stake and is a compass direction. The question mark is the setter admitting his definition describes what the stuff does rather than naming it.

4 down AIKIDO

Duck under perfect child in martial art (6)

Definition: martial art · charade

Aikido is one of the Japanese martial arts, so the definition names the category and leaves the letters to pick which member is meant.

  • perfect → AI A1 means top quality, and the 1 is written as a capital I once it joins a word
  • child → KID a kid is a child
  • Duck → O a duck is a score of nothing in cricket, and nothing is written as O
  • under — In a down entry 'under' is literal geography: the piece it governs is written at the foot of the column, below everything else.

Somebody ducking underneath a faultless youngster at a martial arts class.

'Duck' looks like the move the class would teach you and is the cricket score of nothing, one of the handful of sporting words a British setter treats as common property.

5 down CENTIGRADE SCALE

On which it’s cold and bitter after temperature in liquid decreasing? (10,5)

Definition: On which it’s cold and bitter after temperature in liquid decreasing? · &lit + anagram

The whole clue reads straight: this is the scale on which cold and bitter is where you end up as a liquid's temperature falls.

  • cold → C the weather forecaster's one-letter shorthand
  • bitter → ALE bitter is a kind of ale in any British pub
  • temperature → T the standard one-letter shorthand
  • decreasing → DECREASING ten letters of raw material, the bulk of the mixture
  • liquid — Something liquid holds no shape of its own, so the letters it describes settle into any order they like.

A pint going cold and bitter as the temperature of the liquid keeps dropping.

Read straight the sentence describes the thing itself, and read again the very same words hand over their letters, which is what &lit means. 'Bitter' is the pub sense, ale rather than a taste.

6 down LOVE GAME

Feeling ready for tennis achievement (4,4)

Definition: tennis achievement · charade

A love game is one won without the loser taking a single point, love being tennis's word for zero, which makes it a genuine achievement.

  • Feeling → LOVE the feeling, taken as the plain noun
  • ready → GAME somebody game for anything is ready for it

Being in the mood for a knock-up on the tennis court.

In tennis 'love' is nothing at all, the score you have to hold an opponent to for six straight points, so the warm feeling of the surface is the wrong sense twice over.

7 down BORER

Drill, tedious thing? (5)

Definition: Drill · double definition

A borer is the tool that drills a hole, and anything that bores you is a borer as well — one sense mechanical, one social.

  • Drill → the tool that turns and cuts its way into a wall
  • tedious thing? → anything that sends you to sleep, the agent noun made from the verb

Somebody dismissing a drill — the repetitive kind — as a thoroughly dull way to spend a morning.

Both halves come off the same verb: to bore a hole and to bore a person. The question mark covers 'thing', since the word more often names a beetle or a bit than an occasion.

8 down CREOSOTING

Preserving wood, one going in strong, ecofreak? (10)

Definition: Preserving wood · anagram

Creosote is the tarry liquid painted onto fences and sleepers to stop them rotting, so creosoting is precisely the act of preserving wood.

  • one → I the numeral one written as a letter, the oldest trick in the book
  • strong → STRONG six letters of raw material, used exactly as spelt
  • ecofreak → ECO only the front half of this word is material, three more letters for the mixture; the back half is the instruction
  • going in — 'Going in' sends the single letter into the middle of the others rather than leaving it at either end.
  • ecofreak — The FREAK at the back of this word is the instruction — a freak is something formed wrongly, so the letters come out misshapen — while the ECO in front of it is material.

An eco-warrior wading in hard against somebody treating their timber.

'Ecofreak' is one word in the clue and two in the wordplay: the ECO is material and the FREAK is the instruction. Splitting a word down the middle like that is a Paul regular.

12 down WEREWOLVES

Monsters had been identified as footballers (10)

Definition: Monsters · charade

Werewolves are the men who turn into wolves at full moon, monsters in the plainest storybook sense of the word.

  • had been → WERE 'had been' is the plural past of 'to be'
  • footballers → WOLVES Wolverhampton Wanderers are Wolves to every English football fan, and a club's nickname stands in for its players

Monsters turning out, on investigation, to be a football team.

English club nicknames are standard crossword currency — Wolves for Wolverhampton Wanderers, Spurs for Tottenham — and here the plural does the joining work for free.

15 down GINGER TOM

Cat still gripping tail of dog, another cat upset about that (6,3)

Definition: Cat · container + reversal + last letter

A ginger tom is a marmalade-coloured male cat, so 'Cat' names the category that the two words together pin down to one animal.

  • another cat → MOG a mog is British slang for a cat — the second one in the clue, not the one being defined
  • still → INERT a thing that is still is inert, going nowhere
  • tail of dog → G the last letter of 'dog'
  • gripping — Something gripped is held inside the grip, so the letter taken lands within the word doing the holding.
  • tail of — The tail of a word is the letter it drags behind it, the last one of all.
  • upset — Going down the grid, an upset piece is tipped over so its letters run the other way up the column.
  • about — 'About' means round the outside, so that piece wraps around what it is about rather than sitting beside it.

One cat clinging to a dog's tail while a second cat takes loud offence at the sight.

'Still' is the adjective, not the copper apparatus that gives a setter GIN, which is the first thing most solvers try here. Two cats appear in the clue and neither of them is the one being defined.

16 down BACTERIA

Bugs Bunny’s first song about carrot extremely emotional at first (8)

Definition: Bugs · charade + container + first letters + outer letters

Bugs is the everyday word for the germs that make you ill, and bacteria are exactly those, plural matching plural.

  • Bunny’s first → B the opening letter of 'Bunny'
  • song → ARIA an aria is the song in an opera
  • carrot extremely → CT the two ends of 'carrot', with everything between them dropped
  • emotional at first → E the opening letter of 'emotional'
  • Bunny’s first — The first of a word is its opening letter, and the possessive says which word to take that letter from.
  • about — 'About' wraps one piece round another, so what follows is written inside the word before it.
  • extremely — Take a word extremely and you get its extremes, the letter at each end with the middle thrown away.
  • at first — 'At first' asks for the opening letter alone, the one the word starts with.

Bugs Bunny's debut number about a carrot, sung with enormous feeling to begin with.

'Bugs' is the definition, and the cartoon rabbit is built out of the very same word, which is why the eye runs straight past it into the sentence.

19 down GOTCHA

Caught in German town, you’ve been captured! (6)

Definition: you’ve been captured! · container

Gotcha is what you shout the moment you have got someone, so the definition quotes the captor's line instead of offering a synonym.

  • German town → GOTHA Gotha is a town in Thuringia, in the middle of Germany
  • Caught → C c for caught is how a cricket scorecard records a catch
  • in — 'In' is the plainest container instruction there is: what comes before it goes inside what comes after.

Somebody being seized in the middle of a German town.

Cricket's scorecard supplies the c for caught, and it goes into a town most solvers only ever meet in crosswords. The exclamation mark is the definition's tone of voice, not an instruction.

21 down ENTER

Key tenant initially lost? (5)

Definition: Key · deletion

Enter is the big key you hit to send whatever you have typed, so 'Key' names it by the thing the word is printed on.

  • tenant → RENTER somebody who rents a place from a landlord is its tenant
  • initially lost → the R at the front of those six letters comes away, which is the whole of the deletion
  • initially lost — What is lost initially is the letter at the start, so the word turns up one letter shorter at the front.

An important tenant who went missing early on.

'Key' is the one on a keyboard rather than the one in a lock, and it is the definition, sitting exactly where a solver expects an adjective describing the tenant.

22 down YARN

Embroidered anecdote? (4)

Definition: Embroidered anecdote? · cryptic definition

A yarn is a long story, and an embroidered one is a story stretched in the telling — while yarn is also the thread embroidery is worked in.

  • Embroidered anecdote? → read as the surface intends, this is a story someone has stretched considerably in the telling
  • Embroidered → read the other way it is meant literally, and points at the material embroidery is actually worked in

A tale somebody has quietly dressed up in the telling.

Two senses sit on top of each other: the exaggerated story, and the thread you would embroider with. Both are the same four letters, which is why the clue needs only three words.
