9 across RUMBA

Strange bachelor’s dance (5)

Definition: dance · charade

The rumba is a Cuban ballroom dance, so 'dance' names the category the answer belongs to.

  • Strange → RUM rum is an old adjective for odd or peculiar, as in 'a rum do'
  • bachelor → BA BA is the bachelor's degree, the crossword's standing bachelor

An odd unmarried man taking to the dance floor.

'Strange' looks like an anagram indicator and is a straight synonym instead, so nothing beside it is ever shuffled.

10 across CHEMISTRY

Subject in short dress getting turn (9)

Definition: Subject · charade + deletion

Chemistry is a school and university subject, one of the sciences on any timetable.

  • in short dress → CHEMIS a chemise is a loose dress or slip, and it is docked of its final letter
  • turn → TRY a turn at something is a go at it, a try
  • in short — say a thing in short and you say it in fewer letters, so the word beside it loses its tail

A student in a short dress, waiting for a turn.

'in short dress' reads as a description of what somebody is wearing; 'in short' is the instruction, and 'dress' is the word it cuts.

11 across PROCESSOR

Chip for scrambling scores (9)

Definition: Chip · charade + anagram

The processor is the chip a computer thinks with, so 'Chip' is the everyday name for the component itself.

  • for → PRO to be pro something is to be for it
  • scrambling scores → CESSOR the six letters of the last word, reshuffled
  • scrambling — scrambled eggs are beaten out of shape, so the word beside it comes out in another order

A gadget sold as a chip that jumbles up whatever scores you feed it.

'for' looks like a link word holding two halves together and is the first piece of the wordplay, which is why 'Chip' on its own has to be the definition.

12 across IRATE

Angry judge’s claim? (5)

Definition: Angry · charade

Irate is angry at the settled, indignant end of the range — the fury of somebody writing a letter of complaint.

  • claim → I a claim is made in the first person, so it opens with the speaker's own letter
  • judge → RATE to judge a performance is to rate it

A furious magistrate asserting something from the bench.

The question mark flags the joke: what a judge might claim is 'I rate', which is IRATE said as a sentence instead of spelled as a word.

13 across ORINOCO

Gold icon roughly treated by old recycling team member (7)

Definition: recycling team member · charade + anagram

Orinoco is one of the Wombles, the burrowing creatures of British children's television who collect litter — a recycling team member before the phrase existed.

  • Gold → OR OR is gold in heraldry, the standard crossword shorthand
  • icon roughly treated → INOC the four letters of the image, knocked into a different order
  • old → O O is the standard abbreviation for old
  • roughly treated — handle something roughly and it comes back out of shape, so those letters will not keep the order they were given in

An old gold icon that has been handled badly by somebody on the recycling crew.

'Gold icon' looks like one treasure and is two separate pieces, the first of them heraldry's gold; the same name belongs to a South American river.

15 across SQUEALS

Queen captured by US navy forces wails (7)

Definition: wails · container

To squeal is to let out a high thin cry, matching 'wails' as a third-person verb, with the informer's sense waiting behind the surface.

  • US navy forces → SEALS the US Navy's SEALs are its special forces, the sea-air-land teams
  • Queen → QU QU is the standard shorthand for queen
  • captured by — a captive is held inside by its captor, so the piece named first ends up within the one named after

A monarch taken prisoner by American special forces, and the noise that follows.

'forces wails' reads as one action, the soldiers making her cry out; the definition is the last word on its own, a verb in the third person.

17 across ASDIC

Early sub detector regularly seen near us in Dominica (5)

Definition: Early sub detector · regular letters

Asdic was the Royal Navy's original submarine-detection gear, named after the committee that developed it and later renamed sonar.

  • near us in Dominica → ASDIC count in to the third letter of the phrase, then take every third one from there to the end
  • regularly seen — seen at regular intervals means picked out at a fixed step, and the step here is every third letter rather than every other one

A wartime listening device apparently spotted at regular intervals in the Caribbean.

Most solvers try alternate letters first, because that is what 'regularly' usually asks for; here the step is three, and the count has to start inside the first word.

18 across MAD

State of mind about the boy, from Ella Fitzgerald ultimately (3)

Definition: State of mind · last letters

Mad covers both states of mind the clue plays with: out of one's wits, and helplessly infatuated with somebody.

  • from Ella Fitzgerald → MAD the last letter of each of those three words, taken in the order they stand
  • about the boy → pure surface: it completes the song title the singer put on an album, and hands over no letters
  • ultimately — ultimately means at the very end, so each word beside it is worth nothing but its final letter

Ella Fitzgerald's feelings about a boy, borrowed from the title of one of her records.

'from' looks like a link word and is the first piece of the fodder instead. Ella Fitzgerald released an album called Mad About the Boy, which is the picture the surface is selling.

20 across NORMA

Shearer’s fancy manor (5)

Definition: Shearer · anagram

Norma Shearer was one of MGM's biggest stars of the 1930s, so the surname points to the first name that belongs with it.

  • manor → MANOR five letters of fodder, taken straight off the page
  • fancy — fancy is the adjective for something dressed up out of its plain form, so the word beside it appears in another shape

A footballer's grand country house.

Shearer reads as Alan the striker and is Norma Shearer, so a surname is defining a first name — the sort of definition that only works one person at a time.

22 across EFFUSED

Gushed and swore about American (7)

Definition: Gushed · container

To effuse is to pour out, words as readily as liquid, so somebody who gushed with praise effused.

  • swore → EFFED to eff is to use the f-word, so somebody who swore effed
  • American → US US is the standard abbreviation for American
  • about — about means round the outside of a thing, so the word before it wraps around the letters named after

Somebody gushing and swearing on the subject of an American.

'Gushed and swore' looks like one description of a person losing their temper, and the 'and' is the seam: only the first word is defining anything.

25 across EVOLVED

First woman abruptly cracked without starting to be transformed (7)

Definition: transformed · charade + deletion

Something that has evolved has changed into a new form over time, so it answers 'transformed' as a past participle.

  • First woman abruptly → EV Eve is the first woman of Genesis, cut off before her last letter
  • cracked without starting → OLVED a code that has been cracked has been solved, and the S never gets going
  • abruptly — an abrupt ending is one that comes too soon, so the name beside it stops a letter early
  • without starting — without its start means the opening letter never appears and the word begins one letter in

The first woman cracking up all of a sudden, and the change that came over her.

'First woman' is Eve and 'cracked' is the puzzle-solver's sense, as a case or a code is cracked, while the surface wants both of them in their breaking-down senses.

26 across BRAVO

Tips of bacon rashers are very overcooked or well done (5)

Definition: well done · first letters

Bravo is the cry of approval called out at the end of a performance, which is exactly what 'well done' says.

  • bacon rashers are very overcooked → BRAVO the first letter of each of those five words, in the order they come
  • Tips of — the tip of a thing is its very point, so only the leading letter of each word named after it is taken

Breakfast burnt black at the edges, and somebody sounding pleased about it.

'overcooked or well done' reads as one thought about the bacon, and 'well done' has quietly changed jobs: it is the shout of approval at the end of a performance.

27 across BEGINNING

Start pleading to swap pub for one grand (9)

Definition: Start · substitution

A beginning is the point where a thing starts, so it answers 'Start' in that word's noun sense rather than its verb sense.

  • pleading → BEGGING to plead with someone is to beg them
  • pub → INN an inn is a pub, three letters moving in
  • one grand → G G stands for grand, and just one of the pair of them makes way
  • swap — a swap puts one thing where the other was, so the new letters take up exactly the space the old one leaves

A landlord pleading to trade his pub for a thousand pounds.

'one grand' is money on the surface and an instruction underneath: a single G, not both of them, gives up its place to the pub.

30 across RICHELIEU

Opulent European place for French cardinal (9)

Definition: French cardinal · charade

Cardinal Richelieu was Louis XIII's chief minister and a prince of the French church, so the definition names his office and his country at once.

  • Opulent → RICH opulent surroundings are rich ones
  • European → E E is the standard abbreviation for European
  • place → LIEU lieu is the French for place, the word English kept in 'in lieu of'

A lavish spot somewhere in Europe, set aside for a churchman from France.

'French' is working twice over: it flags the language that turns 'place' into four letters, and it belongs to the definition of the cardinal as well.

31 across KYLIE

Lucky Liechtensteiner keeps throwing stick (5)

Definition: throwing stick · hidden word

Kylie is the Western Australian word for a boomerang-shaped throwing stick, and that is where the given name comes from.

  • Lucky Liechtensteiner → KYLIE five letters running from the end of the first word into the start of the second
  • keeps — what somebody keeps they hold on to, so these letters are being held inside the words as they stand

A fortunate citizen of Liechtenstein hanging on to something.

A kylie is an Australian Aboriginal throwing stick, a flat curved boomerang that is not meant to come back, and the name reached Britain as a girl's name.

1 down DROP

Release tiny amount (4)

Definition: Release · double definition

A drop is the smallest measure of liquid, the bead that falls from a pipette, and to drop something is to release it.

  • Release → DROP what a label does with a new record, or a court with a charge: it lets it go
  • tiny amount → DROP the smallest measure of a liquid, the bead that falls off a pipette

Someone being told to let go of the merest trace of something.

Two senses sit flush against each other with no joining word at all, so the clue reads as one instruction rather than as a pair of definitions.

2 down EMBODIED

Personified Space Boy briefly, then kicked the bucket (8)

Definition: Personified · charade + deletion

Something personified has been given a body to appear in, which is exactly what embodied says.

  • Space → EM an em is the printer's space, the one as wide as the type is tall
  • Boy briefly → BO the boy is cut short and loses his last letter
  • kicked the bucket → DIED the idiom for having passed away
  • briefly — put briefly, a word is stated in fewer letters, so the one beside it is docked at the end

A short-lived comic-strip hero called Space Boy, given flesh for a moment and then dead.

'Space Boy' reads as one name, capitals and all, and splits in two: the space is the printer's, and only the boy is shortened.

3 down VALE

Farewell, depression! (4)

Definition: Farewell · double definition

Vale is the Latin for farewell that English kept for valedictions, and a vale is a valley — a depression in the ground, not in the spirits.

  • Farewell → VALE the Latin parting word, still cut into gravestones and used in valedictions
  • depression → VALE the poet's word for a valley, a dip in the ground between hills

Someone waving a cheerful goodbye to their low mood.

The comma and the exclamation mark sell it as one cry of relief, when the two words are separate definitions standing side by side.

4 down ICE STORM

Tom cries pathetically in severe weather (3,5)

Definition: severe weather · anagram

An ice storm is freezing rain that glazes every surface it lands on and brings power lines down with the weight.

  • Tom cries → TOMCRIES eight letters of fodder, exactly the count the enumeration asks for
  • pathetically — a pathetic effort is a botched one, so the letters beside it come out badly made

A cat called Tom weeping feebly through a spell of terrible weather.

'Tom cries' looks like a small sentence and is a bag of letters; 'pathetically' is shuffling them, not describing how he sounds.

5 down LEARNS

Discovers artist reflected in piece of curved glass (6)

Definition: Discovers · container + reversal

To learn something is to discover it in the finding-out sense, not the explorer's sense of sighting new land.

  • piece of curved glass → LENS the curved glass in a camera or a pair of spectacles
  • artist → RA RA is the Royal Academician, the crossword's standing artist
  • reflected — a reflection shows a thing the other way round, so those two letters run backwards
  • in — the plain instruction to drop one piece inside the letters named beside it

Somebody catching a painter's reflection in a curved pane of glass.

'Discovers' promises a sighting to match the surface, and the answer wants the other kind of discovery — finding a fact out rather than catching sight of a person.

6 down DIMINUENDO

Stupid suggestive remark a knight emitted with decreasing loudness (10)

Definition: decreasing loudness · charade + deletion

A diminuendo is the direction in a score to play with steadily decreasing loudness, so the noun names the fade itself.

  • Stupid → DIM a dim person is a slow or stupid one
  • suggestive remark → INNUENDO an innuendo is a sly suggestive remark
  • a knight → N N is the knight in chess notation, and that is the letter the remark loses
  • emitted — to emit is to send out, so the letter named beside it is sent out of the word it was sitting in

A knight lets slip a stupid off-colour remark, trailing away into quiet.

Music's word for fading away is the definition, and the knight comes from chess notation rather than from the armour the surface has in mind.

7 down OTTAWA

Capital of Texas initially thanks Washington (6)

Definition: Capital · charade + first letters

Ottawa is the capital of Canada, so the bare word 'Capital' defines it by example rather than by synonym.

  • of Texas initially → OT the first letter of each of those two words, in the order they stand
  • thanks → TA ta is the everyday British thank-you
  • Washington → WA WA is the postal abbreviation for Washington state
  • initially — initially means at the outset, so only the opening letters of the words beside it are wanted

A quick note on American geography: the capital of Texas, with thanks to Washington.

'Capital of Texas' wants to be one phrase and would then be Austin; the definition stops after a single word, and 'of Texas' is worth only its two initials.

8 down GYRE

Vortex emptied gully and ravine (4)

Definition: Vortex · charade + outer letters

A gyre is a whirl or circular motion — an ocean gyre is a vortex the size of a sea basin.

  • gully → GY the three letters in the middle are poured out and the ends stay put
  • ravine → RE the same treatment for the second channel: first letter, last letter
  • emptied — emptying a word tips out everything between its walls and leaves the two end letters standing

Water pouring out of a gully and a ravine until both run dry.

'Vortex emptied' reads as one picture, a whirlpool draining away; the emptying is an instruction, and it applies to the two words that follow it.

13 down OSAGE

Ring Herb in Oklahoma’s largest county (5)

Definition: Oklahoma’s largest county · charade

Osage County takes in the entire Osage Nation reservation in northern Oklahoma and is the state's largest county by area.

  • Ring → O a ring drawn on paper is the letter O
  • Herb → SAGE the grey-green kitchen herb, capitalised here so it reads as a man's name

A phone call to a man called Herb, somewhere out in Oklahoma.

The capital H turns a kitchen herb into a person, which is the whole of the disguise; the county is named for the Osage nation whose reservation it covers.

14 down OCCASIONED

Caused pit viper to shed skin one day (10)

Definition: Caused · deletion + charade

To occasion something is to bring it about, so the past tense is a plain synonym for 'Caused' — the verb sense of a word better known as a noun.

  • pit viper → MOCCASIN the water moccasin, or cottonmouth, is a North American pit viper
  • one → ONE the word itself, written straight in
  • day → D D is the standard abbreviation for day
  • to shed skin — a snake sheds the skin on its outside, so the word beside it gives up its first and last letters

A pit viper sloughing its skin on one particular day.

'shed skin' is an instruction rather than part of the picture, and the snake has to be recognised by its species name before anything can be stripped off it.

16 down STAND

Holy person with bear (5)

Definition: bear · charade

To stand something is to bear it — the endurance sense, where what you can stand is what you can put up with.

  • Holy person → ST ST is the abbreviation for saint, the one on every church sign
  • with → AND and is the plain word for with, the one that joins two things

A saint keeping company with a bear.

'bear' is the verb meaning tolerate, not the animal the surface puts beside the saint: the sense wanted is the one in 'I can't stand it'.

19 down DREDGE UP

Raise great fear comedian Miles must be broadcast (6,2)

Definition: Raise · charade + homophone

To dredge something up is to raise it from the bottom, whether that is silt from a riverbed or an old grievance from memory.

  • great fear → DREAD dread is fear of the largest size
  • comedian Miles → JUPP Miles Jupp is a British comedian and radio presenter
  • great fear comedian Miles → DREDGE UP run those two words together out loud and the join between them turns into a soft g
  • must be broadcast — broadcast means put out on air to be heard, so what counts is how the words sound and not how they are spelt

A demand that a nervous comedian's spot be put out on the air.

British listeners know Miles Jupp from Radio 4, and the sound runs straight across the gap between the two words, so the join produces a letter neither of them owns.

21 down REVEILLE

Bugle call distorted in live reel (8)

Definition: Bugle call · anagram

Reveille is the bugle call sounded at daybreak to wake soldiers, from the French for 'wake up'.

  • live → LIVE four letters of fodder, straight off the page
  • reel → REEL four more, and the two words together are exactly the length wanted
  • distorted — a distorted signal arrives misshapen, so the letters beside it turn up out of their proper order

Something going wrong with the sound somewhere in a live reel of film.

'live reel' is film-editing talk on the surface and eight loose letters underneath; the answer is the call that wakes a barracks at first light.

23 down FIASCO

Motorsport firm admitting mid-season cock-up (6)

Definition: cock-up · container

A fiasco is a complete and public failure, which is what British English means by a cock-up.

  • Motorsport → FI the top formula is written F1, and the numeral does duty as the letter next to the F
  • mid-season → AS the two letters sitting at the centre of that six-letter word
  • firm → CO CO is the abbreviation for company
  • admitting — to admit someone is to let them in, so the letters named next go inside the ones already assembled

A motor-racing company owning up to a blunder halfway through the year.

Motorsport's own shorthand is F1, and the 1 has to be read as a letter — a British cock-up is a botched job, which is the sense the definition wants.

24 down DEBRIS

Recollected bride’s scattered stuff (6)

Definition: scattered stuff · anagram

Debris is what a wreck or an explosion leaves lying about, so 'scattered stuff' is the plain description of the mass noun.

  • bride’s → BRIDES the possessive s counts as a letter of fodder, which is what makes the count six
  • Recollected — read it as re-collected, gathered up a second time, and things gathered up again do not come back in the order they fell

Somebody gathering up a bride's belongings after they were flung everywhere.

'scattered' sits right beside the fodder and looks like the shuffling word; it belongs to the definition, and the instruction is the one at the front of the clue.

26 down BURG

Food tossed over fortified town (4)

Definition: fortified town · reversal

A burg is a walled or fortified town, the sense preserved in the -burgh and -burg endings of European place names.

  • Food → GRUB grub is the everyday British word for food
  • tossed over — tossing something over turns it upside down, so the letters beside it are read the other way about

Food being thrown over the walls of a fortified town.

The definition is doing quiet teaching: this is the element that survives in Edinburgh and Hamburg, place names built on a stronghold.

28 down NIKE

The King conceals ascendant spirit of victory (4)

Definition: spirit of victory · hidden word + reversal

Nike is the Greek goddess who personifies victory, so 'spirit of victory' points at the deity rather than at a mood.

  • The King → EKIN four letters running consecutively out of the first word and into the second
  • conceals — what conceals a thing has it inside itself, so the letters wanted are already sitting in the words as written
  • ascendant — ascendant means climbing, and a down entry read from the bottom upwards runs against the letters as they are printed

Elvis keeping a rising spirit to himself.

'The King' is Elvis on the surface and nothing but two printed words underneath; the goddess Nike personified victory for the Greeks, and the trainers are named after her.

29 down GEED

Encouraged U2’s guitarist to split and join up with New Order (4)

Definition: Encouraged · cycling

To gee someone up is to urge them on, from the driver's call to a horse, so 'Encouraged' is the past tense of that.

  • U2’s guitarist → GE the Irish band's guitarist goes by The Edge, and this is the back half of that name, now leading
  • U2’s guitarist → ED the front half of the same name, following on behind after the split
  • to split — splitting the name breaks it into two halves before anything else is done to it
  • join up with New Order — joining up again in a new order puts the two halves back together the other way round

U2's guitarist walking out of the band to sign up with New Order.

Both bands in the surface are real, and the instruction is hiding inside the sentence about them: the front half of the guitarist's name moves round to the back.
