Observer Everyman Crossword No 4,142

Across

1 across PERMISSIVE

Easy-going, according to correspondence (10)

Definition: Easy-going · charade

Easy-going → PERMISSIVE: both describe someone who lets things go rather than laying down rules, the word used of lenient parents and lax societies.

  • according to → PER 'as per the memo' means going by what the memo says, so this little Latin word is the three letters at the front
  • correspondence → MISSIVE a missive is a letter, the formal word for the kind of thing that arrives in the post

The comma invites you to read 'according to correspondence' as one phrase about what the letters said. It is two separate pieces, one Latin tag and one formal word for a letter.

6 across ICED

Knocked off - like some tea? (4)

Definition: Knocked off · double definition

'Knocked off' gives the gangland sense of killed; 'like some tea' gives the chilled version, an adjective for how the drink is served.

  • Knocked off → underworld slang for killed, the way a gangster reports a job finished
  • like some tea? → tea served cold over cubes from the freezer — an adjective describing the drink, not the drink itself

The dash pretends to join a hitman's report to a summer drinks order. Both halves are straight definitions of the same four letters, and neither is wordplay.

9 across DRAWING PIN

Making illustration of leg that's tacky (7,3)

Definition: tacky · charade

tacky → DRAWING PIN: the British name for a thumbtack, so the answer really is a tack, which is where the adjective comes from.

  • Making illustration → DRAWING the act of making an illustration with a pencil
  • leg → PIN 'pins' is British slang for legs, so a single one of them is a pin

The surface offers a sketch of somebody's leg and a rude verdict on it. Both halves of the wordplay are ordinary words hiding inside that picture.

10 across PASS

I don't know that way through mountains (4)

Definition: I don't know · double definition

'I don't know' gives the quiz answer that declines the question; 'way through mountains' gives the gap a road takes between two peaks.

  • I don't know → the word a quiz contestant says to decline a question and move it on
  • way through mountains → the gap between two peaks that a road or a walker can take, like the Khyber

'That' is the hinge, holding up a sentence that means neither of the two things it is made of. Four letters serve a quiz contestant and a mountaineer equally.

11 across CHEESE GRATER

He gets career arranging help for chef (12)

Definition: help for chef · anagram

help for chef → CHEESE GRATER: the kitchen tool that shreds a block into strands, so the help is of the equipment kind rather than a person.

  • He gets career → HEGETSCAREER twelve letters of office talk, handed over to be laid out again
  • arranging — To arrange things is to set them out in an order of your choosing, so the letters may be laid down in any order that works.

The surface is a story about a colleague being given a leg-up at work. The kitchen only appears once you count those twelve letters against the enumeration.

15 across NASCENT

Rising smell of sodium? (7)

Definition: Rising · charade

Rising → NASCENT: just coming into being, said of a talent or an industry in its first growth rather than one already arrived.

  • sodium → NA the chemical symbol for the element, taken from its Latin name natrium
  • smell → SCENT a scent is a smell, usually a pleasant one you follow or wear

Chemistry supplies symbols crosswords use constantly, and this is one of the commonest. The wordplay reads the phrase as the element's own smell, which is what puts the symbol first.

16 across TACTILE

A bit of eye-contact, I learn, is touching (7)

Definition: touching · hidden word

touching → TACTILE: belonging to the sense of touch, as a tactile display is one meant to be handled, not the moving sense of the word.

  • eye-contact, I learn → TACTILE seven consecutive letters starting inside the hyphenated word and running on across the comma and the pronoun into the next
  • A bit of — A bit of something is a piece taken out of the middle of it and left exactly as it was found.

The hyphen and the two commas are punctuation a setter may ignore, and they sit exactly where the letters cross from one word into the next.

17 across SCRATCH

You should come up to this damage (7)

Definition: You should come up to this · double definition

'You should come up to this' points at the required standard in the idiom; 'damage' gives the shallow mark left by something sharp.

  • You should come up to this → the noun inside the idiom 'come up to ___', meaning to meet the standard required
  • damage → the shallow mark a key leaves down the side of a car

Boxers once had to stand at a line drawn in the ground to show they could go on, which is where the idiom comes from and why it means meeting a standard.

19 across OATMEAL

Nothing at repast but porridge, basically (7)

Definition: porridge, basically · charade

porridge, basically → OATMEAL: porridge is oatmeal boiled in water or milk, so 'basically' points at what it is made of rather than offering a synonym.

  • Nothing → O nothing is written as a nought, and a nought on the page is the letter it looks like
  • at → AT the little word taken straight off the clue
  • repast → MEAL a repast is a meal, the formal word for a sitting down to eat

'Porridge' is British slang for a prison sentence, which is the bleak picture the surface is painting. The definition wants the plain breakfast instead.

20 across FIGURE SKATER

Did she hope her 8 would get +5.0? (6,6)

Definition: Did she hope her 8 would get +5.0? · cryptic definition

The competitor the question is about: a woman marked by judges for the figures she cuts on ice, so her 8 is both her shape and her score.

  • her 8 → the surface offers a mark out of ten; the numeral is also a shape, and the shape is one traced on ice
  • would get +5.0? → under the modern judging system the grade of execution runs from minus five to plus five, so this is a perfect one

Everyman writes the numeral rather than the word, so '8' reads as a score. It is the other sense that matters: a figure is a shape, and this one is cut into the ice.

23 across RAIL

Pole, fibber, heading west (4)

Definition: Pole · reversal

Pole → RAIL: a rail is a bar of wood or metal fixed in place, the long pole you lean on at a fence or hang washing from.

  • fibber → RAIL someone who fibs is a liar, and here that word travels the other way along the row
  • heading west — West is leftwards on a map and on the grid, so an across answer sent that way is read out from its last letter back.

Everything points at a Polish fibber until you notice that 'Pole' can be a length of wood, and that 'heading west' is an instruction rather than a journey.

24 across INVIGORATE

Get perked up - fantastic rigatoni and drops of excellent vinos (10)

Definition: Get perked up · anagram + first letters

Get perked up → INVIGORATE: to invigorate someone is to put fresh energy into them, which is getting them perked up.

  • rigatoni → RIGATONI eight letters of ridged pasta tubes, offered up for rearrangement
  • excellent vinos → EV the first letter of each of those two words, joining the eight above
  • fantastic — Here it means fanciful rather than excellent: what is fantastic has no fixed shape, so the letters may take one they never had.
  • drops of — A drop is the smallest amount you can take of a thing, and the smallest amount of a written word is the letter it starts with.

The surface is a menu of Italian food and drink, and that is where 'fantastic' hides: it reads as praise for the pasta rather than as the instruction it is.

25 across HEEL

Part of foot to get better, so we're told (4)

Definition: Part of foot · homophone

Part of foot → HEEL: the rounded back of it, the bit that strikes the ground first with every step you take.

  • to get better → HEEL getting better after an injury is healing, and that word said aloud is written the other way here
  • so we're told — Being told a thing puts it in the ear rather than on the page, so what is wanted is a different spelling of the same sound.

Two homely words and one sound. The trap is reading 'get better' as improve rather than recover, since only the recovering sense supplies what you say aloud.

26 across RAINSTORMS

Idea sessions not started in precipitation (10)

Definition: precipitation · deletion

precipitation → RAINSTORMS: precipitation is the forecaster's word for water falling out of the sky, and a storm of rain is one violent form of it.

  • Idea sessions → RAINSTORMS sessions for generating ideas are BRAINSTORMS, and without the letter they begin with these ten remain
  • not started — A word not started is one you begin without its opening letter, so that letter is simply never written.

The whole surface is a meeting round a whiteboard and the definition is a weather forecast; one letter at the front is all that stands between them.

Down

1 down PADS

Increases apparent value of homes (4)

Definition: Increases apparent value · double definition

'Increases apparent value' gives the puffing-up sense, as of an expense claim; 'homes' gives the informal word for the place someone lives.

  • Increases apparent value → the sense used of an expense claim or a CV puffed up with things that were never there
  • homes → informal for the place someone lives, as in 'back at my place'

Two senses of one four-letter word, welded by 'of' into an estate agent's sentence about property. Nothing here is spelled out of anything else.

2 down ROAM

Travel with Mum - otherwise going back (4)

Definition: Travel · charade + reversal

Travel → ROAM: to roam is to travel with no fixed route, wandering about rather than heading anywhere in particular.

  • Mum → MA ma is what a child calls its mother, the two-letter version setters lean on
  • otherwise → OR 'do it, or else' is 'do it, otherwise' — the same choice between two things
  • going back — Going back is retracing your steps, so the two pieces built before it are read out again from their far end.

A smooth surface about a trip with your mother, and every word of it is working. 'Otherwise' for OR is worth pocketing; setters use it constantly.

3 down IRISH SETTER

Limerick writer offering conundrums, perhaps your best friend? (5,6)

Definition: perhaps your best friend? · charade

perhaps your best friend? → IRISH SETTER: a breed of gundog, and a dog is proverbially man's best friend, with 'perhaps' flagging one example of the class.

  • Limerick → IRISH Limerick is a city and county in the south-west of Ireland, so anything from there is this
  • writer offering conundrums → SETTER the person who writes a crossword is its setter, and a crossword is a page of conundrums

Limerick is a place before it is a five-line verse, and the setter is the person whose puzzle you are holding — a self-reference this paper enjoys.

4 down SUGGEST

Good cops straightened out gusset, getting intimate (7)

Definition: intimate · anagram

intimate → SUGGEST: the verb, stressed on the last syllable, meaning to hint at a thing rather than state it outright.

  • Good → G g is the standard abbreviation for good, the mark on an old school report
  • gusset → GUSSET the six letters of the tailoring word, handed over to be rearranged
  • cops — To cop something is to catch or take it, so the letter named first is what picks up everything that follows.
  • straightened out — Something straightened out has been pulled apart and laid down again in a different arrangement, which is what happens to the letters beside it.

'Good cops' opens like a sentence about the police, which is exactly why 'cops' reads as a plural noun rather than the verb holding the wordplay together.

5 down VAINEST

After revolution, natives most proud (7)

Definition: most proud · anagram

most proud → VAINEST: vanity is pride in one's own looks or worth, and both words are superlatives of it.

  • natives → NATIVES seven letters that already contain everything wanted, in the wrong arrangement
  • revolution — A revolution overthrows the established order, and the word beside it loses the order of its letters the same way.

'Revolution' is the trap: it sounds like an instruction to turn the word round, and turning that word round gives nothing usable. Here it means upheaval.

7 down COASTLINES

Make little effort with script, Land's Ends? (10)

Definition: Land's Ends? · charade

Land's Ends? → COASTLINES: the lines where land stops and sea begins, one at every edge of a country rather than only at the Cornish one.

  • Make little effort → COAST to coast through a job is to let momentum do it for you
  • script → LINES an actor learns their lines, which is the script as it is divided up for speaking

Land's End is the far south-western tip of Cornwall, and the italics mark the name as borrowed. Everyman then pluralises it into a general description.

8 down DISCREETLY

With diplomacy - and one-by-one, did you say? (10)

Definition: With diplomacy · homophone

With diplomacy → DISCREETLY: doing a thing discreetly is doing it without drawing attention, the tact you use when a matter is delicate.

  • one-by-one → DISCREETLY taking things one by one is taking them DISCRETELY, separately, the spelling with an E in the middle — and said aloud it is written the other way
  • did you say? — Asking what you said moves the clue from the page to the ear: what matters now is the sound, not the spelling.

English keeps both spellings and pronounces them identically: the one with the E in the middle means separately, the one wanted here means tactfully.

12 down ROCK THE BOAT

Beth took car, swerving to make trouble (4,3,4)

Definition: make trouble · anagram

make trouble → ROCK THE BOAT: the idiom for upsetting a settled arrangement, the thing you are told not to do when everyone else is content.

  • Beth took car → BETHTOOKCAR eleven letters of the invented story, waiting to be rearranged
  • swerving — A car that swerves leaves the line it was following, and the letters beside it leave the order they were given in.

Beth and her borrowed car exist only to make eleven letters look like a person doing something. The idiom is what you say of someone who will not let things settle.

13 down AND SO FORTH

Ergo, Scottish river etc (3,2,5)

Definition: etc · charade

etc → AND SO FORTH: both are the tag put at the end of a list to say the rest carries on in the same way.

  • Ergo → AND SO ergo is therefore, and 'and so' is the plain English way of saying it
  • Scottish river → FORTH the river running through Stirling into the firth that Edinburgh looks out on

'Ergo' is the word a lawyer uses for a phrase everyone else says plainly, and that Latin flourish is the only misdirection on offer here.

14 down USER'S GUIDE

Manual labour finally, I guess, due for upgrade? (5,5)

Definition: Manual · anagram + last letter

Manual → USER'S GUIDE: the booklet that comes with a machine and tells you how to work it, which is exactly what a manual is.

  • labour finally → R the last letter of the word standing in front of it
  • I guess, due → IGUESSDUE nine letters, the pronoun and both words after it, thrown in with the letter above
  • finally — Finally means at the very end, so only the letter the word beside it finishes on is wanted.
  • upgrade — Anything due for an upgrade is going to be rebuilt, and the setter takes that loosely enough to rebuild the letters themselves.

'Manual labour' is one phrase in the surface and two separate jobs in the parse: the first word defines, the second only lends the letter it ends on.

18 down HOSANNA

Primarily happy outcry, sung, about nifty Nazarene's advent? (7)

Definition: happy outcry, sung, about nifty Nazarene's advent? · &lit + first letters

The whole clue describes the cry: a shout of praise raised at the coming of Jesus of Nazareth, sung by the crowds on Palm Sunday.

  • happy outcry, sung → HOS the opening letter of each of those three words, in the order the clue gives them
  • about nifty Nazarene's advent → ANNA the same trick continued through the remaining four words, the preposition included
  • Primarily — Primarily points at what comes first, and what comes first in a written word is the letter it opens with.

Seven words and seven letters, and 'about' earns its keep twice: it reads as a preposition and hands over its own initial. Read straight, the sentence describes the shout itself.

19 down ORATION

Relation in on speech (7)

Definition: speech · container

speech → ORATION: a formal public speech, the grand kind delivered from a platform rather than a few words said at a table.

  • on → ON the two-letter word taken at face value, and it splits to make room
  • Relation → RATIO the relation between two quantities is their ratio, as in three to one
  • in — The plainest insertion word there is: whatever is named before it gets written inside whatever comes after.

'Relation' meaning ratio is the step to spot; the family sense is the decoy, and 'in on' reads as a phrase about being party to something.

21 down FAIR

Just panache student's missing (4)

Definition: Just · deletion

Just → FAIR: a fair decision is an even-handed one, the umpire's sense of the word rather than the weather's or the funfair's.

  • panache → FAIR panache is FLAIR, and with one letter taken out of it these four remain
  • student's → L L is the letter on the plate a British learner driver must display until they pass the test
  • missing — What is missing has been left out, so the letter named beside it never makes it into the word.

'Just panache' reads as a shrug about style over substance, which is the whole disguise: 'just' is the definition and 'panache' is only a word to be trimmed.

22 down MESS

Everyman on vacation sightsees in confusion (4)

Definition: confusion · charade + outer letters

confusion → MESS: a state of disorder, things scattered about with nothing where it belongs — the muddle sense rather than the soldiers' dining room.

  • Everyman → ME the setter signs every one of these puzzles with that name, so when he writes it he means himself
  • sightsees → SS the outer letters of that word, with everything between them cleared out
  • on vacation — A house whose owners are on vacation stands empty, and a word emptied that way keeps only the letters at its two ends.

Everyman putting himself in his own clue is a habit worth expecting here. The surface reads as a holidaymaker wandering about, and both halves of it are instructions.
