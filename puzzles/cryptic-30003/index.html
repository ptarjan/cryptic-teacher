Across

1 across FLIP-FLOP

Wearing this, somersault a failure (4-4)

Definition: Wearing this · charade

Flip-flops are the rubber sandals held on by a thong between the toes, so 'wearing this' identifies the answer as a thing you put on.

  • somersault → FLIP a flip is a somersault, the gymnast's word for turning right over in the air
  • a failure → FLOP a flop is a thing that fails — the show that closes in a week

Turning a somersault in the wrong footwear and coming badly unstuck.

The definition is the two words at the front, and 'this' does all the pointing: the clue never names a shoe, it only says the answer is worn.

6 across MUGGLE

Simpleton with broken leg he can’t magically fix (6)

Definition: he can’t magically fix · charade + anagram

In those books a Muggle is a person born with no magic at all, so being unable to fix anything by spell is the whole of what defines them.

  • Simpleton → MUG a mug is a gullible fool, the one who falls for it every time
  • leg → GLE anagram fodder, three letters
  • broken — A broken thing has come apart and been put back together wrong, which is what happens to the three letters sitting next to it.

A dim character hobbling about on a leg that no spell of his is going to mend.

Muggle is the Harry Potter books' word for anyone born without magic, and 'broken' works twice over: it describes the leg in the picture and shuffles it in the wordplay.

9 across SPOT ON

Something to eat with tea say coming in? Exactly (4,2)

Definition: Exactly · container + homophone

'Spot on' is the everyday British way of saying dead right, so it stands in for 'exactly' as a flat expression of agreement.

  • Something to eat with → SPOON what you eat with rather than what you eat — the implement, not the food
  • tea → T said aloud, the name of this single letter is the word for the drink
  • say — 'Say' asks for the spoken name of a thing rather than its spelling, and the drink named beside it is exactly how one letter is pronounced.
  • coming in — Something coming in is arriving inside rather than alongside, so what precedes it is planted in the middle of the longer word.

Asking whether anything edible is arriving with the tea, and being told there is.

The question mark flags the pun on 'tea': the drink in the picture, the name of a letter in the wordplay. 'Something to eat with' is the implement, not the cake.

10 across MARINADE

Pelting rain made a liquid mixture over some food (8)

Definition: a liquid mixture over some food · anagram

A marinade is the seasoned liquid you steep meat or fish in before cooking, which is a liquid mixture poured over food.

  • rain made → RAINMADE anagram fodder, eight letters
  • Pelting — Rain that pelts is thrown down hard and scattered where it lands, and letters scattered like that come to rest in a new order.

A downpour heavy enough to soak everything laid out for the picnic.

The clue runs as one unbroken sentence about a ruined picnic, so 'pelting' reads as a description of the rain rather than the instruction it is.

11 across RELAY RACE

One may start this contest, but one won’t finish (5,4)

Definition: contest · cryptic definition

A relay race is a contest between teams whose members run in turn, so 'contest' defines it by the broader class it belongs to.

  • One may start this → reads as the official with the pistol, the person whose job is getting an event under way
  • but one won’t finish → the setter means an ordinary competitor both times: whoever runs the first leg hands the baton on and stops

A competition somebody sets going and then never sees the end of.

Read straight, this sounds like a complaint about an event nobody sees through; the two 'one's are teammates, and handing over is the point rather than a failure.

13 across FREED

Fine supplier of venison returns, out of jail (5)

Definition: out of jail · charade + reversal

To be freed is to be let out of confinement, and 'out of jail' is that release at its most literal — the state afterwards, not the act.

  • Fine → F F is the grade stamped on a pencil for fine
  • supplier of venison → REED venison is deer meat, and this deer is facing the other way
  • returns — A thing that returns comes back along the way it went, so the animal named before it is written from its last letter to its first.

The venison dealer comes back to trade once his fine has been paid.

'Fine' opens the clue like a word of approval and is a single letter off a pencil. The comma is the only sign that the definition starts where it does.

15 across TALENT

Story books, a special gift (6)

Definition: a special gift · charade

A talent is an ability you were born with rather than one you earned, which is exactly what makes 'gift' the everyday word for it.

  • Story → TALE a tale is a story
  • books → NT NT is the New Testament, one of the two Testaments a crossword means by 'books'

A book of stories handed over as a particularly thoughtful present.

A talent was once a weight of silver, and the parable of the talents is how a unit of money came to mean an inborn ability — which is why books and gifts sit so easily in one sentence.

17 across FLY-TIP

End of insect: illegally dispose of it (3-3)

Definition: illegally dispose of it · charade

To fly-tip is to dump waste illegally, the British term for leaving rubbish in a lay-by or a field rather than taking it to a refuse site.

  • insect → FLY the commonest insect of all, and conveniently three letters
  • End → TIP the tip of a thing is its end, its furthest point

The back end of an insect, and an instruction to get rid of it on the quiet.

Read the answer as a possessive and the first two words are the wordplay outright — the tip of a fly — with the colon standing in for an equals sign.

18 across RENDER

Hand over a first thin coat (6)

Definition: Hand over · double definition

To render is to hand something over or give it up, and in building the render is the thin first coat of mortar laid on a wall.

  • Hand over → the verb for giving something up, as in the biblical instruction to give Caesar his due
  • a first thin coat → the builder's word for the layer of mortar spread straight onto brickwork

Passing across the first light layer of plaster.

Both senses come from the French rendre, to give back: one gives up a possession, the other gives a wall its finish. 'First' matters — the later coats have their own names.

19 across BARREL

Large quantity of beer over which one is powerless (6)

Definition: Large quantity of beer · double definition

A barrel is the cask beer is sold by, and the idiom 'over a barrel' means helpless, so the second half names it through the phrase it lives in.

  • Large quantity of beer → the cask brewers still sell by, thirty-six gallons of it
  • over which one is powerless → this half points at an idiom rather than a synonym: held over one of these, you have no way to resist

So much ale in the cask that nobody could do a thing about it.

'Over a barrel' is the idiom the second half aims at — someone bent over one for a flogging could not fight back — and beer is still sold by the barrel.

21 across MARSH

On planet, hot waterlogged area (5)

Definition: waterlogged area · charade

A marsh is low ground permanently soaked and soft underfoot, which is what a waterlogged area is.

  • planet → MARS the fourth planet out from the sun, the red one
  • hot → H H is the letter on the hot tap

Somewhere out on another planet, hot and soaking wet.

'Hot' looks like a temperature in a sentence about a planet's terrain and is really the single letter stamped on a bathroom tap.

22 across WOLVERINE

Drunken wine lover is a glutton (9)

Definition: a glutton · anagram

The wolverine's older English name is the glutton — Gulo gulo, the greedy northern weasel — so this is a second name, not a remark about appetite.

  • wine lover → WINELOVER anagram fodder, nine letters
  • Drunken — A drunk cannot hold a straight line, and letters that will not hold their line arrive in a different order from the one you were given.

A wine buff who has had far too much and eats like a pig.

'Drunken' describes the wine lover in the picture and instructs you in the wordplay, and one word doing both jobs is what keeps the fodder out of sight.

25 across INCIDENT

As part of detective force, sent first off to disturbance (8)

Definition: disturbance · charade + deletion

An incident is any disturbance that breaks the ordinary run of things — the neutral word police and reporters both use for public trouble.

  • As part of → IN to be part of something is to be in it
  • detective force → CID the CID is the Criminal Investigation Department, the British police's plain-clothes detectives
  • sent → ENT the word 'sent' with its opening letter taken away
  • first off — This phrase takes away the letter at the front of the word beside it, so what is left begins one letter later.

A detective dispatched the moment trouble gets reported.

'Sent first off' reads as being despatched straight away, and the phrase is really the word plus an instruction to dock its opening letter.

26 across DAMAGE

Barrier to become weaker perhaps, a likely result of crash (6)

Definition: a likely result of crash · charade

Damage is the harm a collision leaves behind, so the definition reaches the answer through its cause instead of offering a synonym.

  • Barrier → DAM a dam is the barrier thrown across a river
  • become weaker → AGE to age is to grow older, and growing weaker is the part of that the clue picks out
  • perhaps — This word concedes the equation runs one way only — growing weaker is one of the things the piece beside it covers, not the whole of it.

A wall bound to weaken eventually, and what a collision would leave of it.

Everything before the last comma is wordplay, and the grammar disguises that: 'barrier to become weaker' reads as a purpose rather than two pieces laid end to end.

28 across CRISES

Crucial moments for which Charlie gets up (6)

Definition: Crucial moments · charade

A crisis is the turning point at which a situation must go one way or the other, so 'crucial moments' is the plural of exactly that.

  • Charlie → C C is Charlie in the radio alphabet
  • gets up → RISES to get up is to rise

Emergencies serious enough to get a man called Charlie out of bed.

Charlie is C in the NATO alphabet, the list that also gives Oscar and Victor, and the capital letter is there to sell you a man rather than a letter.

29 across BERGAMOT

Essential oil for a car test on mass of ice (8)

Definition: Essential oil · charade

Bergamot is the citrus oil pressed from a Mediterranean orange and used to scent Earl Grey tea, so it is one of the essential oils by name.

  • mass of ice → BERG a berg is the floating mass, short for iceberg
  • a → A the bare article, taken straight off the page
  • car test → MOT the MOT is the annual roadworthiness test every older British car has to pass
  • on — In an across clue this word means resting against the far side of what follows, so the pieces named first end up to the right of it.

An indispensable lubricant needed to test a car out on the ice.

'Essential oil' is a set phrase for a plant extract, and the surface asks you to hear it as an oil that is essential — necessary — instead.

2 down LAP

Part of body disappearing as one stands (3)

Definition: Part of body · double definition

A lap is the fold from waist to knees of a seated person — a real part of the body, and one that exists only while you are sitting.

  • Part of body → the shelf a seated person makes from waist to knees, where the cat settles and the plate goes
  • disappearing as one stands → a children's riddle used as the second pointer: which bit of you is only there while you are sitting down?

A piece of anatomy that vanishes the moment its owner gets to their feet.

Chambers has it as the fold of clothes and body from waist to knees of a person sitting down, so what goes missing when you get up is a shape you make rather than any flesh.

3 down PITTA

Bread is mine, thank you (5)

Definition: Bread · charade

Pitta is the flat pocket bread of Greek and Middle Eastern cooking, so the definition names the answer by the class it belongs to.

  • mine → PIT a pit is a mine, the coal sort with a winding gear over it
  • thank you → TA ta is the everyday British thanks, said far more often than written

Claiming the loaf as your own and being polite about getting it.

'Is mine' reads as a possessive claim, and 'mine' in the wordplay is a noun — the hole in the ground, not the pronoun.

4 down LONE RANGER

Wild West law enforcer, one separating two hands with fury (4,6)

Definition: Wild West law enforcer · charade

The Lone Ranger is the masked ex-Texas Ranger of American radio and television westerns, so the definition names him by the job he does out there.

  • two hands → L the two hands are left and right, and this is the left one
  • one → ONE the number written out in full rather than left as a figure
  • two hands → R the right hand, the second of the same pair
  • fury → ANGER anger is fury
  • separating — To separate two things is to stand between them, so the number named here takes its place in the gap between the pair of letters.

A frontier marshal angrily prising two people's hands apart.

Hands abbreviate to L and R the way they are stamped inside a pair of gloves, and the surface picture of a peacemaker is what stops you seeing letters.

5 down PUMICE

Abrasive, turned up with computer peripherals (6)

Definition: Abrasive · charade + reversal

An abrasive is any substance used to rub a surface down, and pumice — the frothy volcanic stone on the bathroom shelf — is the domestic one.

  • up → PU the two letters of 'up' written the other way round
  • computer peripherals → MICE the things that sit beside a keyboard, plural of mouse
  • turned — A thing that has been turned now faces the other way, so the little word beside it is written from its last letter back to its first.

A rough character who arrived carrying the office keyboards and mice.

'Turned up' looks like one phrase meaning arrived, and it is two things: the first word is the instruction and the second is what it acts on.

6 down MARE

Horse that appears on the moon (4)

Definition: Horse · double definition

A mare is a female horse, and the same four letters name the dark plains on the moon that early astronomers took for water.

  • Horse → the female of the species, the one in the paddock with a foal beside her
  • appears on the moon → astronomers' name for the dark basalt plains you can see with the naked eye

A horse somehow visible up on the surface of the moon.

Latin mare means sea, and the moon's dark patches were christened seas by early observers — Mare Tranquillitatis and the rest — so one spelling covers a horse and a lunar plain.

7 down GENERATOR

Shivering, got nearer source of energy (9)

Definition: source of energy · anagram

A generator turns motion into electricity, so it is literally where the power in a circuit comes from rather than merely a supply of it.

  • got nearer → GOTNEARER anagram fodder, nine letters
  • Shivering — Something shivering cannot hold still, and letters that will not hold still do not stay in the order they were handed over in.

Someone trembling with cold edging closer to the fire.

'Got nearer' is the picture of creeping towards warmth and the letters themselves at once, and the cold in 'shivering' is what makes you read it as description.

8 down LED ZEPPELIN

Band of light aircraft (3,8)

Definition: Band · charade

Led Zeppelin were the English rock group of the seventies, so 'Band' defines the answer by naming the class one particular band belongs to.

  • light → LED the light-emitting diode in every torch and gadget, three letters
  • aircraft → ZEPPELIN the rigid airship built by Count von Zeppelin, an aircraft in earnest

A stripe of light thrown down by a small plane.

'Light aircraft' is a phrase in its own right and the clue splits it straight down the middle, so the trap is reading three words where there are only two pieces.

12 down ENTERTAINER

Somehow retrain teen for job in showbiz (11)

Definition: job in showbiz · anagram

An entertainer is anyone who performs for a paying audience — singer, comic, conjuror — so the definition points at the trade rather than any one act.

  • retrain teen → RETRAINTEEN anagram fodder, eleven letters
  • Somehow — This word refuses to say by what means, and an instruction that will not name its method is telling you the letters arrive in an order of their own.

Putting a teenager through a retraining scheme aimed at a career on the stage.

The fodder hides in plain sight as a careers-advice sentence, and 'teen' is the last word you would suspect of being letters rather than a person.

14 down EL SALVADOR

Salad lover moved to the country (2,8)

Definition: the country · anagram

El Salvador is a small Central American republic, so 'the country' defines it only by category — the whole class standing in for one member.

  • Salad lover → SALADLOVER anagram fodder, ten letters
  • moved — Something moved is no longer where it was put, and letters shifted out of their places come back in a different arrangement.

A devotee of salads giving up the town and going to live among fields.

'Moved to the country' is one ordinary phrase about leaving the city, and it hides both the instruction and the point where the definition begins.

16 down LANDSLIPS

Naturally, they may fall off a cliff (9)

Definition: they may fall off a cliff · cryptic definition

A landslip is a mass of earth and rock breaking away and sliding downhill, so the thing falling off the cliff is the cliff itself.

  • Naturally → the surface hears 'of course', the shrug you put in front of something everybody already agrees with
  • they may fall off a cliff → read one way it is walkers or objects going over the edge; the setter means the edge itself coming away, and by natural causes

An offhand remark that of course things go over the edge of a cliff.

'Naturally' carries the whole clue: not 'obviously' but 'by natural process, with nobody pushing'. A landslip is the British word for a landslide.

20 down HOT TUB

Centre installs unwarranted bath (3,3)

Definition: bath · container

A hot tub is the heated outdoor bath people sit around in, so 'bath' names it plainly — just not the enamel indoor kind you picture first.

  • Centre → HUB the hub is the centre of a wheel, and of anything else that radiates
  • unwarranted → OTT OTT is short for over the top — excessive, more than the case warrants
  • installs — To install something is to fit it into the fabric of a place, so the letters named next are set into the middle of the word before them.

A leisure centre putting in a bath that nobody had asked for.

'Centre installs' reads as a sports centre doing building work and 'unwarranted' as somebody's complaint about the spending; both are wordplay.

23 down RUMBA

A Cuban export, strong drink, going by airline (5)

Definition: A Cuban export · charade

The rumba is the ballroom dance that came out of Cuba, so 'a Cuban export' names the answer by where it travelled from.

  • strong drink → RUM the spirit distilled from sugar cane
  • airline → BA BA is British Airways, the crossword's default carrier
  • going by — To go by a thing is to pass along its side, so the two letters that follow settle next to the piece already in place.

Somebody flying out of Havana with duty-free bottles in the bag.

'A Cuban export' does the misdirecting: the island's famous exports are cigars and rum, and the rum has been put to work in the wordplay instead.

24 down MEWS

Ponder aloud in old stables (4)

Definition: old stables · homophone

A mews is a row of stables built behind a London terrace, later turned into houses — the word came from the royal hawk cages the stables replaced.

  • Ponder → MEWS to muse is to ponder, and the spelling changes when you write down what you hear
  • aloud — Aloud asks for the sound of a word rather than its spelling, so what goes into the grid is a different word said the same way.

Thinking out loud in a cobbled yard behind the houses.

'Ponder aloud' looks like one action, musing out loud, and the second word is the instruction. Mews survive all over London because the horses went and the buildings stayed.

27 down GOO

Sticky stuff not entirely satisfactory (3)

Definition: Sticky stuff · deletion

Goo is the informal word for any thick sticky substance, the stuff that clings to your fingers and will not shake off.

  • satisfactory → GOO the four-letter word for a satisfactory result, here with its last letter left off
  • not entirely — This says the word is there but not all of it, and what gets left off is the letter at the end.

Something adhesive that falls just short of being good enough.

'Not entirely satisfactory' is such a natural phrase — the mildest of complaints — that it reads as one verdict rather than a word plus an instruction.
