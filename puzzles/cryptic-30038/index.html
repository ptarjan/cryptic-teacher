Across

9 across LIBRA

Left underwear lying around one’s house (5)

Definition: house · charade + container

Astrologers call each division of the zodiac a house, so the seventh sign answers to 'house' in that specialist sense.

  • Left → L L is the standard abbreviation for left
  • one → I the Roman numeral for one
  • underwear → BRA the everyday undergarment, three letters long
  • lying around — Something lying around another thing encircles it, so the letter named next is dropped inside.

After underwear and somewhere to live, 'house' reads as a home. The house wanted is one of the twelve in astrology, which is how a zodiac sign gets clued.

10 across YORKSHIRE

Pig bowl is her potty (9)

Definition: Pig · charade + anagram

A Yorkshire is a breed of large white pig, so the answer is defined by example: one breed standing for the animal.

  • bowl → YORK in cricket, to york a batsman is to bowl the ball that pitches right under his bat
  • is her → SHIRE these five letters, rearranged
  • potty — Potty is British for mildly mad, and letters that have gone mad will not hold the order they were given in.

'Pig bowl' reads as a trough, which is the joke — bowl is the cricketer's verb, and several British pig breeds are named after counties.

11 across TWENTY TEN

2x + x = y? (6,3)

Definition: y? · charade

Written out in words, the year 2010 is twenty ten, and y is the usual abbreviation for year on forms and in dates.

  • 2x → TWENTY x is the Roman numeral ten, and two of them make this
  • + x → TEN the same numeral again, on its own

The clue is laid out as algebra and solved as Roman numerals — nothing here is a sum. The question mark warns that the answer is a date rather than a number.

12 across AILED

Cockney showered and was unwell (5)

Definition: was unwell · deletion

To ail is to be unwell, an old verb kept alive mostly by crosswords and by the phrase 'what ails you?'.

  • showered → HAILED hail is what showers down, so to shower is to hail
  • Cockney → the H at the front is the letter that goes
  • Cockney — A Cockney speaker drops the aitch at the front of a word, so that letter is not written here either.

Guardian setters use 'Cockney' as a standing instruction to drop an aitch. The surface, someone caught out in a downpour, is doing nothing else at all.

13 across PET NAME

Maybe 25 go touring Thailand and Vietnam (3,4)

Definition: Maybe 25 · container + charade

The answer at 25 across is one particular term of endearment, and 'Maybe' flags that it is only an example of the class.

  • go → PEE 'go' is the nursery euphemism for what you do in the lavatory
  • Thailand → T T is Thailand's international vehicle registration code
  • Vietnam → NAM 'Nam is the familiar short form, from the war and its films
  • touring — A tour goes right round its subject, so the short word here ends up enclosed by the letters on either side.

The holiday surface is a good disguise. Neither country hands over its name: one gives a car code, the other a nickname, and 'go' is at its most childish.

15 across HARD-WON

Whiskey drunk by upstanding member requiring great effort (4-3)

Definition: requiring great effort · container

A hard-won victory is one that cost great effort to get, the adjective for anything gained only after a long struggle.

  • upstanding member → HARDON 'member' is the anatomical euphemism and 'upstanding' means erect; the vulgar phrase is in Chambers
  • Whiskey → W W is Whiskey in the NATO phonetic alphabet
  • drunk by — What is drunk goes inside the drinker, so the letter before it is swallowed by the phrase that follows.

The surface is a drinking party and the member is not a club's. The hyphen in the enumeration is the tell: the answer is an adjective, not a body part.

17 across SCENE

Old boy leaves disgusting picture (5)

Definition: picture · deletion

A scene is a picture of a place or an incident — the word covers a painted view and a moment in a play alike.

  • Old boy → OB is the standard abbreviation for an old boy, a former pupil of a school
  • disgusting → OBSCENE obscene = disgusting, offensively indecent
  • leaves — This is the departure sense and not the foliage one: what it names walks out of the longer word.

'Old boy' looks like the subject of a sentence about a school and is really two letters to be taken away. Nothing else in the surface is doing any work.

18 across HIM

That bloke’s song on the radio (3)

Definition: That bloke · homophone

It is the object pronoun for a male person, so 'that bloke' points at exactly the same man in words rather than grammar.

  • song → HIM a hymn is a song sung in church
  • on the radio — Radio carries sound and not spelling, so what you want is the word you hear rather than the one printed.

The two are exact sound-twins once the silent letters go. 'That bloke's' reads as a possessive and is really 'that bloke is', which is the joinery.

20 across LOWER

Take down bloomers having removed outer wear (5)

Definition: Take down · deletion

To lower a flag, a blind or a drawbridge is to take it down, so the two verbs swap in either direction.

  • bloomers → FLOWERS bloomers are things that bloom
  • outer wear → the F and the S at the two ends come off
  • having removed outer wear — The outer wear is the letter at each end, and taking those off leaves the middle of the word standing.

Bloomers are underwear, which is the joke — what comes off is the outside of the word, not the outside of the outfit.

22 across TERRIER

Nameless casserole recipe for dog (7)

Definition: dog · deletion + charade

A terrier is a small breed of dog, originally bred to bolt foxes from their earths, so 'dog' names it by category.

  • casserole → TERRINE a terrine is the earthenware pot for a slow-cooked dish, and the dish itself
  • Nameless → the N, standing for name, is what goes
  • recipe → R R is the abbreviation for recipe, the Latin instruction at the head of a prescription
  • Nameless — N is the standard abbreviation for name, and the '-less' on the end is the instruction to do without it.

'Nameless casserole' sounds like something unpleasant found at the back of the fridge. Eddie, the dedicatee of the whole puzzle, is thought to have been one of these.

25 across SWEETIE

What’s behind James Bond cuddling little baby? (7)

Definition: baby · container + charade + last letter

Sweetie is an affectionate name for a loved one, the same endearment as baby — and in Britain it is also a boiled sweet.

  • James → S the last letter of the name
  • little → WEE wee is the Scots word for little, and standard crossword shorthand
  • Bond → TIE a bond between two people is a tie
  • What’s behind — What is behind a word is its back end, so only the final letter of the name is taken.
  • cuddling — A cuddle wraps arms right round someone, so these letters hold the short word between them.

'James Bond' is one name on the surface and two separate jobs in the wordplay, which is why the 007 reading is so hard to put down.

26 across TETRA

Arsenal boss abruptly flipping fish (5)

Definition: fish · deletion + reversal

A tetra is a small, brightly coloured freshwater fish from South America, the kind sold for home aquariums.

  • Arsenal boss → ARTETA Mikel Arteta, manager of Arsenal, the north London football club
  • abruptly → the last letter is docked before anything is turned round
  • abruptly — Something done abruptly is cut short before its proper end, so the final letter never arrives.
  • flipping — Flipping a thing over presents it the other way round, so the letters are read in the opposite direction.

Football managers date a puzzle — this clue works only while that name is current. 'Flipping' is mild swearing on the surface and an instruction underneath.

27 across MEANDERED

Fix bit of Elastoplast to cover a bloody wound (9)

Definition: wound · container + charade + first letter

Here wound is the past of wind, not a gash: a river that meandered wound through the fields, so the verbs match exactly.

  • Fix → MEND to fix a broken thing is to mend it
  • a → A taken straight from the clue
  • bit of Elastoplast → E Elastoplast is the British sticking plaster, the equivalent of a Band-Aid, and only its front letter is wanted
  • bloody → RED blood is red, so 'bloody' is the colour
  • bit of — A bit of a word is a piece of it, and the piece a setter means by that is its opening letter.
  • to cover — To cover something is to lie over it, so what follows is tucked inside the letters already assembled.

'Bloody wound' is the trap. It reads as an injury, and the definition is 'wound' alone — the past tense of wind. The first-aid surface exists to sell that.

30 across EMOTIONAL

Intense Germany escaping relegation before the end of Europa League (9)

Definition: Intense · deletion + charade + last letter

An emotional scene is one charged with strong feeling, so the adjective covers the same ground whenever the intensity is a matter of feelings.

  • Germany → D, for Deutschland, is Germany's international vehicle code, and that is the letter getting away
  • relegation → EMOTION relegation is demotion, one letter lighter here
  • the end of Europa → A the last letter of the word
  • League → L L is the standard abbreviation for league
  • escaping — What escapes gets out, so the letter it names leaves the word it was sitting in.
  • the end of — The end of a word is its final letter, and that is all that is taken from the one following.
  • before — Before means in front, so the long piece is written ahead of the two short ones.

The football surface is seamless — relegation, a country escaping, the Europa League — and every word of it is wordplay. 'Intense', at the front, is the only plain one.

31 across ADOPT

Take in song and dance part (5)

Definition: Take in · charade

To adopt a child is to take them in permanently and make them part of the family, so the phrasal verb matches sense for sense.

  • song and dance → ADO to make a song and dance about something is to make a fuss, and ado is the crossword's fuss
  • part → PT pt is the standard abbreviation for part

'Song and dance' is an idiom for a fuss rather than a stage turn, and the theatrical reading is what the surface is banking on throughout.

Down

1 down FLAT

Monotonous place to live (4)

Definition: Monotonous · double definition

A monotonous voice is a flat one, and a British apartment is a flat — one spelling covering an adjective and a noun with nothing in common.

  • Monotonous → the sense of a voice or a tune with no rise or fall in it
  • place to live → the British word for an apartment, one floor of a larger building

Two everyday senses of one short word, laid side by side with nothing between them. Three words of clue is usually the sign that no wordplay is coming.

2 down ABSENTEE

A bin originally delivered Eddie – gutted he’s not here (8)

Definition: he’s not here · charade + first letter + outer letters

An absentee is the person who has failed to turn up, so the setter defines them by the gap they leave rather than by naming them.

  • A → A the clue's own first word, taken at face value
  • bin → B the opening letter of the word
  • delivered → SENT a parcel delivered is a parcel sent
  • Eddie → EE the name emptied out, its middle three letters gone
  • originally — A thing's origin is where it began, so only the opening letter of the word beside it is wanted.
  • gutted — To gut a fish is to take out everything inside it, leaving the two ends of the name standing.

Eddie is not just surface: the unchecked letters of the top and bottom rows spell FAREWELL EDDIE BOY, and dogs run through the whole puzzle. The dash hides the join between name and instruction.

3 down RANT

President blowing his top in Stormy diatribe (4)

Definition: diatribe · deletion

A diatribe is a bitter, sustained verbal attack delivered at length — the same outburst, in a more formal register.

  • President → GRANT Ulysses S. Grant, the Union general who became the eighteenth US president
  • Stormy → surface only: the capital letter points at a recent White House scandal and hands over no letters
  • blowing his top — Blowing your top is losing your temper, and here the top of a down entry is literally its first letter, blown off.

'President' and 'Stormy' together conjure one particular administration, which is the whole trick. The president you want is the one on the fifty-dollar bill.

4 down EYE TEETH

Alien stops to watch the angry canines (3,5)

Definition: canines · charade + anagram + container

The eyeteeth are the pointed upper canine teeth, so named because they sit directly below the eyes — an exact anatomical synonym.

  • to watch → EYE to eye something is to watch it closely
  • the → TEH the clue's own three letters, shaken out of order
  • Alien → ET ET is the crossword's standard alien, after the film
  • stops — In British usage to stop somewhere is to stay there, so the alien lodges among the other letters instead of pausing.
  • angry — Anger throws things into disorder, and letters described as angry will not stay in the order they were handed over.

'Canines' sets you hunting for dogs, in a grid that is full of them. They are teeth, and the surface's alien pausing at a dog fight is pure decoration.

5 down WRENCH

Architect on top of church reveals tool (6)

Definition: tool · charade

A wrench is the spanner for gripping and turning nuts, the plain American name for a tool in any British toolbox too.

  • Architect → WREN Sir Christopher Wren, who designed St Paul's Cathedral
  • church → CH ch. is the standard abbreviation for church
  • on top of — A down entry is read from the top, so a piece placed on top of another is written first.

Wren is the crossword's default architect, and the surface — a man standing on a church roof — draws exactly the stack the down entry asks for.

6 down ESPADRILLE

Mongrel pees over a training shoe (10)

Definition: shoe · anagram + container

An espadrille is a summer shoe with a canvas upper and a rope sole, so the bare category word defines it by kind.

  • pees → ESPE these four letters resettled, then split to make the outside
  • a → A taken straight from the clue
  • training → DRILL drill = training, as in square-bashing or a fire drill
  • Mongrel — A mongrel is a dog of no fixed breed, a mixture of several — and letters described as mixed do not keep their order.
  • over — Something laid over another thing covers it, so these letters wrap right round the pieces named next.

'Training shoe' is the trap: it reads as one thing, a trainer, and breaks in half. The training is the drill and the shoe alone is the definition.

7 down LIE LOW

Keep doggo in mattress did you say? (3,3)

Definition: Keep doggo · homophone

To keep doggo is British slang for staying hidden and perfectly still until trouble passes, which is what the phrase means.

  • mattress → LIELOW a Li-Lo is the British inflatable air mattress you take camping, and its two syllables are heard as two words
  • did you say? — The question asks how a word sounds rather than how it is spelt, so what you want is heard and not read.

Say the mattress brand out loud and it falls apart into a phrase about hiding. 'Doggo' is old army slang, and in this grid it is also one more dog.

8 down LEAD

Guide dog’s accessory (4)

Definition: Guide · double definition

To guide is to lead someone along, and the strap on a collar is a lead in British English: one spelling doing a verb and a noun.

  • Guide → the verb: to show someone the way by going in front of them
  • dog’s accessory → the strap you clip to a collar for a walk; Americans call it a leash

The split falls inside 'guide dog', which is why it is so hard to see — the two definitions overlap in the one phrase every reader knows.

13 down POSIT

Revolutionary surgery on model is put forward (5)

Definition: put forward · reversal + charade

To posit something is to put it forward as a proposition for discussion — the verb a philosopher uses for advancing a claim.

  • surgery → PO an op is surgery, turned about
  • model → SIT to model for a painter is to sit for them
  • Revolutionary — A revolution turns a thing full circle, so the short word beside it is read the other way round.
  • on — In a down entry the piece sitting on another is written above it, so it starts the answer.

'Revolutionary' looks political next to surgery and models, and is only an instruction to turn two letters round. No politics anywhere in the clue.

14 down ACETIC ACID

Hermit rejecting society’s help to collar canine that’s in a pickle (6,4)

Definition: that’s in a pickle · charade + deletion + container

Pickling vinegar is a dilute solution of acetic acid, so the thing literally sitting in a pickle jar is this acid.

  • Hermit → ASCETIC an ascetic lives alone and denies himself comfort, as a hermit does
  • society → S is the standard abbreviation for society, and that is the letter thrown out
  • help → AID help = aid, as noun or verb
  • canine → C the first letter of the word, which the setter takes without an explicit signal — dentists write C for a canine tooth too
  • rejecting — To reject something is to throw it out, so the letter named beside it is discarded from the word.
  • to collar — To collar someone is to seize them round the neck, so these letters close around the one that follows.

'In a pickle' reads as being in trouble and is meant literally: this is what pickling vinegar is made of. Collaring a canine belongs to the dog theme, not the chemistry.

16 down NURSE

Climbing ladder on slide without stopping to get treat (5)

Definition: treat · charade + reversal + deletion

To nurse a cold or an injury is to treat and tend it, the everyday verb for looking after someone who is ill.

  • ladder → NUR in Britain a ladder in a stocking is what Americans call a run, taken upwards here
  • stopping → a stopping is a filling — a dentist's, or the plug in a bottle — and it is the LID in the middle
  • slide → SE SLIDE keeps only its two ends
  • Climbing — Climbing goes up the page, and in a down entry that means the letters are collected from the bottom end first.
  • on — In a down entry one piece sits on another, so this one is written above the next.
  • without — Without means lacking, so whatever is named after it is taken out of the word in front.

Two British senses do all the work: a ladder is a run in a stocking, nothing you climb, and a stopping is a filling rather than a halt.

19 down MISSABLE

Clever girl swapping places is difficult to spot (8)

Definition: difficult to spot · charade

Something missable is easy to overlook, so it is by definition hard to spot — the adjective is built straight from the verb miss.

  • girl → MISS a miss is a young unmarried woman
  • Clever → ABLE able = clever, capable
  • swapping places — Two things that swap places exchange positions, so the pieces go in the reverse of the order the clue names them.

The two halves are clued the wrong way round on purpose, which is what lets 'clever girl' read so naturally.

21 down WATERLOO

Single defeat is terminal (8)

Definition: defeat · double definition

The 1815 battle gave English 'meet your Waterloo' for a final defeat, and the London station is a terminal in the railway sense.

  • Single → a third definition: ABBA's Eurovision winner of 1974, released on a 45
  • defeat → Napoleon's last battle, in 1815, and ever since the byword for anyone's final rout
  • terminal → one of London's great railway termini, on the south bank of the Thames

Three definitions of one word and no wordplay at all: a pop record, a battle and a station. 'Is' is there only to hold the three together.

23 down RETROD

Old school Head of Dance performed steps again (6)

Definition: performed steps again · charade + first letter

To tread is to step, so the past tense with 're-' in front says the steps were gone over a second time.

  • Old school → RETRO retro means deliberately old-fashioned, in the style of an earlier decade
  • Dance → D the initial of the capitalised word
  • Head of — The head of a word is the letter at its front, so only the initial of the name that follows is used.

'Old school' reads as a place in the surface and is an adjective in the wordplay. The capital letter on Dance is the only signal that a word is about to be cut down.

24 down RIMINI

Brit stripped car in Italian city (6)

Definition: Italian city · charade + middle letters

Rimini is a resort city on Italy's Adriatic coast, famous for its beaches and as Fellini's birthplace.

  • Brit → RI the middle of the word, once the letters at each end have gone
  • car → MINI the Mini, the small British car of the 1960s
  • stripped — To strip a thing is to take its outer covering off, leaving only what was inside the word.

The whole surface is a package holiday and it hides nothing. What makes it hard is that both halves of the wordplay look like scene-setting.

26 down TREE

Shirt is OTT for Robert Plant (4)

Definition: Plant · container

A tree is the largest kind of plant, a woody perennial — the capital letters only make it look like the Led Zeppelin singer.

  • Shirt → TEE a tee, or T-shirt, is a shirt
  • Robert → R the first letter of the name, as in R. Plant on a record label
  • OTT — OTT is 'over the top': the shirt goes over, and the top is the front letter of the name that follows.

The clue is one rock star's name and both halves of it are cryptic: the shirt supplies letters and Plant is the definition, capital and all.

28 down DRAB

Boring poet making comeback (4)

Definition: Boring · reversal

Drab describes something dull and colourless, which is the visual sense of boring rather than the tedious-conversation one.

  • poet → BARD a bard is a poet, Shakespeare being the Bard
  • making comeback — A comeback is a return the way you came, so the letters are taken in the opposite order.

'Making comeback' does double duty: a poet enjoying a revival, and the instruction to read the letters the other way. Both senses sit in the one phrase.

29 down DUTY

Henry Tudor raised housing tax (4)

Definition: tax · hidden word + reversal

A duty is a tax levied on goods — customs duty, stamp duty — the fiscal sense rather than the sense of obligation.

  • Henry Tudor → YTUD four consecutive letters straddling the join between the two names
  • raised — Raising a thing lifts it, and in a down entry that means reading the run of letters from the bottom up.
  • housing — To house something is to give it a home inside, so the letters are already sitting in the clue rather than being clued.

Henry Tudor is Henry VII, and a name that specific is exactly what stops you looking at the letters themselves. The span crosses both words and runs up the entry.
