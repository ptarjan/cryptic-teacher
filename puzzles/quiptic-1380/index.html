Across

4 across WRENCH

Damsel embracing another, ultimately a hard thing to bear (6)

Definition: a hard thing to bear · container + last letter

A wrench is the pang of a painful separation — 'leaving was a wrench' — which is exactly something hard to bear.

  • Damsel → WENCH a wench is an old word for a girl or serving-woman
  • another, ultimately → R the last letter of anotheR
  • embracing — To embrace is to fold your arms right round somebody, so the word doing the embracing closes over the letter named next.
  • ultimately — Whatever comes ultimately in a word is its final letter, taken here from the word standing just before it.

Two young women in an embrace, with something painful said to be waiting for them.

'Bear' is the pivot: it means endure rather than carry, so the answer is the pang of a hard parting and not the tool in the toolbox.

6 across BED-QUILT

Teaching degree? Resign about student coverage at night (3-5)

Definition: coverage at night · charade + container

A bed-quilt is the padded cover spread over a bed, so it is what does the covering when you are asleep.

  • Teaching degree? → BED a BEd is a Bachelor of Education, the usual British teaching qualification
  • Resign → QUIT to quit a post is to resign from it
  • student → L L is the learner plate a British driver displays, and crosswords use it for any student
  • about — 'About' in a clue means round the outside, so the word before it opens up and takes the piece named after it inside.

A teacher walks out over a student, and the conversation turns to what happens after dark.

The question mark flags 'Teaching degree' as one example among several, which is the setter admitting that the letters come from a particular qualification.

9 across TROPIC

Discussion item admitting river forms line on map (6)

Definition: line on map · container

A tropic is one of the two latitude circles ruled across an atlas page, so it is literally a line drawn on a map.

  • Discussion item → TOPIC a topic is an item up for discussion
  • river → R R is the map-maker's abbreviation for river
  • admitting — To admit a visitor is to let them in, so the word doing the admitting takes the next letter inside itself.

An agenda point that lets a river into the conversation.

Cancer and Capricorn are the two of them printed across every globe, which is what makes a singular definition fair here.

10 across LOCALITY

Pub and brewery finally introducing it in the area (8)

Definition: the area · charade + container + last letter

A locality is a district or neighbourhood — the slightly formal word estate agents and police reports keep alive for the area around you.

  • Pub → LOCAL your local is the neighbourhood pub in Britain
  • it → IT used literally, the word's own two letters
  • brewery finally → Y the last letter of brewery
  • finally — What comes finally in a word is its closing letter, and that single character is all the preceding word contributes.
  • introducing — Introducing something means bringing it inside, so the two letters named next are planted within the pieces already assembled.

A pub and the brewery behind it bring something new to the neighbourhood.

'The local' is what a British drinker calls their nearest pub, and the clue needs you to hear that noun rather than the adjective.

11 across DEMOGRAPHIC

Protest followed by explicit part of the population (11)

Definition: part of the population · charade

A demographic is a segment of a population sharing some characteristic, the sense advertisers use when they talk about reaching one.

  • Protest → DEMO a demo is a demonstration, a public protest
  • explicit → GRAPHIC graphic detail is explicit detail
  • followed by — Saying one thing is followed by another fixes their order, so the piece named first is written down first.

A march through the streets, and then something described in unsparing detail.

'Explicit' is aiming you at rude pictures, and the answer is the flat marketing noun for a slice of people picked out by age or income.

15 across ANTONYM

Opposite of Caesar, J? (7)

Definition: Opposite · charade

An antonym is a word of contrary meaning — hot to cold — so the noun 'Opposite' is its everyday equivalent.

  • Caesar → ANTONY Mark Antony was Julius Caesar's ally and then the man set against his heirs
  • J? → M the clue writes Caesar as a register would, surname then initial, so the rival gets his initial too — M for Mark

A history quiz question: who stood on the other side from Julius Caesar?

'Caesar, J' is the surname-then-initial form the joke depends on: his opponent set down the same way is Antony, M, which is also an ordinary English word.

17 across BARGAIN

Reader initially gripped by book once more – it’s a good buy (7)

Definition: a good buy · container

A bargain is something bought for less than it is worth, so a good buy names it from the buyer's side of the counter.

  • book → B b is the standard abbreviation for book
  • Reader initially → R the first letter of Reader
  • once more → AGAIN once more = again
  • initially — A word taken initially yields the letter it opens with, so one character comes from the noun before it.
  • gripped by — To be gripped is to be held fast inside something's grasp, so that letter lands within the pieces named afterwards.

Someone reading is caught up in a book for a second time, and it was cheap at the price.

The insertion lands inside a two-piece assembly rather than inside one word, which is why the seam is so hard to see; the dash marks where the definition starts.

18 across SINGAPOREAN

Person again troubled resident of city-state (11)

Definition: resident of city-state · anagram

Singapore is a city that is also a sovereign country, so one of its nationals is exactly a resident of a city-state.

  • Person again → PERSONAGAIN anagram fodder, eleven letters
  • troubled — Someone troubled is unsettled, and unsettled letters do not stay in the order the clue handed them over in.

Somebody is bothered all over again.

'Again' is passing itself off as an ordinary adverb, which is what keeps the fodder from looking like fodder — those five letters are working, not commenting.

22 across MELODIST

Song-writer seldom getting confused with it (8)

Definition: Song-writer · anagram

A melodist is a writer of melodies, the tunes that carry songs, so the job title and the definition name the same trade.

  • seldom → SELDOM anagram fodder, six letters
  • it → IT two more letters joined to the fodder
  • getting confused — Something confused is muddled up, and muddled letters settle into an order nobody chose.

A composer who is hardly ever mistaken for anything else.

'Getting confused with it' reads as praise for a distinctive musician, and it is the instruction plus the last two letters it works on.

23 across JOSHUA

Religious writer to ridicule leaders of Unitarian Adventists (6)

Definition: Religious writer · charade + first letters

Joshua is a book of the Hebrew Bible traditionally credited to the man it is named after, so the definition works by that attribution.

  • ridicule → JOSH to josh somebody is to tease or make fun of them
  • leaders of Unitarian Adventists → UA the first letters of those two words
  • leaders of — The leader of a word is the letter at its head, so the fronts of the two names that follow are what get used.

A religious author pokes fun at the heads of two church groups.

The Old Testament book carries his name, and by the old convention that a book's title names its author he counts as its writer.

24 across OVERVIEW

Completed challenge with summary (8)

Definition: summary · charade

An overview is a broad account with the detail left out, which is what a summary gives you — the whole thing seen from above.

  • Completed → OVER when something is over it is finished
  • challenge → VIE to vie is to compete, to challenge for something
  • with → W w/ is the standard shorthand for with

A challenge has been finished, and written up in brief.

The smoothest word in the sentence is the one carrying a letter: 'with' looks like joinery holding two halves together and is a piece of the build.

25 across BROWSE

Items often furrowed before end of intense look over the shelves? (6)

Definition: look over the shelves · charade + last letter

To browse is to scan goods or books at leisure with nothing particular in mind, and drifting along a shelf is the everyday instance of it.

  • Items often furrowed → BROWS brows are what you furrow when you frown
  • end of intense → E the last letter of intense
  • before — Standing before something means coming in front of it, which fixes which of the two pieces is written first.
  • end of — The end of a word is its closing letter, and that single character is all this takes from the word after it.

A deep frown, held right up to the end of an intense stare.

The question mark admits that shelves are only one place you can do this, which is what lets a whole phrase stand as the definition.

Down

1 down ACTING

Account with money? Grand, though not really in the post (6)

Definition: not really in the post · charade

Someone acting head or acting captain fills the post only for now, deputising rather than truly holding it.

  • Account → AC ac is the standing abbreviation for an account, as on a bank statement
  • money → TIN tin is old British slang for cash
  • Grand → G a grand is a thousand pounds, shortened to its first letter

A brisk tot-up of somebody's finances, with a payment apparently still sitting in the mail.

'In the post' reads as the mail and means holding a job, and 'not really' is the word that turns the job into a temporary stand-in.

2 down XENOPHOBIA

Vandalised phone-box: one’s beginning to anticipate anti-foreigner sentiment (10)

Definition: anti-foreigner sentiment · anagram + first letter

Xenophobia is Greek for fear of the stranger, and English uses it for settled hostility towards foreigners rather than for any private phobia.

  • phone-box → PHONEBOX anagram fodder, eight letters; the hyphen counts for nothing
  • one’s → I one written as the single figure I, the way a lone person is
  • beginning to anticipate → A the first letter of anticipate
  • Vandalised — Vandals wreck what they get hold of, and a wrecked word keeps none of its letters where the setter put them.
  • beginning to — What stands at the beginning of a word is its opening letter, so just one character comes from the word that follows.

A smashed-up phone box, and somebody starting to see what is coming next.

Hearing 'one's' as the letter I is what makes the arithmetic work: the phone-box supplies eight letters and the sentiment needs ten.

3 down SQUAD CAR

Police vehicle: evidence of damage seen around one in four? (5,3)

Definition: Police vehicle · container

A squad car is the patrol car a police squad rides in, so the phrase names it by the job it does.

  • evidence of damage seen → SCAR a scar is the mark left where damage can still be seen
  • one in four? → QUAD a quad is one of four children born together
  • around — Going around something means passing outside it on both sides, so the word named first splits open and encloses the next one.

A battered motor is looked over, as though damage showed on one vehicle in every four.

The colon is doing the work of an equals sign, and 'one in four' is a person rather than a proportion — quads arrive four at a time.

4 down WITHDRAW

Retract intelligence on origin of huge lottery (8)

Definition: Retract · charade + first letter

To withdraw a remark is to retract it, take it back — the verb of statements rather than of money or troops.

  • intelligence → WIT wit is quickness of mind, intelligence
  • origin of huge → H the first letter of huge
  • lottery → DRAW a lottery is a draw, the pulling out of winning numbers
  • origin of — The origin of a word is the point it starts from, so only the opening letter of what comes next is used.

Secret intelligence and an enormous prize draw, with somebody told to take it all back.

In a down clue 'on' means sitting on top of, so the pieces stack down the column in the order the clue names them.

5 down ENORMITY

Minor yet somehow a great crime (8)

Definition: a great crime · anagram

Enormity is an outrage or atrocity — moral monstrousness rather than mere hugeness — so a great crime is its strict sense.

  • Minor yet → MINORYET anagram fodder, eight letters
  • somehow — The word admits the route from one to the other is unstated, and letters that travel by no stated route arrive in a fresh order.

An offence being brushed aside as small when it was anything but.

Enormity is the most misused word in the language: it means monstrous wickedness, not great size, and 'Minor' is teasing that very confusion.

7 down IRIS

Flag of European country getting trimmed (4)

Definition: Flag · deletion

Flag is the common English name of the wild iris, so the definition is one plant name standing in for another.

  • of European country → IRISH belonging to Ireland = Irish
  • getting trimmed → IRIS that word loses its final H
  • getting trimmed — Trimming takes a bit off the end of something, so the last letter is snipped from the word before it.

A national flag described as being cut down to size.

Flag is a genuine plant name — the yellow flag that grows at the water's edge is a wild iris — so a flower is being defined by a flower's other name.

8 down TOYS

Component of extravagant oyster trifles (4)

Definition: trifles · hidden word

Trifles are things of no importance, and toys carries that dismissive sense — a plaything, something nobody need take seriously.

  • extravagant oyster → TOYS the end of the first word runs straight on into the start of the second, with no break in the letters
  • Component of — A component sits inside the thing it belongs to, which is where these letters are — already lying in the words that follow.

The ingredient list for a lavish dessert, oysters and all.

'Trifles' is doing the defining as a noun meaning things of no consequence, while the surface has you tasting pudding.

12 down ADMINISTER

Run a diocese, at first followed by member of clergy (10)

Definition: Run · charade + first letter

To administer a department is to run it, handling its day-to-day management — the transitive verb matching 'Run' in the same voice.

  • a → A used literally, the word's own letter
  • diocese, at first → D the first letter of diocese
  • member of clergy → MINISTER a minister is an ordained member of the clergy
  • at first — Taken at its first, a word gives up only the letter it starts with and nothing else.
  • followed by — The phrase puts the pieces in sequence, so whatever it introduces is written after what came before it.

Running a diocese, with a clergyman apparently trailing along behind.

'Run' looks like part of the church business the surface describes, and it is the definition: manage, not move quickly.

13 down GAME SHOW

Was home, excited to support good TV programme? (4,4)

Definition: TV programme · anagram + charade

A game show is a television format built round contestants competing for prizes, so it is one kind of TV programme rather than a synonym for the phrase.

  • good → G G is the standard abbreviation for good, as on a school report
  • Was home, excited → AMESHOW the seven letters of WAS HOME in a new order
  • excited — An excited crowd will not stand in the order it was put in, and neither will these letters.
  • to support — In a down clue, supporting something means standing underneath it, so this batch goes below the letter already named.

Somebody was in and thrilled about it, and the sentence ends up recommending an evening's viewing.

The question mark marks the definition as one example of the category rather than the whole of it, which is the setter being honest about a loose fit.

14 down INUNDATE

Popular time to go round a French swamp (8)

Definition: swamp · charade + container

To inundate is to flood or overwhelm, and swamp as a verb means precisely that — swamped with letters, inundated with them.

  • Popular → IN what is popular is in, in fashion
  • a French → UN un is the French for a
  • time → DATE a date is a fixed time in the calendar
  • to go round — Going round something means passing outside it, so the pieces already assembled close over the word named next.

A fashionable season for touring a French marsh.

French words are fair game in British cryptics, and 'a French' is the standard way of asking for one; the definition is the verb, to flood.

16 down NOSEDIVE

Decline instantly: one’s worried over dodgy bar (8)

Definition: Decline instantly · anagram + charade

To nosedive is to plunge suddenly, the aircraft's nose-first drop borrowed for prices and reputations, so the decline is instant by definition.

  • one’s worried → NOSE the four letters of ONE'S in a new order
  • dodgy bar → DIVE a dive is a seedy drinking den
  • worried — A dog worries a bone by shaking it about, and letters shaken about come out in a different arrangement.
  • over — In a down clue, over means higher up the column, so the piece named before it is entered above the other.

Somebody turns an offer down flat, anxious about a shabby drinking den.

'Decline' pulls hard towards refusing an invitation and means falling steeply here, which is the whole of the misdirection.

19 down ODOURS

Party, upsetting for us, stinks (6)

Definition: stinks · reversal + charade

Stinks as a plural noun means bad smells, and odours is the neutral word for the same things, doing duty for the rude one.

  • Party, upsetting → OD a do is a party, entered the other way up
  • for us → OURS what belongs to us is ours
  • upsetting — To upset a thing is to tip it over, and a word tipped over in a down clue is read back up the column.

A party that has gone badly wrong for the people throwing it.

'Do' for a party is standard crossword vocabulary — a works do, a birthday do — and 'upsetting' is positional here rather than emotional.

20 down AMMO

Material to support attack in backgammon (4)

Definition: Material to support attack · hidden word

Ammo is the clipped everyday form of ammunition, the material an attack is supplied with, and it carries the figurative sense too.

  • backgammon → AMMO four consecutive letters sit in the word, starting from its fifth
  • in — The smallest of signposts: it says the letters are inside the word that follows, not made from it.

A board game, and the supplies an attacking player needs.

Backgammon is doing double duty — a game in the surface, a hiding place in the wordplay — and that one-letter 'in' is the only signpost you get.

21 down GLUE

Limits to general use adhesive (4)

Definition: adhesive · outer letters

Adhesive is the class name for anything that sticks things together, and glue is the everyday member of that class.

  • general → GL the two edges of g-enera-l
  • use → UE the two edges of u-s-e
  • Limits to — The limits of a word are its two edges, so the opening and closing letters of each word named next are taken.

Restrictions written into what everybody is allowed to use.

'Limits to general use' reads as small print on a licence, and that entirely plausible sentence is what hides an instruction inside it.
