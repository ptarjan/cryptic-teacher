1 across TRAFALGAR SQUARE

Wind from the east Portuguese region wiping out half of five hundred, say, in city centre (9,6)

Definition: city centre · charade + reversal + deletion

Trafalgar Square is the point London's road distances are measured from, so it answers 'city centre' as one capital's official middle.

  • Wind → TRAF Wind in the bodily sense is a FART, and it arrives here facing the other way.
  • Portuguese region → ALGAR Portugal's sunny southern province is the ALGARVE, turning up two letters lighter.
  • hundred → SQUARE A hundred is ten times ten, which makes it one example of a square number.
  • from the east — Something coming from the east is travelling west, and on an across line that means its letters are read from the far end back.
  • wiping out — Wiping out is erasing, so whatever is named next is rubbed off the word in front of it.
  • half of five — Cut the word 'five' down the middle and the back half, VE, is the pair that gets rubbed off.
  • say — 'Say' is the setter's 'for example', so the number quoted is only one instance of the class being described.

An easterly gale over a Portuguese holiday coast while somebody scrubs half of five hundred off a total.

'Wind' is the bodily sort rather than the weather, and 'hundred, say' asks not for the number but for the class it belongs to: ten times ten.

9 across UNCHAIN

Loveless workers' association introducing tea for free (7)

Definition: free · container + deletion

To unchain something is to release it from its fetters, which is 'free' in its plainest verb sense.

  • workers' association → UNIN A workers' association is a UNION, here with its O gone and a gap left in the middle.
  • tea → CHA Cha is tea, a word English took from Chinese and kept in 'a nice cup of cha'.
  • Loveless — Love is the tennis word for a nought, so something loveless has lost its O.
  • introducing — Introducing one thing to another puts it in among them, so the short word lands inside the long one.

A trade union without a shred of affection in it, handing out free tea.

'Loveless' reads as a description of a hard-hearted union and is really an instruction to drop a letter. Love for nought comes from tennis scoring.

10 across STERILE

Tireless criminal abandons a son for being unproductive (7)

Definition: unproductive · anagram + deletion

Sterile ground grows nothing and a sterile discussion produces nothing, so 'unproductive' is the sense the two share.

  • Tireless → TIRELESS The word itself is the raw material, eight letters for a seven-letter answer.
  • a son → S S is the genealogist's abbreviation for son, and one of the two esses walks out.
  • criminal — Something criminal is bent out of its proper shape, so the letters beside it come out in a different order.
  • abandons — To abandon something is to walk away and leave it behind, so the letter named is left out.

A crook who never stops working walking out on his own boy.

The letter count does the telling: eight letters clued for a seven-letter answer means something has to go before any shuffling starts.

11 across OMAHA

After retirement I see a way of working in US city (5)

Definition: US city · charade + reversal

Omaha is the biggest city in Nebraska, so 'US city' defines it by example rather than by anything peculiar to it.

  • I see → AH 'Ah!' is the noise you make when the penny drops.
  • a → A The article is taken as the single letter it is.
  • way of working → MO A criminal's MO is their modus operandi, their way of working.
  • After retirement — Retiring is going back the way you came, so the assembled letters are read from the far end.

A pensioner explaining that they have finally grasped how the job gets done.

'I see' is not a claim about eyesight but the noise of understanding, and the surface's talk of retirement is the only word doing mechanical work.

12 across ALDERSHOT

Try to follow lunar astronaut circling earth who's missing in Hampshire town (9)

Definition: Hampshire town · charade + container + deletion

Aldershot is the Hampshire garrison town that calls itself the home of the British Army.

  • lunar astronaut → ALDR Buzz Aldrin was the second man on the Moon; his surname turns up without the letters the clue says are missing.
  • earth → E E is the electrician's abbreviation for earth, the wire that runs to the ground.
  • in → IN These are the two letters the astronaut's name is missing.
  • Try → SHOT A shot at something is a try at it, as in 'give it a shot'.
  • to follow — What follows comes after, so the piece clued first ends up at the back of the answer.
  • circling — Circling something means going round it, so one group of letters closes round a single one.
  • who's missing — The pronoun points back at the astronaut, and what he is missing is the two letters named next.

An astronaut in lunar orbit circling the Earth, with somebody unaccounted for down below.

The word 'in' does double duty: it reads as part of 'missing in Hampshire' and is really the pair of letters taken out of the astronaut's name.

13 across SEMICOLON

House dependency, conservatory, in the end leaves a mark (9)

Definition: a mark · charade + deletion + last letter

A semicolon is a punctuation mark, so 'a mark' names the family it belongs to rather than the thing itself.

  • House → SEMI A semi is Britain's semi-detached house, one of a pair sharing a wall.
  • dependency → COLON A dependency is a COLONY, a territory run from elsewhere, and it turns up a letter short.
  • conservatory → Y The last letter of 'conservatory' is the one that departs.
  • in the end — 'In the end' points at the final letter of the word beside it rather than at the word itself.
  • leaves — To leave is to depart, so the letter just identified walks out of the second piece.

An estate agent listing a house, its granny flat and finally its conservatory.

COLONY and 'conservatory' end in the same letter, which is what lets one word name the letter another has to lose.

15 across EMOJI

Sign Keir's opponent is losing her head when drinking orange juice (5)

Definition: Sign · container + deletion

An emoji is a little picture standing for a feeling or a thing, which is a sign in the plain sense of a symbol.

  • Keir's opponent → EMI Keir Starmer's opposite number across the despatch box is Kemi Badenoch, the Conservative leader, and her first name goes headless.
  • orange juice → OJ OJ is the breakfast-menu shorthand for orange juice.
  • losing her head — A head is the top or front of a thing, so the name loses its first letter.
  • drinking — Someone drinking takes the liquid inside them, so those letters go within.

The leader of the opposition losing her composure over a glass of breakfast juice.

Britain's two main party leaders face each other weekly at Prime Minister's Questions, so 'Keir's opponent' is a particular person rather than a general one.

16 across LAPSE

Wrongly ordered energy drinks, a mistake (5)

Definition: a mistake · anagram

A lapse is a slip or small failure of attention, so 'a mistake' is the everyday synonym.

  • energy → E E is physics' symbol for energy, as in Einstein's equation.
  • drinks → LAPS A cat laps its milk, so a word for drinking hands over four letters.
  • Wrongly ordered — Something wrongly ordered has its parts in the wrong sequence, so the letters need putting back into the right one.

Somebody has put in the wrong order for the energy drinks, which is a slip.

'Energy drinks' reads as one thing off a supermarket shelf; the setter uses the two words separately, and 'drinks' is the verb, not the noun.

18 across INSIDE JOB

Criminal activity in team work (6,3)

Definition: Criminal activity · charade

An inside job is a crime committed by someone within the organisation robbed, so it is one particular kind of criminal activity.

  • in → IN The preposition is taken at face value, two letters as they stand.
  • team → SIDE A side is a team in British sport — the first team is the first side.
  • work → JOB A job is a piece of work.

Something shady going on within the team at work.

'In team work' reads as one phrase about co-operation and is three separate pieces laid end to end, with 'in' meaning nothing beyond its own two letters.

20 across UNASHAMED

A mixture of sad and humane but not embarrassed (9)

Definition: not embarrassed · anagram

Unashamed means feeling no shame about something, so 'not embarrassed' catches it with the negative spelled out.

  • sad → SAD Three of the nine letters, going in as they stand.
  • humane → HUMANE The other six letters, which with the first three make exactly the enumeration.
  • A mixture of — A mixture is what you get when things are stirred together and lose the order they arrived in.

Someone both miserable and kind, and not the least bit awkward about it.

The two fodder words are a pair of character traits, which is why they read as description rather than as nine letters waiting to be stirred.

23 across RASTA

Opera stars covering Bob Marley? (5)

Definition: Bob Marley · hidden word

Bob Marley was the most famous Rastafari of all, so he defines the answer by example rather than by meaning.

  • Opera stars → RASTA Five consecutive letters straddle the join between these two words, starting three letters in.
  • covering — To cover something is to lie over it and keep it out of sight, so the letters are concealed by the words carrying them.

Opera singers having a go at Bob Marley's back catalogue.

Covering is what one band does to another band's song, which is the surface joke; the question mark admits that one man stands for a whole faith.

24 across RINGLET

Right around fireplace is a butterfly (7)

Definition: a butterfly · container

The ringlet is a dark brown European butterfly named for the small eye-rings on its wings, so the definition is by example.

  • Right → RT Rt is the abbreviation used in titles such as Rt Hon, the Right Honourable.
  • fireplace → INGLE An ingle is an old word for a fireplace, the one still standing in 'inglenook'.
  • around — Going around something means enclosing it, so the short piece opens up and the other lands inside.

Something turning about the hearth turns out to be a butterfly.

'Right' looks like the single R that crosswords use constantly for compass points and politics, and here it is the two-letter Rt of Rt Hon instead.

25 across SCHLOCK

Inferior school shut (7)

Definition: Inferior · charade

Schlock is cheap trashy stuff, and the word doubles as an adjective — a schlock film is an inferior one.

  • school → SCH Sch is the standard abbreviation for school, the one on signs and timetables.
  • shut → LOCK To lock a door is to shut it fast.

A failing school closing its gates for good.

'Inferior school shut' carries no instruction word at all, so the join between definition and wordplay has to be found by counting letters.

26 across CARBON FOOTPRINT

Anarchic contra not pro FBI's measure of green policy (6,9)

Definition: measure of green policy · anagram

A carbon footprint is the tonnage of greenhouse gas an activity causes, which is the number green policy is measured against.

  • contra not pro FBI → CONTRANOTPROFBI Fifteen letters, exactly the enumeration, going into the mix as they stand.
  • Anarchic — Anarchy is the absence of any order at all, so the letters beside it have none either.

Anarchists lining up against the FBI rather than for it.

'Contra not pro' says the same thing twice over, which is what makes it read as political sloganeering rather than as a heap of letters.

1 down TOULOUSE-LAUTREC

With added height, being short, this artist would represent three cities (8-7)

Definition: this artist · charade + deletion

Henri de Toulouse-Lautrec is the Montmartre painter of the Moulin Rouge posters, so 'this artist' points at one particular man.

  • three cities → TOULOUSE The first is the pink-brick city on the Garonne in southern France.
  • three cities → LA The second is Los Angeles, which everybody shortens to its two initials.
  • three cities → UTREC The third is Utrecht in the Netherlands, arriving without its last two letters.
  • height → HT HT is the standard abbreviation for height, and those are the two letters the cities have and the wordplay does not.
  • With added — The height is added to the answer to reach the cities, so the answer itself must be those letters short.
  • being short — The painter was famously short in the leg, and the letters are short too — they stop before the third city is complete.

A painter who, given a few more inches, would somehow stand for three cities instead of one man.

Toulouse-Lautrec's legs stopped growing when he was a teenager, so 'being short' is a joke about the man before it is an instruction about letters.

2 down ACCLAIM

Surprisingly AC Milan dropping name for club initially gets praise (7)

Definition: praise · anagram + substitution + first letter

Acclaim is loud public approval, so 'praise' is a plain synonym — the noun, the applause a crowd gives.

  • AC Milan → ACMILAN The Italian football club's name is the raw material, all seven letters of it.
  • name → N N is the standard shortening of name, and it is the letter that goes.
  • club initially → C The letter 'club' begins with comes on in its place.
  • Surprisingly — What turns out surprisingly has not come out in the order you expected, so these letters are re-sorted.
  • dropping — Dropping a player is leaving them out of the side, and here a letter is left out of the fodder.
  • for — 'Dropping one thing for another' is the everyday phrase for a swap, so a new letter takes the old one's place.
  • initially — 'Initially' asks only for the letter the word beside it begins with.

AC Milan leave a player out of the squad in favour of a new signing and are applauded for it.

Both 'AC Milan' and 'club' point at football, which hides the fact that one of them is a pile of letters and the other is a single one.

3 down AVALANCHE

A drapery that’s hard to break is falling down? (9)

Definition: falling down · charade + container

An avalanche is a mass of snow falling down a mountainside, so the phrase names the event by describing it.

  • A → A The opening word is taken at face value as the single letter it is.
  • drapery → VALANCE A valance is the strip of drapery hung round a bed frame or along a curtain rail.
  • hard → H H is the pencil-grading abbreviation for hard, and it is what breaks in near the end.
  • to break — Breaking into a place means getting inside it, so the letter beside it forces its way in.

A curtain too tough to tear, apparently on the point of coming down.

'Hard to break' reads as one phrase about tough fabric; the setter splits it, so 'hard' is a letter and 'to break' is an instruction.

4 down GENOA

A dose of Farage no alternative for the City (5)

Definition: the City · hidden word

Genoa is a big Italian port, so 'the City' defines it by example — any city fits, and this one is buried in the clue.

  • Farage no alternative → GENOA Five letters run straight on across these three words, starting four letters into the first.
  • A dose of — A dose is a measured portion taken out of something larger, here a run of letters lifted from the middle.

A dose of Nigel Farage prescribed to the Square Mile, with nothing else on offer.

'The City' with its capital letter is London's financial district on the surface; the setter means an Italian port instead.

5 down RESIDENTS

People like Trump and Putin lacking leadership for members of the local community (9)

Definition: members of the local community · deletion + first letter

Residents are the people who live in a place, which is what 'members of the local community' says in longer words.

  • People like Trump and Putin → PRESIDENTS Both men hold their country's top office, and the word for people like them runs to ten letters.
  • leadership → P Leadership here is the leading letter, the P at the front, and that is what goes missing.
  • lacking — What is lacking is simply absent, so the letter named after it never appears.

Two strongmen of the modern sort, said to have no leadership about them whatsoever.

Presidents are leaders by definition, so 'lacking leadership' is a joke about the two men long before it is an instruction about a letter.

6 down QUEER

Unconventional rock band dismissive of new record's opening (5)

Definition: Unconventional · charade + deletion + first letter

Queer's older sense is odd, off the usual pattern, which is exactly what 'unconventional' asks for.

  • rock band → QUEE The rock band is Freddie Mercury's, and it turns up minus the letter that has been brushed aside.
  • record's opening → R The first letter of 'record' is tacked on at the end.
  • dismissive of new — Being dismissive of something is brushing it aside, and what gets brushed aside here is N, the usual abbreviation for new.
  • opening — An opening is the very start of a thing, so only the first letter of the word before it is taken.

A rock band turning up its nose at the first track on a new record.

The band's name has to be spotted before anything can be taken off it, and 'new' turns out to be a letter rather than an adjective describing the record.

7 down ATISHOO

Symptom of a cold caught an hour after noon - go away! (7)

Definition: Symptom of a cold caught · charade

Atishoo is the comic-strip spelling of a sneeze, the symptom you get once a cold has been caught.

  • an hour after noon → ATI The hour after noon is one o'clock — 'at 1', with the 1 written as the Roman numeral.
  • go away! → SHOO Shoo is what you say to send a cat or a pigeon off.

Somebody catches a cold at one in the afternoon and is told in no uncertain terms to clear off.

Clock time is written straight into the letters: 'at one' becomes AT plus a Roman numeral, a numeral-for-number swap worth expecting whenever a clue names an hour.

8 down ELECTRIC BLANKET

Exciting comprehensive that won't leave you cold (8,7)

Definition: that won't leave you cold · charade

To leave someone cold is to fail to excite them, and this thing does the opposite twice over: it warms the bed and it thrills.

  • Exciting → ELECTRIC An electric atmosphere is an exciting one, charged and alive.
  • comprehensive → BLANKET A blanket ban covers everything without exception, which is what comprehensive means.

A thrilling non-selective secondary school, the sort that takes all comers.

A comprehensive is Britain's non-selective state secondary, which is the school the surface conjures up; the setter wants the adjective sense instead.

14 down LEITMOTIF

Working title of film oddly omitted recurring theme (9)

Definition: recurring theme · anagram + alternate letters

A leitmotif is the short musical phrase Wagner attached to a character and brought back each time, so a theme that recurs.

  • title of → TITLEOF These seven letters go into the mix exactly as they stand.
  • film → IM Take out the first and third letters of 'film' and IM is what is left.
  • Working — A word being worked is a word being put through a process, and the process here rearranges its letters.
  • oddly omitted — The odd-numbered letters are the ones left out, so only the even ones survive.

A film's working title being quietly dropped, and a theme that keeps coming back.

'Working title' is a real phrase about films, which is why it takes a while to see 'Working' pulling away from it to do a job of its own.

15 down ELDERSHIP

Position in church Liz mostly combined with shepherd after second husband left (9)

Definition: Position in church · anagram + deletion

An eldership is the office held by a church elder, the lay post that governs a Presbyterian congregation.

  • Liz mostly → LI The name loses its tail, leaving two letters for the mix.
  • shepherd → SEPHERD The whole word joins in, minus one of its two aitches.
  • second husband → H H is the abbreviation for husband, and it is the second of the two in 'shepherd' that goes.
  • mostly — 'Mostly' keeps most of the word and drops the last letter.
  • combined — Combining things mixes them together, so the letters on either side come out in a new order.
  • left — Someone who has left is gone, so the letter named just before it is not in the mix.

Liz teaming up with a shepherd once her second husband has walked out on her.

'Second husband' is one instruction rather than a remark about Liz's marriages: it counts the aitches in the word before it and removes the later one.

17 down PLANNER

One organises nationals in real power going to heaven (7)

Definition: One organises · container + charade + reversal

A planner is the one who draws up the plan, so the definition names the person by the job they do.

  • nationals → NN Doubling a letter is how crosswords make an abbreviation plural, so two Ns stand for more than one national.
  • real → REAL The word goes in whole, with the pair of Ns pushed into the middle of it.
  • power → P P is the physics symbol for power, the one printed on plugs and bulbs.
  • in — 'In' is the plainest container instruction there is: what precedes it goes inside what follows.
  • going to heaven — Heaven is up, and in a down clue up means from the bottom of the entry towards the top, so the letters are read the other way.

Somebody arranging a national championship while the real power quietly ascends.

A down entry runs top to bottom, so anything heading for heaven is climbing against it and the whole assembly has to be read from the bottom up.

19 down JUS SOLI

Swedish tenor Björling accepting old incomplete birthright citizenship (3,4)

Definition: birthright citizenship · container + deletion

Jus soli is the rule that being born on a country's soil makes you its citizen, which is birthright citizenship exactly.

  • Swedish tenor Björling → JUSSI Jussi Björling was Sweden's great operatic tenor, and it is his first name that does the work here.
  • old incomplete → OL 'Old' turns up without its last letter.
  • accepting — Accepting something is taking it in, so the name closes around the letters that follow.
  • incomplete — Incomplete means not all there, and what is missing is the word's final letter.

A famous Swedish tenor taking on an unfinished old song about where you were born.

Jus soli is Latin for right of the soil, and it is argued against jus sanguinis, right of blood, whenever countries fall out over who counts as a citizen.

21 down HELLO

Instrument's initial temperature increases which is welcome (5)

Definition: welcome · substitution

A hello is the greeting you give someone arriving, which is what 'welcome' names when it is used as a noun.

  • Instrument → CELLO The instrument is a cello, and only its first letter is touched.
  • initial temperature increases → H C is the abbreviation for cold and H for hot, so raising the temperature swaps one for the other.
  • initial — 'Initial' points at the very first letter of the instrument's name, the only one that changes.
  • increases — Temperature going up means the cold letter is replaced by the hot one, C out and H in.

An instrument's opening note warming up as the room does.

Both letters here are temperature abbreviations, C for cold and H for hot, which is what allows a rise in temperature to edit a word.

22 down DISCO

TV channel doesn't show very popular dance music (5)

Definition: popular dance music · deletion

Disco is the four-to-the-floor dance music that filled clubs in the seventies, so 'popular dance music' names the genre.

  • TV channel → DISCOVERY The TV channel is Discovery, the documentary station.
  • very → VERY These four letters are the ones taken out of the channel's name.
  • doesn't show — What a channel doesn't show is not on screen, so the word named after it is not among the letters.

A television channel refusing to broadcast some very popular dance music.

'Very popular dance music' reads as one phrase, and the split comes a word earlier than it looks: 'very' belongs to the channel, not to the music.
