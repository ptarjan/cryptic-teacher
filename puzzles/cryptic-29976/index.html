Across

7 across SHOEHORN

Its application could help you get into Oxford? (8)

Definition: Its application could help you get into Oxford? · cryptic definition

A shoehorn is the smooth curved blade you slip behind your heel to ease a tight shoe on, and an Oxford is a shoe.

  • Its application → The reading the surface pushes is a university application — the form you fill in and post off.
  • could help you get into Oxford? → The reading the setter means is physical: an Oxford is a plain lace-up, and getting into one is a matter of a tight heel.

A hopeful student wondering what might improve their chances of a place at the university.

Oxford is a style of lace-up shoe as well as a university, and 'application' slides from a form you send off to a tool you press into use.

9 across ODOURS

Understood our son’s bedding smells (6)

Definition: smells · hidden word

Odours are smells — the neutral noun for whatever the nose picks up, though it leans towards the unpleasant end.

  • Understood our son’s → ODOURS six consecutive letters run from the tail of the first word, straight through the second and into the third
  • bedding — Something bedded into a surface lies embedded within it, so the letters sit unbroken inside the words around them.

A family agreeing that their son's bedclothes have started to smell.

The apostrophe-s reads as an ordinary possessive, and the letters run straight on through it, which is what makes this span so hard to see.

10 across SEAT BELT

House band feature in saloon (4,4)

Definition: feature in saloon · charade

A saloon is the standard enclosed four-door car, and a seat belt is one of the fittings every one of them carries.

  • House → SEAT a country seat is a grand house — the family seat
  • band → BELT a band running round something is a belt

The resident band that plays most nights in a bar.

'House band' is one phrase in the surface and two separate pieces in the wordplay, and 'saloon' is a body style of car rather than a drinking den.

11 across IN GOOD TIME

Early on, I go to party with deviant I’d met (2,4,4)

Definition: Early · anagram

In good time means with time still in hand rather than at the last minute, which is the sense of early wanted here — ahead of schedule.

  • on, I go → ONIGO anagram fodder, five letters
  • I’d met → IDMET anagram fodder, five letters
  • to party — To party is to let loose and mix freely, and letters told to do that will not stay in the order they were given.
  • deviant — Something deviant departs from the expected pattern, which is what these letters do to the sequence they arrive in.

The speaker setting off early for a party in the company of an oddball they had run into before.

Two anagrams sit side by side and neither indicator announces itself: 'to party' is the invitation the whole surface is built on, and 'deviant' is the odd guest.

12 across FRENCH POLISH

Finish, as elements of Marie Curie’s typical description are transposed? (6,6)

Definition: Finish · charade + reversal

French polish is the shellac coating rubbed into wood in many thin layers, so 'Finish' names the surface treatment rather than an ending.

  • elements of Marie Curie’s typical description → FRENCH she took French nationality, and this is the back half of the usual Polish-French label
  • elements of Marie Curie’s typical description → POLISH she was born Maria Skłodowska in Warsaw, which is the front half of that label
  • transposed — Transposing two things exchanges their places, so the two halves of the description swap ends.

The two-nationality label usually hung on Marie Curie, and what happens when its halves change places.

'Elements' is the joke — she discovered polonium and radium, and named polonium after her birthplace. 'Finish' looks like the end of something and turns out to be a substance.

14 across IRON-CLAD

Warship covered by press (4-4)

Definition: Warship · charade

An ironclad was the mid-Victorian warship built of wood and sheathed in iron armour plate, so the word names the ship itself.

  • press → IRON to press a shirt is to iron it
  • covered → CLAD clad means clothed or covered over

A naval vessel getting plenty of coverage in the newspapers.

'Covered by press' reads as newspaper attention; the press here is the household appliance and 'covered' is the clothing word.

15 across NO PAIN NO GAIN

Turning on Paganini number to begin – as motivation for fitness class? (2,4,2,4)

Definition: motivation for fitness class? · charade + anagram

No pain, no gain is the gym-wall slogan saying effort has to hurt before it pays — exactly the line an instructor shouts at a class.

  • number → NO No. is the everyday abbreviation for number
  • on Paganini → ONPAGANINI anagram fodder, ten letters
  • Turning — Something turning is being spun round rather than left alone, so these letters come out in a different order.
  • to begin — Whatever comes 'to begin' is set at the front, so this piece leads the answer instead of following.

Putting a Paganini piece on to get going, the way some people psych themselves up before the gym.

'Turning on' reads as switching a stereo on and 'number' as a piece of music; here 'number' is the abbreviation and 'turning' is a plain instruction.

17 across FERRIS WHEELS

Ferraris and Rolls are mostly stolen for joy rides? (6,6)

Definition: joy rides? · deletion + charade

A Ferris wheel is the giant upright fairground wheel with hanging cars, ridden purely for the fun of it — joy rides in the fairground sense.

  • are mostly → AR 'are' docked of its final letter, and this pair is what gets taken
  • Ferraris → FERRIS the sports-car marque with that pair lifted out of its middle
  • Rolls → WHEELS a Rolls is a motor car, and the capital letter marks it as one example of the slang plural for cars
  • mostly — Most of a word is all of it bar the last letter, so only the opening pair of this one is wanted.
  • stolen — What is stolen is carried off, so those two letters disappear from the word they were sitting in.

Expensive cars being taken without permission by people who only want to drive them fast.

'Joy rides' is the pivot — joyriding in a stolen car on the surface, a paying fairground customer in the answer. 'Rolls' keeps its capital to flag an example.

20 across DEVILISH

Stayed over in Soho – oddly it’s diabolical (8)

Definition: diabolical · charade + reversal + alternate letters

Devilish and diabolical both mean 'of the devil', and both have drifted into everyday use for anything fiendishly difficult.

  • Stayed → LIVED somebody who stayed in a place lived there
  • in Soho → ISH the first, third and fifth letters of these two words
  • over — Something turned over ends up the other way round, so the word in front of it is read backwards.
  • oddly — Odd counts positions here rather than describing behaviour: the first, third and fifth letters are the ones taken.

Someone who spent the night in Soho reporting that, strangely, the experience was dreadful.

'Stayed over' reads as spending the night, which is exactly where the clue wants you, and the little word 'in' is not scenery — it is lending letters.

22 across COOKIE-CUTTER

Template censor used after identifying code (6-6)

Definition: Template · charade

A cookie cutter is the shaped tin pressed into rolled dough so that every biscuit comes out identical, which makes it a template in the literal sense.

  • identifying code → COOKIE a cookie is the small file a website leaves on your machine so it recognises you next time
  • censor → CUTTER a censor's work is snipping material out
  • used after — 'After' fixes the running order, so the piece it governs follows the other rather than leading.

An official blanking out material once a piece of code has identified it.

Both halves read as modern office language — the tracking file and the censor's shears — while the definition is quietly asking for a kitchen object.

23 across REARMAMENT

Fresh investment in Arsenal meant changes on wing at kick-off (10)

Definition: Fresh investment in Arsenal · charade + anagram

Rearmament is fresh spending on a country's arsenal, its stock of weapons, building the stockpile back up after years of running it down.

  • on → RE 're' is the standard word for 'on the subject of', as at the head of a memo
  • wing → ARM a wing of an organisation is one of its arms
  • meant → MEANT anagram fodder, five letters
  • changes — What changes does not stay as it was, so these letters are re-set into a new order.
  • at kick-off — Kick-off is the start of the match, so the piece it governs is placed at the very front.

A football club putting new money in and reshuffling its wingers before the whistle goes.

Arsenal is a north London football club and also a store of weapons, and 'wing' plays the same double game — a winger on the pitch, a branch of a movement.

24 across STAR SIGN

Perhaps Leo Sayer’s trying another rewrite – initially with chorus moving note lower (4,4)

Definition: Leo · first letters + anagram

Leo is one of the twelve signs of the zodiac, so it defines by example: the answer is the category, not the instance.

  • Sayer’s trying another rewrite → STAR the opening letters of these four words, taken in order
  • chorus → SING to chorus a line is to sing it in unison
  • Perhaps — The word flags the name beside it as one instance of something larger, so what is wanted is the class it belongs to.
  • initially — Initially points at the beginnings of words, so only the opening letter of each is taken.
  • moving note lower — 'Note' is the abbreviation n, and lower in a down entry means further down the column, so that letter slides to the end.

Leo Sayer having another go at reworking a song, with the chorus dropped a note.

Leo Sayer is the British pop singer of the 1970s, so 'Leo' reads as a first name; the definition stops there and hands 'Sayer' over to the wordplay.

25 across FRINGE

Less important call when 14 (6)

Definition: Less important · container

Fringe as a modifier means out at the edge of the main event — fringe theatre, fringe politics — so it reads as less important than the centre.

  • 14 → FE 14 across is a word meaning clad in iron, and Fe is iron's chemical symbol
  • call → RING to call a friend is to ring them

The cross-reference is an instruction and not just a source of letters: 14 across means clad in iron, so iron's chemical symbol wraps itself round the other piece.

26 across SVENGALI

Supreme influencer leaving a little something out (8)

Definition: Supreme influencer · anagram + first letter

Svengali, the hypnotist in du Maurier's Trilby, gave his name to anyone who dominates and controls another person — influence at its most complete.

  • leaving → LEAVING anagram fodder, seven letters
  • something → S just the opening letter of the word
  • out — Out here means out of order rather than removed, and that is the sense that disturbs the letters.
  • a little — A little of a word is a small piece of it — its opening letter — rather than a small quantity of anything.

A hugely powerful influencer quietly omitting one small detail.

'Leaving ... out' begs to be read as an instruction to delete something, and that is the trap: 'leaving' supplies the letters and 'out' is what disturbs them.

1 down SHEEP RUN

Place to ruminate clue for ‘ewer’? (5,3)

Definition: Place to ruminate · charade

A sheep run is a stretch of grazing land a flock is turned out on, and sheep are ruminants — so it is literally a place to ruminate.

  • ‘ewer’ → SHEEP the first three letters inside the quotes spell a ewe, and a ewe is a female of the flock
  • ‘ewer’ → RUN the letter left at the end is R, cricket's abbreviation for a single score

Somewhere quiet to sit and mull over how you might go about cluing the word 'ewer'.

The answer is itself a cryptic clue, and reading it as one spells the quoted word. Ruminants chew the cud, so 'ruminate' is pointing at grazing animals rather than at thinking.

4 down MONOTONE

One colour only – second one not included (8)

Definition: One colour only · container + charade

Monotone describes something carried out in a single colour or shade throughout, with no variation from end to end.

  • second → MO a mo is a moment — 'half a mo' — which is a second in the loose sense
  • not → NOT the word goes in whole, exactly as it stands
  • one → ONE likewise taken as written
  • included — Something included in a package is carried along inside it, so this word is planted between the two pieces around it.

A price quoted for a single colour, with the second item excluded from the deal.

The dash reads as a dealer's small print and 'second one not included' as the exclusion clause. Read as instructions, 'included' does the opposite job — it puts something in.

5 down MONTE CARLO

Resort to cornmeal unfortunately (5,5)

Definition: Resort · anagram

Monte Carlo is the casino quarter of Monaco on the Riviera, a place people travel to for pleasure — a resort in the holiday sense.

  • to cornmeal → TOCORNMEAL anagram fodder, ten letters
  • unfortunately — An unfortunate turn of events leaves things in a state nobody arranged, which is what happens to these letters.

Somebody down on their luck having to fall back on cornmeal for dinner.

'Resort to' reads as one phrase — falling back on something — and the definition is only the first word of it. The 're-sort' buried in that word is the setter's wink.

6 down CRIMEA

Peninsula of Palermo – if rock climbing regularly (6)

Definition: Peninsula · alternate letters + reversal

The Crimea is the diamond-shaped peninsula hanging off the northern coast of the Black Sea.

  • Palermo – if rock → AEMIRC the second, fourth, sixth, eighth, tenth and twelfth letters of this run of words
  • climbing — In a down clue, climbing runs from the foot of the entry towards the top, so the letters are read the other way up.
  • regularly — Regular means at a fixed interval rather than at every step, so only every second letter is picked up.

A regular rock-climbing trip on the headland somewhere near Palermo.

Palermo sits on Sicily, an island, so 'Peninsula of Palermo' cannot be the definition it pretends to be — the town is in the clue only to lend letters.

13 down NAPKIN RING

Family group putting down first part of table setting? (6,4)

Definition: part of table setting? · charade

A napkin ring is the little band each rolled napkin is threaded through, one of the small objects laid out in a formal table setting.

  • putting down → NAP the nap of a fabric is its down — the soft pile raised on velvet or baize
  • Family → KIN your kin are your family
  • group → RING a ring is a group acting together, as in a smuggling ring
  • first — The instruction places this piece at the head of the answer, ahead of the two clued before it.

A household laying the table, beginning with the very first thing they set down.

'Family group' reads as one phrase and is two separate pieces, and 'down' is the fabric word here — the pile on velvet — not a direction.

16 down IMITATED

Cambridge university worried boring papers will be copied (8)

Definition: copied · charade + container

Imitated and copied are plain synonyms; imitating leans towards reproducing a manner or style, copying towards duplicating a thing.

  • papers → ID your ID is the papers that prove who you are
  • Cambridge university → MIT the Massachusetts Institute of Technology stands in Cambridge, Massachusetts
  • worried → ATE what eats at you is what worries you — 'what's eating him?'
  • boring — To bore is to drill a hole through something, so what is boring ends up right inside the word it attacks.

An anxious Cambridge academic ploughing through a pile of dull papers.

The Cambridge in play is the Massachusetts one, and 'boring' means drilling rather than dull — two misreadings the surface works hard to keep in place.

18 down SUITABLE

Appropriate clubs potentially qualified (8)

Definition: Appropriate · charade

Suitable and appropriate are near-exact synonyms, both saying a thing fits the occasion, with 'appropriate' the more formal of the pair.

  • clubs → SUIT clubs is one of the four suits in a pack of cards
  • qualified → ABLE someone able is qualified to do the job
  • potentially — Clubs are only one of four, so the word says to move up from the instance to the class it belongs to.

A set of golf clubs that might just turn out to be good enough.

'Clubs' is a card suit rather than golf equipment, and 'potentially' is the small word doing the lifting from example to category.

21 down EMEERS

Appear upset about King supporting Eastern Arabic rulers (6)

Definition: Arabic rulers · charade + reversal + container

An emeer — the older spelling of emir — is an Arab prince or commander, so the plural names a set of rulers in the Arab world.

  • Eastern → E E is the compass abbreviation for eastern
  • Appear → SEEM to appear anxious is to seem anxious
  • King → R R for Rex, Latin for king, as on coins and royal signatures
  • upset — In a down clue, upset means turned on its head, so the word is read from its foot upwards.
  • about — To go about something is to go round it, so one piece wraps itself around the letter that follows.
  • supporting — A support sits underneath whatever it holds up, so this assembly goes below the piece named next.

Looking annoyed that the King has come out in support of rulers in the East.

'Upset' and 'supporting' are both down-clue words: one turns a word on its head, the other puts a piece underneath rather than behind.
