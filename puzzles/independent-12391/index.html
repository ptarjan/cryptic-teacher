1 across FILAMENT

Thread appropriate to be worn by some Californians? (8)

Definition: Thread · container

A filament is a single fine thread — the fibre in a rope, the wire in a bulb — so 'thread' is a plain synonym.

  • appropriate → FIT Appropriate as an adjective is fit, and the word splits after two letters.
  • some Californians? → LAMEN LA is Los Angeles, so a few of its males are LA men.
  • to be worn by — Clothing worn by someone wraps round them, so the garment word closes over the wearers rather than following them.

An item of clothing that would suit a few men from Los Angeles.

'Fit' is doing double duty in the surface, where clothes fit and a fit is an outfit; the wordplay wants only the flat adjective, appropriate.

6 across PROMPT

Instigate frolicking, in part (6)

Definition: Instigate · container

To prompt someone is to set them going, so 'instigate' is the same verb in a more formal register.

  • part → PT PT is the standard abbreviation for part, as in Pt 1.
  • frolicking → ROMP A romp is a frolic.
  • in — Placed in something, a word goes bodily inside it and splits it open.

Setting off a bit of playful fun.

'In part' reads as the everyday phrase for partly, and it is an instruction with an abbreviation attached — two letters with something dropped between them.

9 across SCAB

Sing composition about bobbin initially wound once? (4)

Definition: wound once · first letters

A scab is the crust that forms over a healing cut, so it marks a spot that was wounded at one time.

  • Sing composition about bobbin → SCAB The opening letter of each of these four words, taken in the order they stand.
  • initially — Initially asks for opening letters, and it reaches back over every word in the run before it.

A song written about a reel of cotton that went round just once.

'Wound' sits beside 'bobbin' so it reads as the past tense of wind; the injury sense is the one that pays, and the question mark admits the definition is a description.

10 across TRAJECTORY

Path of hauling vehicle transporting most of jet back to bay (10)

Definition: Path · container + charade + deletion + last letter

A trajectory is the curved path a thrown or fired object follows through the air, so 'path' is the plain noun.

  • hauling vehicle → TRACTOR A tractor is the machine that hauls a plough or a trailer.
  • most of jet → JE Most of the word, which is all of it bar the final letter.
  • back to bay → Y The letter at the back of the word 'bay'.
  • transporting — A vehicle transporting a load carries it inside, so the letters go within rather than alongside.

A tow truck hauling most of a jet back down to the harbour.

'Back to bay' belongs to the towing picture and is really an instruction: take the letter standing at the back of the word 'bay'.

11 across BUTTERFINGERS

She often drops in but regrets dancing with fellow (13)

Definition: She often drops · anagram

A butterfingers is someone who cannot keep hold of anything, so 'she often drops' names the person by the habit.

  • in but regrets → INBUTREGRETS Twelve of the thirteen letters, offered up as ordinary words.
  • fellow → F F is the standard abbreviation for fellow.
  • dancing — Dancing letters are letters on the move, and they come to rest in a different order.

A woman keeps calling round, dancing with a chap she wishes she had never met.

'Drops in' reads as one phrase, paying a visit; the definition stops at 'drops', and 'in' joins the letters being shuffled.

12 across STEW

Cook makes moist rolls (4)

Definition: Cook · reversal

To stew is to cook slowly in liquid, so 'cook' is a verb-for-verb swap in the general sense.

  • makes moist → WETS To wet something is to make it moist, and this word then turns over.
  • rolls — Something that rolls over ends up the other way up, so the letters are read from the far end.

A cook turning out damp bread rolls.

'Cook' looks like the person doing the baking and is the verb instead — the answer also names a dish, which is what makes the kitchen surface so comfortable.

14 across WALRUSES

Reversal of rule tricks swimmers (8)

Definition: swimmers · charade + reversal

Walruses spend their lives swimming in Arctic water, so 'swimmers' names them by what they do, plural to match.

  • rule → WAL A rule is a law, written out backwards.
  • tricks → RUSES A ruse is a trick, and the plural matches.
  • Reversal — A reversal turns a word end for end, so its letters are read the other way round.

A change in the rules catches the bathers out.

'Rule' means law here, not a ruler or a reign, and it is the only word being turned; 'tricks' arrives the right way round.

17 across TRAYBAKE

Fish, bass, placed in appropriate dish for sharing (8)

Definition: dish for sharing · container + charade

A traybake is a cake baked flat in a single tin and cut into squares for a crowd, so it is a dish for sharing.

  • appropriate → TAKE To appropriate something is to take it.
  • Fish → RAY A ray is the flat sea fish with the whip tail.
  • bass → B B is the musical abbreviation for bass.
  • placed in — Placed in a word, the pieces named before it go bodily inside it.

Fish and bass set out in the right platter for everyone to share.

'Appropriate' is the verb meaning to take, not the adjective meaning suitable, and the surface leans its whole weight on the adjective.

19 across DUET

Song cover lacking volume (4)

Definition: Song · deletion

A duet is a piece written for two voices or instruments, so 'song' names it by its kind.

  • cover → DUVET A duvet is the cover on a bed.
  • volume → V V is the standard abbreviation for volume.
  • lacking — What a word lacks is missing from it, so the letter named is taken away.

A cover version of a song, played far too quietly.

'Cover' sits next to 'song' so it reads as a cover version, and 'volume' as the knob on the amplifier; both belong to the bedroom instead.

21 across CHERRY-PICKING

Be visibly upset about that woman's photo, man being biased? (6-7)

Definition: being biased · container + charade

Cherry-picking is choosing only the evidence that suits you, which is being biased by selection rather than by opinion.

  • Be visibly upset → CRY To cry is to show your upset where everyone can see it.
  • that woman's → HER That woman's coat is her coat.
  • photo → PIC Pic is the everyday short form of photo.
  • man → KING A king is a man of the chess set and of the card pack.
  • about — A word set about another wraps round it, leaving it in the middle.

Weeping over that woman's photograph, a man stands accused of prejudice.

'Man' for the king of a pack of cards or a chess set is shorthand worth keeping; the surface wants you to read it as the fellow doing the crying.

24 across TRIUMPHANT

Celebrating two caps spanning one November on separate occasions (10)

Definition: Celebrating · container + charade

Someone triumphant has won and is showing it, which is what a celebrating winner looks like.

  • two caps → TRUMPHAT To cap a story is to trump it, and a hat is the cap you wear — two words for capping, laid one after the other.
  • one → I The Roman numeral for one.
  • November → N November is N in the radio alphabet.
  • spanning — Something that spans a gap reaches right over whatever is sitting in the middle.
  • on separate occasions — The two single letters go into different words rather than both into the same one.

A player celebrating two international caps won on separate days in November.

A cap is an appearance for a national side, which is the sport the surface is playing; the wordplay wants the other two senses, the hat and the card that beats the rest.

25 across CUTE

Clever tool for breaking walls closer to joist (4)

Definition: Clever · container + last letter

Cute keeps an older sense of sharp-witted — 'don't get cute with me' — and that is the clever meant here.

  • tool for breaking → CUE The cue is the stick that plays the break at the start of a snooker frame.
  • closer to joist → T The closing letter of the word 'joist'.
  • walls — To wall something is to build round it, which leaves it shut inside.

A handy implement for knocking a hole through the wall next to the joist.

'Tool for breaking walls' reads as a sledgehammer. 'Breaking' is the snooker sense, the opening shot of a frame, and 'walls' is no part of that phrase at all.

26 across BECKON

Sign man potted overwhelms beginner on Crucible floor (6)

Definition: Sign · container + charade + first letter

To beckon is to signal someone over with a movement of the hand, so 'sign' is the verb for making that silent signal.

  • man → BEN Ben is the short form of Benjamin, a man's name.
  • Crucible → C Only the first letter of the theatre's name is taken.
  • floor → KO To floor an opponent is to knock him out, and a knockout is a KO.
  • potted — A potted history is a condensed one, so the name arrives in its short form.
  • overwhelms — To overwhelm something is to come down over it on every side, leaving it enclosed.
  • beginner on — The beginner on a word is the letter it starts with, and only that letter is wanted.

A player pots a ball and overwhelms a newcomer on the floor of the Crucible.

The Crucible in Sheffield is the theatre where the World Snooker Championship is played, so 'potted' and 'floor' both read as part of that arena rather than as instructions.

27 across ENDURING

Going on foot, mathematician leaves capital (8)

Definition: Going on · charade + deletion

Something enduring goes on and on, 'going on' in the sense of continuing rather than of happening.

  • foot → END The foot of a page or a bed is its end.
  • mathematician → URING Alan Turing, the mathematician who broke Enigma, arrives without the T he is written with.
  • leaves capital — What leaves is the capital letter itself, the initial the name is written with.

On foot, a mathematician walks out of the capital city.

'Capital' wants to be a city, especially with a man leaving one; it is a capital letter, and the man is the only one who never goes anywhere.

2 down INCUBATE

Sit in with youngster at the end of lunchtime (8)

Definition: Sit · charade

A hen sits on her eggs to incubate them, so 'sit' is the poultry-keeper's verb for exactly this brooding.

  • in → IN The little word is taken at face value, as the two letters it spells.
  • youngster → CUB A cub is a young fox, bear or lion.
  • at the end of lunchtime → ATE By the time lunchtime is over, you have eaten.

Someone settles down beside a youngster as lunchtime finishes.

'Sit in' reads as one phrase — a sit-in is a protest — but the definition stops after the first word, and 'in' is a piece of the build.

3 down ABBOT

One brother looks up to grab both boxes (5)

Definition: One brother looks up to · hidden word

An abbot heads a monastery, so the ordinary brothers of the house are exactly the people who look up to him.

  • grab both → ABBOT Five consecutive letters run across the join between these two words.
  • boxes — To box something is to shut it in a container, so the word before it is holding the letters unbroken.

A monk gazing upwards to seize a pair of crates.

'Boxes' is a verb here, not a plural noun, and that is what turns the two ordinary words in front of it into a hiding place.

4 down EITHER WAY

No matter what I wear they must change (6,3)

Definition: No matter what · anagram

'Either way' is the phrase for 'whichever of the two it turns out to be', which is the shrug 'no matter what' makes.

  • I wear they → IWEARTHEY Nine letters handed over in plain sight, to be rearranged.
  • must change — Something that must change will not be left in the arrangement the clue hands you.

Whatever I put on, the clothes have to be altered.

'No matter what' looks like the opening of a sentence rather than a definition, and the ungrammatical 'I wear they' is the tell that these words are fodder.

5 down TEARFUL

Close to crying, student at the back gets reprimand (7)

Definition: Close to crying · charade + last letter

Someone tearful has tears already coming, which is what being close to crying describes.

  • student → T The hindmost letter of the word 'student'.
  • reprimand → EARFUL An earful is the telling-off you get for it.
  • at the back — What is at the back of a word is its rearmost letter, and only that letter is taken.

A pupil in the back row is being told off.

'Student' is not the crossword's usual L here: the clue wants a letter of the word itself, which is what 'at the back' is pointing at.

6 down PREEN

Groom not fully prepared, suffering head loss under pressure (5)

Definition: Groom · charade + deletion

To preen is to smarten yourself up, and a bird preening is grooming its feathers, so 'groom' is the verb sense.

  • pressure → P P is the standard symbol for pressure.
  • not fully prepared → REEN Green is the word for unseasoned or not fully prepared, and it arrives here minus its first letter.
  • suffering head loss — A word that loses its head loses the letter it starts with, and here that is a G.
  • under — Under, in a down entry, means lower on the grid, so these letters sit below the single one.

A bridegroom, only half ready, is losing his head under the strain.

'Groom' at the front reads as the bridegroom of the surface, which is where the misdirection lives; the answer is a verb, and it is what a bird does to its feathers.

7 down ON THE MEND

Tendon he damaged covering miles is healing (2,3,4)

Definition: healing · anagram

Someone on the mend is recovering, mending in the sense of a body repairing itself, which is what healing describes.

  • Tendon he → TENDONHE Eight letters offered up as an ordinary pair of words.
  • miles → M M is the map and road-sign abbreviation for miles.
  • damaged — Damaged letters have been knocked out of the order they were given in.
  • covering — Fodder that covers something takes it in, so the extra letter joins the pile being rearranged.

A runner with a damaged tendon is still clocking up the miles.

'Tendon he' is an awkward pair of words to hand over as fodder, and the sentence disguises it by making you hear 'tendon he damaged' as an ordinary relative clause.

8 down PHRASE

Expression clashes in speech (6)

Definition: Expression · homophone

A phrase is a set group of words used as a unit, which is what an expression is.

  • clashes → PHRASE A fray is a clash or brawl, and clashes are frays; said aloud, that plural is spelt quite differently.
  • in speech — In speech you get the version you hear, not the version anyone writes down.

Arguments breaking out in the middle of a conversation.

'Clashes' is a noun here, the fights people have, not a verb about colours or cymbals — and the noun is the word you have to say out loud.

13 down WORSE LUCK

Sadly try hard to pen fresh clues (5,4)

Definition: Sadly · container + anagram

'Worse luck' is what you tack on to bad news — 'he isn't coming, worse luck' — which is the job 'sadly' does in a sentence.

  • try hard → WORK To work at something is to try hard at it.
  • clues → SELUC The five letters of 'clues' in a new order.
  • to pen — To pen animals is to shut them in an enclosure, so one word closes around the others.
  • fresh — Letters made fresh are made new, which here means put in an order they were not given in.

A setter struggling to write new crossword clues.

'Sadly' opens the clue where an anagram indicator usually sits, and it is the definition; what it defines is a phrase you say, not an adverb.

15 down UNEXCITED

Football team admitting former champion's top is cool (9)

Definition: cool · container + charade + first letter

Cool here means unmoved rather than fashionable: showing no excitement about something.

  • Football team → UNITED Half the clubs in English football are United — Manchester, Leeds, Newcastle.
  • former → EX An ex is a former one, of anything.
  • champion's top → C The top letter of the word 'champion'.
  • admitting — A club that admits someone takes them inside, so the other letters end up within it.

A club that has signed a former champion's best player is keeping calm.

'Champion's top' looks like a shirt in the signing picture, and 'top' is the instruction: in a down entry, the letter standing highest is the first one.

16 down HEIGHTEN

Hearts card seen ahead of the turn, finally? Raise! (8)

Definition: Raise · charade + last letters

To heighten something is to raise it, increasing its height or its intensity.

  • Hearts → H H is the card-table abbreviation for the suit of hearts.
  • card → EIGHT An eight is a card in every suit of the pack.
  • the turn → EN The closing letters of these two words, in order.
  • seen ahead of — What is seen ahead of something else stands in front of it, so these letters come first.
  • finally — Finally asks for the letter each word finishes on, and here two words are giving one up.

At the poker table a card comes down before the turn, and the betting goes up.

The turn is the fourth community card in Texas hold'em and 'Raise!' is a call at the same table, so the whole surface is one hand of poker.

18 down APPEASE

Satisfy upset Thorpe Park-goers at last following early closure of attraction (7)

Definition: Satisfy · charade + deletion + last letters + reversal

To appease people is to satisfy them enough to quiet them, so 'satisfy' is the plain verb behind it.

  • attraction → APPEA An attraction is an appeal, and the L it ends on never arrives.
  • Thorpe Park-goers → SE The final letters here are E and S, and they climb back up the grid the other way round.
  • upset — Upset, in a down entry, sends the letters back up the way they came.
  • at last — At last picks the letter each word ends on, here from two words in a row.
  • following — What follows something is written after it, so these letters take second place.
  • early closure of — A closure that comes early shuts the word before its real ending, losing the final letter.

Visitors to a theme park are annoyed when a ride shuts down early.

Thorpe Park is a big English theme park, so 'Park-goers' reads as its visitors; 'upset' looks like their mood and is the instruction to read two letters upwards.

20 down SCARCE

Hard to find vehicle near small church (6)

Definition: Hard to find · charade

Something scarce is in short supply, which is why you would be hard put to find one.

  • small → S S is the clothing-label abbreviation for small.
  • vehicle → CAR A car is the everyday vehicle.
  • church → CE CE is the Church of England, the usual church in a British crossword.
  • near — Near only asks for the pieces to stand beside each other; it never says which side, which is the setter being loose.

A car parked beside a little chapel.

'Hard to find' is the whole definition, so the wordplay starts at 'vehicle' — three conventional abbreviations laid end to end in an order the clue never quite states.

22 down RIPEN

Mature lone pirate held up harbours (5)

Definition: Mature · hidden word + reversal

To ripen is to grow mature — fruit ripening on a tree is fruit maturing, and both are verbs.

  • lone pirate → RIPEN Five consecutive letters straddle the join between these two words, running the other way.
  • held up — Held up a down entry, a run of letters is read from the bottom of the grid upwards.
  • harbours — To harbour someone is to shelter them out of sight, so these words are concealing letters rather than describing them.

An old pirate, delayed and on his own, is sheltering somebody.

'Harbours' is a verb and belongs to the docks on the surface; 'Mature' opens the clue as an adjective describing the pirate, and it is the verb that pays.

23 down INCUR

Receive hot dog (5)

Definition: Receive · charade

You incur a fine or a debt in the sense of receiving it as a consequence of what you did, not of being handed it.

  • hot → IN What is hot is in, in the fashion sense.
  • dog → CUR A cur is a mongrel dog.

Somebody being handed a hot dog at a stall.

Three words, and the two that make the snack are separate pieces: 'hot' is the fashion sense, a hot ticket being the one everybody wants.
