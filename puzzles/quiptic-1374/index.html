Across

1 across CLAUSE

Loudly rips part of contract (6)

Definition: part of contract · homophone

A clause is one numbered provision of a contract, the unit lawyers point at when they argue over what was actually agreed.

  • rips → CLAUSE what rips at the furniture claws at it, and CLAWS said out loud is written down quite differently
  • Loudly — Something done loudly is done aloud rather than on paper, so the wordplay hands you a sound and leaves the spelling to you.

Someone noisily tearing up a page of a contract.

'Rips' looks like a verb belonging to the surface story of destroyed paperwork, and the sound you need out of it belongs to a cat.

4 across PAUSES

Personal assistant exploits interruptions (6)

Definition: interruptions · charade

A pause is a short break in something already running — speech, music, a meeting — so pauses are the interruptions of it.

  • Personal assistant → PA PA is the everyday short form of personal assistant
  • exploits → USES to exploit a resource is to use it

A personal assistant turning every interruption to their own advantage.

'Exploits' can be a noun meaning daring deeds, which is how the surface would like to be read; the wordplay wants the plain verb.

9 across LACE

Spike netting (4)

Definition: Spike · double definition

To lace a drink is to spike it with alcohol, and lace is netting in the sense of a fine open fabric of knotted threads.

  • Spike → the verb: to add spirits to somebody's drink without telling them
  • netting → the noun: fabric made of fine threads knotted into an open mesh

An instruction to drive a spike through some netting.

Two senses sit side by side with nothing joining them: the verb for doctoring a drink, and the noun for openwork fabric.

10 across UNEQUALLED

A dull queen strangely without peer (10)

Definition: without peer · anagram

Unequalled says nothing matches it, and 'without peer' makes the same claim using the older sense of peer, an equal.

  • A dull queen → ADULLQUEEN anagram fodder, ten letters
  • strangely → anagram indicator
  • strangely — Something done strangely is not done the usual way, so the letters beside it refuse to stay in the order they arrived in.

A tedious monarch behaving oddly and with no equal anywhere in the land.

'Without peer' looks like part of the picture of a lonely queen with no equals at court, and peer there means an equal, which makes it the definition.

11 across AGATES

An entrance with small stones (6)

Definition: stones · charade

Agates are banded semi-precious stones, the striped ones cut for cheap jewellery and rolled as marbles.

  • An → A the article gives up its shorter form, a single letter
  • entrance → GATE a gate is the entrance through a wall or a fence
  • small → S S is the size abbreviation for small, as on any clothing label

A doorway set about with little stones.

The sentence reads as one continuous description of a gateway, so the join between wordplay and definition falls inside the picture rather than at a natural pause.

12 across DRESSING

Director redesigned singer’s clothing (8)

Definition: clothing · anagram

Dressing and clothing are both the verbal noun for putting garments on somebody — what you are doing while you get dressed.

  • Director → D d is the standard abbreviation for director
  • singer’s → SINGERS anagram fodder, seven letters once the apostrophe is dropped
  • redesigned → anagram indicator
  • redesigned — To redesign something is to build it again to a different plan out of the same materials, so these letters end up in a new arrangement.

A film director giving the singer's stage outfit a makeover.

The apostrophe in 'singer's' reads as an ordinary possessive, and its S is one of the eight letters being shuffled.

13 across GRAVEYARD

Serious distance to resting place (9)

Definition: resting place · charade

A graveyard is the ground where the dead are laid to rest, and 'resting place' is the standard euphemism for exactly that.

  • Serious → GRAVE a grave expression is a serious one; this adjective has nothing to do with burial
  • distance → YARD a yard is three feet, a unit of distance

A long hard trek still to go before anywhere you could stop and rest.

'Serious' means grave in the sense of a solemn face, an adjective that arrived in English by a completely separate route from the burial noun it happens to spell.

15 across FORM

Class protocol (4)

Definition: Class · double definition

A form is a class in a British school — the third form, the sixth form — and form is also the done thing, as in 'good form'.

  • Class → the school sense: a year group of pupils taught together in Britain
  • protocol → the etiquette sense: the accepted way of behaving, praised or condemned as good or bad

The etiquette that governs a school classroom.

In Britain a school year group is a form, and behaving as expected is good form — two senses that share nothing but their spelling.

16 across SAND

Smooth leaders of society attracting new devotees (4)

Definition: Smooth · first letters

To sand a surface is to rub it down with abrasive paper until it is smooth, so the verb takes its name from the grit.

  • society → S the opening letter of Society
  • attracting → A the opening letter of Attracting
  • new → N the opening letter of New
  • devotees → D the opening letter of Devotees
  • leaders of — A leader walks at the front of the group, so the front letter of each word that follows is what you collect.

Prominent members of society picking up a fresh crop of followers.

'Smooth' sits where an adjective describing those leaders would sit, and it is the verb — what you do to a rough plank.

17 across VERSATILE

Flexible relatives cavorting (9)

Definition: Flexible · anagram

Someone versatile turns their hand to anything, which is flexible in the adaptable sense rather than the physically bendy one.

  • relatives → RELATIVES anagram fodder, nine letters
  • cavorting → anagram indicator
  • cavorting — To cavort is to caper about with no order to it at all, so the letters beside this word will not stand still.

A family of limber relations larking about together.

The enumeration is the whole hint on a three-word clue: only one of the three words is nine letters long, which settles which is fodder and which is definition.

21 across DOMINEER

Browbeat active person carrying explosive (8)

Definition: Browbeat · container

To domineer over people is to bully them into doing as you say, which is what browbeating them amounts to.

  • active person → DOER a doer is someone who acts rather than talks — 'a doer, not a dreamer'
  • explosive → MINE a mine is an explosive charge, buried or moored, waiting to be set off
  • carrying — Someone carrying a package has it on their person, inside their coat, so this word takes the next piece within itself.

A go-getter walking around with a bomb on them.

'Explosive' reads as an adjective describing that energetic person, and the wordplay needs the noun — a specific device.

22 across GOVERN

Control bit of manufacturing overnight (6)

Definition: Control · hidden word

To govern is to control — a country, a company, or the speed of an engine, which is where a governor takes its name from.

  • manufacturing overnight → GOVERN six consecutive letters run from the tail of the first word straight into the second
  • bit of — A bit of something is a piece broken out of the middle of it, so a run of letters is lifted from inside the words that follow.

A factory keeping its production line running through the night.

'Bit of' is about as quiet as a hidden-word signal gets, because it reads as a scrap of the manufacturing, which is exactly what the surface needs it to mean.

24 across GREAT-NIECE

Relative is unusually energetic around middle of day (5-5)

Definition: Relative · anagram + middle letter

Your great-niece is the daughter of your niece or nephew, one further generation down the family tree, so she is a relative by blood.

  • energetic → ENERGETIC anagram fodder, nine letters
  • middle of day → A the central letter of dAy
  • unusually → anagram indicator
  • unusually — What is done unusually is not done the usual way, so these letters abandon the order the clue lists them in.
  • around — Going around something means closing over it, so the letter named next is gathered in with the rest rather than left outside.

A lively family member charging about the house at lunchtime.

'Middle of day' reads as noon and asks instead for the letter standing between the D and the Y.

25 across CHEF

Cook cooked halved elephant fish heads (4)

Definition: Cook · first letters

A chef is a professional cook, the one running a kitchen rather than anybody who happens to be at the stove.

  • cooked → C the opening letter of Cooked
  • halved → H the opening letter of Halved
  • elephant → E the opening letter of Elephant
  • fish → F the opening letter of Fish
  • heads — The head of a word is its first letter, and the plural asks for one from each of the words listed before it.

A recipe calling for half an elephant and a few fish heads.

'Cooked' and 'halved' both look like instructions to do something to their neighbours, and both are only there to donate a letter.

26 across EVENLY

Beryl never holds back consistently (6)

Definition: consistently · hidden word + reversal

To do something evenly is to do it at a steady unvarying rate from start to finish, which is doing it consistently.

  • Beryl never → YLNEVE six consecutive letters run from inside the first word straight into the second
  • holds — To hold something is to have it contained within your grasp, so the letters sit unbroken inside the words before this.
  • back — This entry runs across the grid, so back means reading from the right-hand end towards the left.

Beryl, who never manages to hold anything back for long.

'Holds back' reads as one phrase meaning restrains, and the clue needs it as two words doing two different jobs.

27 across STURDY

Solid investigation involving leader of research (6)

Definition: Solid · container + first letter

Something sturdy takes weight and knocks without giving, which is solid in the well-built sense rather than the not-hollow one.

  • investigation → STUDY a study of a problem is an investigation into it
  • leader of research → R the first letter of Research
  • involving — To involve someone is to draw them into the thing, so the letter named next ends up inside the word before it.
  • leader of — A leader stands at the head of the line, so only the opening letter of the word that follows is taken.

A solid inquiry that brings the head of the research team on board.

'Leader of research' reads as a person running a project, and it asks for a single letter.

1 down CLANGER

Vacant colonel with temper makes stupid mistake (7)

Definition: stupid mistake · charade + outer letters

A clanger is a blunder loud enough that everyone in the room notices — you drop one, the way a bell rings out in a silence.

  • Vacant colonel → CL COLONEL emptied of everything inside it leaves the C and the L standing at its ends
  • temper → ANGER to be in a temper is to be in an anger — the two nouns swap freely
  • Vacant — A vacant building has had everything between its walls removed, so the word keeps only the letters at its two ends.

An empty-headed colonel losing his temper and getting something badly wrong.

'Vacant' reads as a description of the colonel — dim, absent-minded — while it is quietly an instruction to hollow the word out.

2 down AVERT

Deflect state tracing initially (5)

Definition: Deflect · charade + first letter

To avert something is to turn it aside before it arrives — you avert your eyes, or avert a disaster — which is deflecting it.

  • state → AVER to aver something is to state it flatly, the way a witness does
  • tracing initially → T the opening letter of Tracing
  • initially — What happens initially happens at the very start, so only the opening letter of the word standing beside it is taken.

Dodging the government's surveillance right at the outset.

'State' is the trap: it reads as the government doing the tracing, and it is the verb aver, to declare something as fact.

3 down SQUASHY

Coy about question by a school’s head being soft (7)

Definition: soft · container + charade + first letter

Something squashy gives way the moment you press it — an overripe peach, a beanbag — which is soft in the yielding sense.

  • Coy → SHY coy and shy both describe someone hanging back from attention
  • question → QU qu. is the standard short form of question, on a form or in a textbook
  • a → A the article is lifted straight out of the clue as a single letter
  • school’s head → S the front letter of School
  • about — To be about something is to be all round it, so this word wraps itself over the pieces named after it.
  • head — The head of anything is its front end, so only the leading letter of the word it belongs to is used.

A shy pupil ducking a question put to them by the head of a school.

The 'school's head' sounds like a person and asks for one letter, and 'question' contracts to the two-letter qu. rather than the single Q you would expect.

5 down AMULET

Charm a stubborn person over time (6)

Definition: Charm · charade

An amulet is an object worn to keep harm off, so it is a charm in the lucky-token sense rather than the personal-appeal one.

  • a → A the article taken as it stands from the clue
  • stubborn person → MULE a mule is proverbially obstinate, and the word is used of people who will not budge
  • time → T t is the standard abbreviation for time
  • over — This entry runs downwards, and one thing over another sits above it, so the pieces named first are stacked on top of the last.

Slowly winning over an obstinate person as the years go by.

'Charm' is the noun for a lucky object, not the verb for winning someone round, and the surface leans its whole weight on the verb.

6 down SPLASH OUT

Spend a lot of money, getting belt aboard jet (6,3)

Definition: Spend a lot of money · container

To splash out is the British idiom for spending freely on something you did not strictly need — opening the wallet wide.

  • jet → SPOUT a jet of water is a spout of it
  • belt → LASH to belt someone is to lash them — both are blows, not straps
  • aboard — Anything taken aboard is carried within the vessel, so one piece travels inside the other rather than beside it.

A big spender taking a belt on board a private jet.

Both short words are doing violence to your expectations: a belt is a blow rather than something worn, and a jet is a spurt of water rather than an aircraft.

7 down STERNUM

Bone bird in entirety (7)

Definition: Bone · container

The sternum is the breastbone, the flat plate running down the front of the ribcage where the ribs meet.

  • entirety → SUM the sum of a thing is the whole of it, as in 'the sum total'
  • bird → TERN a tern is a small sea bird, and one of the crossword's regulars
  • in — The plainest container word there is — what is in something sits inside it, here between the letters of the piece named next.

A bird studied whole, bones and all.

TERN is worth banking as vocabulary, and 'in entirety' reads as a single phrase while the clue needs it as two separate words.

8 down TENDER-HEARTED

Offer card to editor being kind (6-7)

Definition: kind · charade

Someone tender-hearted is easily moved by other people's troubles, which is kind in the soft, sympathetic sense.

  • Offer → TENDER you tender a resignation, and a tender is a formal offer to do a job
  • card → HEART one of the four suits in a pack of playing cards
  • editor → ED ed is the usual short form of editor

Handing a greetings card to your editor to show you care.

'Card' does the real hiding: it reads as something you buy in a shop and post, and the wordplay wants a red suit.

14 down VENTILATE

Air talent I’ve developed (9)

Definition: Air · anagram

To ventilate a room is to air it, letting fresh air move through, and both verbs also cover airing a grievance in public.

  • talent I’ve → TALENTIVE anagram fodder, nine letters once the apostrophe is dropped
  • developed → anagram indicator
  • developed — What has been developed has been worked up into a shape quite unlike the material it started from, so the letters do not stay put.

A performer boasting about the air of talent they have developed over the years.

'Air' is a verb here, not the noun the surface offers, and it is doing the defining from the very first word of the clue.

16 down SCOURGE

Whip company into upswing (7)

Definition: Whip · container

A scourge is a whip of knotted cords, the flogging kind, long before the word came to mean any affliction.

  • upswing → SURGE an upswing in prices is a surge in them
  • company → CO Co. is the abbreviation printed on any firm's letterhead
  • into — Putting one thing into another leaves it enclosed, so the piece named first is buried inside the piece named last.

Driving a struggling firm hard until its fortunes turn upward.

'Whip' reads as the verb for driving somebody on, and the definition wants the noun — the object doing the driving.

18 down SEGMENT

Division of, say, soldiers in street (7)

Definition: Division · container + charade

A segment is a piece cut off a whole — an orange segment, a segment of a circle — so it is a division of that thing.

  • street → ST St is the abbreviation on any road sign
  • say → EG eg introduces an example, which is the job 'say' does in a sentence
  • soldiers → MEN the men of a regiment are its soldiers, as in 'officers and men'
  • in — Whatever is in something sits inside it, so the pieces named before this word drop between the letters of the one after it.

A division of soldiers drawn up in the street.

'Division of, say, soldiers' invites you to read the whole opening as one definition; only the first word is, and 'say' is the abbreviation eg.

19 down LARGELY

Mostly rearranged gallery (7)

Definition: Mostly · anagram

Largely and mostly both mean for the greater part: a statement largely true is true across most of it.

  • gallery → GALLERY anagram fodder, seven letters
  • rearranged → anagram indicator
  • rearranged — Rearranging furniture keeps every piece and changes only where each one stands, which is what happens to these letters.

A gallery that has had most of its pictures rehung.

'Mostly' looks like an instruction to take most of a word and drop the rest, which is its usual job in a clue; here it is the definition.

20 down FENNEL

Aromatic plant found in swamp and North Eastern lake (6)

Definition: Aromatic plant · charade

Fennel is a feathery herb with a strong aniseed smell, grown for its seeds and its bulb, so it is an aromatic plant.

  • swamp → FEN a fen is low waterlogged ground; the Fens of eastern England are the best known stretch
  • North Eastern → NE NE is the compass abbreviation for north eastern
  • lake → L L is the map abbreviation for lake

A herb growing wild by a marsh beside a lake in the north east.

Everything after the definition is landscape vocabulary, which is why the sentence reads as a line from a field guide rather than as three instructions.

23 down VICAR

Vehicle after six for clergywoman (5)

Definition: clergywoman · charade

A vicar is the priest in charge of a Church of England parish, a post women have held since 1994, so the word covers either.

  • six → VI VI is six in Roman numerals
  • Vehicle → CAR the everyday vehicle on the drive
  • after — What comes after something follows behind it, so the piece named first in the clue is written second in the answer.

A car turning up after six o'clock to collect the parish priest.

'Six' is a Roman numeral rather than the hour the surface offers, and the vehicle ends up second despite being named first.
