Across

1 across MALTA

Old woman going round key or island (5)

Definition: island · container

Malta is the Mediterranean island republic south of Sicily, so the definition names it by the geography that gave the country its name.

  • Old woman → MA ma = mother, the crossword's stock old woman
  • key → ALT alt is a key on every computer keyboard
  • going round — something going round another thing travels about its outside, so the old woman ends up either side of what she circles

'Key' wants to be the definition — a key is a low sandy island, and the enumeration fits. It is the keyboard sort, and 'or' is only joinery.

4 across CLASSMATE

Schoolfellow had meal after ship brought in shellfish (9)

Definition: Schoolfellow · container + charade

A classmate is someone taught in the same class, which is what the slightly formal British word schoolfellow names.

  • shellfish → CLAM a clam is a shellfish, and it opens up after three letters
  • ship → SS SS prefixes a steamship's name, as in SS Great Britain
  • had meal → ATE to have had a meal is to have eaten
  • brought in — bringing something in carries it inside, so the vessel is stowed within the shellfish rather than beside it
  • after — in an across clue what comes after follows to the right, so the meal is tacked on at the end

The surface is one smooth sentence about supper on the quayside, and the instructions inside it run in a different order from the answer, which is what hides them.

9 across PUCCINI

Composer recalled trophy given to contralto in Italy (7)

Definition: Composer · charade + reversal

Puccini wrote La Bohème, Tosca and Madama Butterfly, so the definition works by example — one named member of a very large class.

  • trophy → PUC a trophy is a cup, written here back to front
  • contralto → C c = contralto in the abbreviations used for voice parts
  • in → IN the word is taken exactly as it stands
  • Italy → I I is Italy's international vehicle code, seen on number plates
  • recalled — to recall a thing is to call it back, and in an across clue back means the letters read right to left

Three of the four pieces are a letter or two long, which is what makes the turned-round trophy easy to miss — the surface, a prize handed to a singer, is entirely innocent.

10 across FELT TIP

Writer was keenly aware of suggestion (4,3)

Definition: Writer · charade

A felt tip is a pen, and 'writer' names it as the thing that writes rather than the person who does — a swap setters make constantly.

  • was keenly aware of → FELT to have felt something is to have been keenly aware of it
  • suggestion → TIP a tip is a suggestion or hint

Two words joined and nothing else done to them, so the whole clue turns on 'Writer' — a person in the surface, an implement in the answer.

11 across TIARA

Sailor embracing one with a crown (5)

Definition: crown · container + charade

A tiara is the jewelled semicircular headpiece worn by a queen or a bride, so 'crown' defines it by the family of ceremonial headgear it sits in.

  • Sailor → TAR tar is old slang for a sailor, from the tarpaulin he wore
  • one → I one = I, the Roman numeral
  • a → A the article is used as itself
  • embracing — an embrace closes arms round what it holds, so the sailor's letters finish up either side of the numeral

'Sailor' is one of the handful of words with a fixed crossword translation, worth learning beside 'worker' for ANT — three letters, always the same three.

12 across GERIATRIC

Rather ancient anger returning in Georgia with scheme falling short (9)

Definition: Rather ancient · reversal + container + charade + deletion

Geriatric properly belongs to the medicine of old age, and is used loosely of anything long past its prime, which is what 'rather ancient' asks for.

  • anger → ERI anger is ire, turned round
  • Georgia → GA GA is the postal abbreviation for the US state
  • scheme → TRIC a scheme is a trick, one letter short of the whole word
  • returning — anger that returns comes back the way it went, and in an across clue that means its letters read right to left
  • in — the bare preposition does the containing: what it introduces is placed between the two letters of the state
  • falling short — a word that falls short stops before its proper end, giving up its final letter

The Georgia wanted is the American state rather than the country by the Black Sea; setters use whichever of the two hands them the letters they need.

13 across BATHING MACHINE

Victorian device indicative of changing times? (7,7)

Definition: Victorian device · cryptic definition

It was a wheeled hut horses towed into the shallows so a swimmer could undress unseen, which makes it a device and squarely of its century.

  • Victorian device → the surface offers nineteenth-century machinery: an engine, a contraption with moving parts
  • indicative of changing times? → two readings sit here — an era in flux, and the plain business of changing your clothes on a beach

There is no wordplay to unpick, only a second reading to find: 'changing' is the getting-undressed sort, and the times are Victorian bathing manners rather than history.

17 across BUSMAN'S HOLIDAY

Vacation inspiring Sayers novel (7,7)

Definition: Vacation · cryptic definition

The phrase names time off spent doing the very job you are supposed to be off from, so 'Vacation' gives the category and the joke is in which sort.

  • Vacation → the surface promises an ordinary fortnight away from work
  • inspiring Sayers novel → Dorothy L. Sayers took this phrase for a 1937 Lord Peter Wimsey title, swapping its second word for Honeymoon

There are no letters to assemble: the first word is the definition and the rest is a literary nod, so this is a clue you finish from the crossing letters.

19 across VACILLATE

Be unsteady, being sick during leave (9)

Definition: Be unsteady · container

To vacillate is to waver between two courses, so the definition catches it as a verb phrase — unsteadiness of mind rather than of the feet.

  • leave → VACATE to leave a place is to vacate it
  • sick → ILL sick = ill
  • during — one thing that happens during another falls inside its span, so the illness is placed within the leave

'Leave' is a noun in the surface — the sick leave you book — and a verb in the wordplay.

22 across BEECH

Person who’s busy with letters on taps, wooden (5)

Definition: wooden · charade

Beech is the pale hardwood used for chairs, worktops and floors, so the adjective points at the answer as the stuff such things are made from.

  • Person who’s busy → BEE a busy bee is the standing phrase for someone who never stops
  • letters on taps → CH British taps are marked C for cold and H for hot

Taps carry exactly two letters, and spotting that pair is the way in. The comma after 'taps' is where the wordplay stops and the definition begins.

23 across POLY BAG

Activist willing to talk, but losing heart when backing common use of plastic (4,3)

Definition: common use of plastic · charade + deletion + reversal

A poly bag is the flimsy polythene carrier or wrapper that most plastic ends up as, which is exactly the everyday use the definition describes.

  • Activist → POL a pol is a politician, chiefly in American usage — the clue's political operator
  • willing to talk → YBAG willing to talk = gabby; the middle letter goes and what is left is turned round
  • losing heart — the heart of a word is the letter at its centre, and losing it takes just that one out
  • backing — in an across clue backing means the letters travel right to left, the order you would read them in a mirror

Two scraps of unfamiliar vocabulary meet here: 'pol' for a political operator, and 'poly' as the British shortening of polythene that names the thing in every shop.

24 across TRANCHE

Dazed state after consuming hot slice (7)

Definition: slice · container

Tranche is French for slice and English keeps it for a portion cut off a larger whole, usually of money, shares or debt.

  • Dazed state → TRANCE a dazed state is a trance, opened up before its last letter
  • hot → H h = hot, the letter on the tap
  • consuming — to consume a thing is to take it in and swallow it, so the letter disappears inside the word rather than following it

The surface has somebody eating in a daze, which is a long way from the boardroom and the kitchen where the answer's two everyday senses live.

25 across ROYAL GALA

Bit of apple glory, cultivated in the style of NZ variety (5,4)

Definition: NZ variety · anagram + first letter + charade

Royal Gala is the redder strain of the Gala apple bred in New Zealand, so the definition names the cultivar by where it comes from.

  • Bit of apple glory → AGLORY the first bit of 'apple' plus GLORY — six letters to rearrange
  • cultivated → anagram indicator
  • in the style of → ALA à la is French for in the style of, as in à la carte
  • Bit of — asking for a bit of a word takes only its opening letter, not the whole of it
  • cultivated — the gardening sense is the one at work: cultivated ground has been dug over and turned, and so have the letters beside it

An apple clue about apples: 'Bit of apple' is fodder rather than definition, and the shuffled letters run straight past the gap between the answer's two words.

26 across NIPPY

Feeling cold? Note fast-food shop, not church (5)

Definition: Feeling cold · charade + deletion

Nippy describes air cold enough to nip at fingers and ears, so the definition names the sensation the weather gives rather than the weather itself.

  • Note → N n = note, the dictionary abbreviation
  • fast-food shop → CHIPPY a chippy is a fish-and-chip shop, the British high street's own fast food
  • church → CH ch = church on maps and signposts — these are the letters taken away
  • not — the bare word is the deletion instruction: whatever it names next is absent from the shop

The chip shop carries the joke: strip the church abbreviation off its front and what remains describes the weather you queue in outside it.

Down

1 down MAPUTO

Prominent Communist holding position in African capital (6)

Definition: African capital · container

Maputo has been the capital of Mozambique, on the Indian Ocean coast, since independence, which is exactly what the definition asks for.

  • Prominent Communist → MAO Mao Zedong, the Communist leader setters reach for when they need three letters
  • position → PUT to put something somewhere is to position it
  • holding — a man holding something keeps it inside his grasp, so the piece named next is enclosed rather than tacked on

'Position' reads as a noun in the surface — a post held in the party — and the wordplay needs it as a verb instead.

2 down LACKADAISICALLY

Need a platform - supported by one Conservative associate ineffectually (15)

Definition: ineffectually · charade

Doing a thing lackadaisically is doing it listlessly, with no energy or care — which comes to the same as doing it ineffectually.

  • Need → LACK to lack something is to need it
  • a → A the article is taken exactly as it stands
  • platform → DAIS a dais is the low platform a speaker stands on
  • one → I one = I, the Roman numeral
  • Conservative → C C is the ballot-paper abbreviation for the Conservative party
  • associate → ALLY an ally is an associate
  • supported by — what supports a thing sits underneath it, and in a down clue underneath means later in the answer

Fifteen letters from six ordinary words laid end to end, and nothing is disturbed on the way. The hard part is hearing 'Need' as a verb rather than a noun.

3 down ARIMATHEA

Biblical city with one American subject in region (9)

Definition: Biblical city · container + charade

Arimathea is the town the gospels give as Joseph's home, so the definition points at a place known almost entirely from that one story.

  • region → AREA a region is an area; it splits open after its second letter
  • one → I one = I, the Roman numeral
  • American subject → MATH math is what Americans call the school subject Britons call maths
  • in — the bare preposition is the container instruction: what it introduces is dropped inside the region rather than added after it

'American subject' looks like it wants a citizen, and the word 'subject' is doing the pointing: it means a subject on the timetable, spelt the American way.

4 down CLING

Demanding to drop target? Hold on (5)

Definition: Hold on · deletion

To cling is to keep a grip and refuse to let go — what the definition's phrasal verb says of a child on a leg or a stubborn habit.

  • Demanding → CLAIMING someone demanding a right is claiming it
  • target → AIM your aim is your target — and these are the three letters that go
  • to drop — dropping a thing lets it fall away, so the letters named next are simply gone from the longer word

The question mark warns that this is not a sentence about artillery. 'Demanding' is the word to translate first, and everything after it is an instruction.

5 down ALFA ROMEO

A sign outside ranch, home to old car marque (4,5)

Definition: car marque · charade + container

Alfa Romeo is the Milanese car maker, and 'marque' is the trade's word for a manufacturer's badge rather than one of its models.

  • A → A the clue's opening word, used as itself
  • sign → LEO Leo the lion is a sign of the zodiac
  • ranch → FARM a ranch is a farm, opened up after its third letter
  • old → O o = old, as in OE for Old English
  • outside — the sign stands on the outside of what follows, wrapping round it instead of sitting beside it
  • home to — a place that is home to something has it living within, so the abbreviation goes inside the ranch

'Sign' has half a dozen crossword senses — omen, placard, gesture, symbol — and the zodiac is the one setters use most. The surface's ranch keeps the answer's Italian pedigree out of sight.

6 down SALSA

Son sadly getting half twisted in dance (5)

Definition: dance · charade + reversal

Salsa is the Cuban-rooted partner dance, and the music written for it, so the definition names the category the answer belongs to.

  • Son → S s = son, the family-tree abbreviation
  • sadly → ALAS sadly = alas; its first two letters stay put and its last two are the ones that turn
  • half twisted — 'half' limits what 'twisted' may touch: only the back two letters of the word are turned round, and the front stays where it is

Asking for half an operation is unusual and easy to miss: on its own 'twisted' would turn the whole word round, and here it reaches only as far as 'half' allows.

7 down A STORM IN A TEACUP

Crap to animate us immoderately? It’s nothing important (1,5,2,1,6)

Definition: nothing important · anagram

A storm in a teacup is a great commotion over a trifle, so the idiom names precisely the sort of fuss that turns out to be nothing important.

  • Crap to animate us → CRAPTOANIMATEUS anagram fodder, fifteen letters — exactly the enumeration
  • immoderately → anagram indicator
  • immoderately — to do a thing immoderately is to do it without restraint or proper order, and letters handled that way will not stay as they were given

A long anagram sitting in plain sight is the hardest kind to spot, because the fodder reads as an opinion about bad entertainment rather than as a bag of loose letters.

8 down EXPECTED

Former partner went to involve court, as anticipated (8)

Definition: anticipated · charade + container

As past participles the two are near-exact synonyms: something anticipated is something looked for in advance, which is what the answer says of an event.

  • Former partner → EX your ex is a former partner
  • went → PEED went = peed, the nursery past tense for going to the loo
  • court → CT ct is the standard abbreviation for court in an address
  • to involve — involving someone draws them into what you are already doing, so the abbreviation lands inside rather than after

'Went' for PEED is a small piece of crossword vocabulary worth storing away: setters use the polite nursery verb whenever those four letters are needed.

14 down NOSTALGIA

Reminiscences? No way seaweed will grip one (9)

Definition: Reminiscences · charade + container

Nostalgia is the wistful longing for the past that reminiscing produces, so the setter names the feeling by the memories people swap when they have it.

  • No → NO the word is used exactly as it stands
  • way → ST st = street, the crossword's usual way
  • seaweed → ALGA a single alga is one seaweed; the plural is algae
  • one → I one = I, the Roman numeral
  • grip — to grip a thing is to close round it, so the seaweed takes the numeral inside itself rather than alongside

'No way' is a single exclamation in the surface and two separate pieces in the wordplay, which is where the clue hides. The question mark warns the definition is loose.

15 down CHILBLAIN

Bill getting misplaced in sequence? It’s due to the cold (9)

Definition: It’s due to the cold · anagram + container

A chilblain is the itchy red swelling that cold, damp weather raises on fingers and toes, so the definition names the cause and lets you supply the effect.

  • sequence → CHAIN a sequence of linked things is a chain; it splits open after two letters
  • Bill → ILBL the four letters of the name, shuffled
  • getting misplaced — a thing misplaced has been put somewhere it does not belong, and letters put in the wrong places are letters to rearrange
  • in — the preposition does the inserting: the shuffled letters land inside the sequence rather than after it

The capital B makes Bill look like a man standing in a queue when he is only four letters to stir. Phi's question mark flags a definition that describes rather than translates.

16 down ABOVE PAR

A pig securing very old record? It’s better than average (5,3)

Definition: better than average · container + charade

In everyday speech the phrase means better than usual, though in golf a score above par is the worse one — the idiom keeps the general sense.

  • A → A the clue's first word, used as itself
  • pig → BOAR a boar is a male pig, opened up after two letters
  • very → V v = very, as in 'v good'
  • old record → EP an EP, or extended play, is a record format long out of date
  • securing — to secure something is to hold it fast inside your grasp, so the pig closes round the letters that follow

'Very old record' looks like one phrase about an antique disc and is really two separate jobs, which is the trap. Everything else is farmyard scenery.

18 down CHEEKY

Central heating smelly, not initially fresh (6)

Definition: fresh · charade + deletion

Cheeky means impudent in a likeable way, and 'fresh' carries the same charge of taking a liberty — the sense in 'don't get fresh with me', not newness.

  • Central heating → CH ch is the estate agent's abbreviation for central heating
  • smelly → EEKY smelly = reeky, robbed of its first letter
  • not initially — 'initially' points at a word's opening letter and 'not' takes it away, so the smell arrives without its R

The surface sells a flat with the heating on and a window that needs opening. 'Fresh' is the definition and it is the impudent sense, not the airy one.

20 down LABEL

Record company bringing up some imperishable ballad (5)

Definition: Record company · hidden word + reversal

A label is the firm that signs musicians and issues their records, so the definition is the music trade's own name for that business.

  • imperishable ballad → LABEL five consecutive letters lie across the join between these two words, running the wrong way
  • bringing up — in a down clue up means from the bottom towards the top, so the buried letters are read backwards
  • some — 'some' asks for a part of what follows rather than the whole of it, which is how a concealed run of letters is signalled

Long words are the best hiding places because nobody reads across them letter by letter, and the reversal keeps the run invisible even once you start looking.

21 down EXTRA

Edition of newspaper - English Times - reflected skill (5)

Definition: Edition of newspaper · charade + reversal

An extra is a special edition rushed out between the regular ones, the sense kept alive by the old newsboy's cry of 'Extra! Extra!'

  • English → E e = English, the usual abbreviation
  • Times → X times is the multiplication sign, x
  • skill → TRA skill is art, turned back to front
  • reflected — a reflection shows a thing the other way round, so the skill's letters are written in mirror order

The italics point straight at the newspaper, which is the wrong Times entirely: the wordplay wants the arithmetic one, and the italics are surface dressing.
