Across

1 across SOUP KITCHENS

Ultimately, Katie Hopkins cuts off support for the poor (4,8)

Definition: support for the poor · anagram + last letter

A soup kitchen hands out free meals to people who cannot afford food, so a row of them is support for the poor.

  • Katie → E The final letter of Katie joins the shuffle.
  • Hopkins cuts → HOPKINSCUTS Eleven letters of fodder, taken with the E.
  • Ultimately — Taken ultimately, a name is reduced to the single letter it finishes on rather than the whole of it.
  • off — Milk goes off when it turns bad, and letters that have gone off will not stay in the order they are given.

A newspaper columnist is being blamed for slashing the help available to people with nothing.

Katie Hopkins is a British newspaper columnist notorious for hard-hearted remarks about the poor, so the sentence reads as a headline about welfare cuts.

9 across GORED

Pierced from stab and stained with blood (5)

Definition: Pierced · charade

Gored is what a bull's horn does to whoever it catches, so it is pierced with a point driven right through.

  • stab → GO A stab at something is a go at it, an attempt.
  • stained with blood → RED Blood stains what it touches red.

A stabbing has left the victim and everything round them covered in blood.

'Stab' is the attempt rather than the knife — as in having a stab at it — and that everyday sense is the one the violent surface hides.

10 across ROUNDHAND

Way to write cards after game of golf (9)

Definition: Way to write · charade

Roundhand is a style of penmanship with rounded, evenly formed letters, so it is literally a way to write.

  • game of golf → ROUND A game of golf is a round.
  • cards → HAND The cards you are dealt are your hand.
  • after — Coming after the other piece puts these letters behind it rather than in front of it.

Somebody deals a hand of cards in the clubhouse once the golf is over.

The surface keeps everything at the golf club, where a round and a hand are both unremarkable, and nothing points at penmanship until the first two words.

11 across CHEVRON

Check – moving over knight, one might give away rank (7)

Definition: one might give away rank · charade + anagram

A chevron is the V-shaped stripe worn on a sleeve, and counting them tells anyone what rank the wearer holds.

  • Check → CH Ch. is the old chess notation for check.
  • over → EVRO The four letters of over, shifted into a new order.
  • knight → N A knight is N on the chessboard.
  • moving — Letters that are moving have left their places, so they arrive in a different order.

A chess player escapes check by moving over the knight and betrays their standing in the game.

Chess supplies both short pieces — ch. for check, N for the knight — and 'rank' is a row of the board on the surface and a soldier's stripe in the answer.

12 across HUNCHES

Hotel food around midday, that’s left out, curls up (7)

Definition: curls up · charade + deletion

Someone who hunches curls their shoulders up and forward, so 'curls up' describes the same movement of the body.

  • Hotel → H Hotel is H in the radio alphabet.
  • food around midday → UNCHES Lunches are the meals eaten around midday, with the L gone.
  • left → L Left is L, and that is the letter dropped.
  • out — A piece marked out is taken away from the letters rather than added to them.

A hotel serves lunch at noon and something has been left off the plate.

The seam is hidden by the comma: 'that's left out' reads as food nobody wanted, and it is the instruction to lose a letter.

13 across PHOTOSHOOT

A Spice Girl over making comeback around fiery studio session (10)

Definition: studio session · container + reversal + charade

A photoshoot is the booked session in which a photographer works through a set of pictures, usually in a studio.

  • A Spice Girl → POSH Victoria Beckham was Posh Spice, and she opens up to take the next piece in.
  • fiery → HOT Something fiery is hot.
  • over → OOT Over in the sense of excessively is 'too', turned back to front.
  • making comeback — A comeback is a return along the way it came, so these letters are read the other way round.
  • around — Going around something means closing over it, so the piece named next sits inside.

A Spice Girl returns to the limelight for a heated session in front of the cameras.

Posh Spice is Victoria Beckham; the harder word is 'over', standing in for 'too' in the sense it carries in over-eager.

15 across CHIN

Restrain having thrown a punch (4)

Definition: punch · deletion

To chin a person is to punch them on the jaw, the slang verb matching 'punch' used the same way.

  • Restrain → CHIN To restrain is to chain, and the A has been thrown out of it.
  • a → A The single letter A is what leaves.
  • having thrown — Something thrown away is gone, so the letter named after it is no longer in the word.

One fighter is being held back after landing a blow.

To chin someone is British slang for hitting them on the jaw, so the definition is a verb and 'Restrain' at the front is the wordplay, not the meaning.

18 across JUTS

Saucy thing admits backside in miniskirt sticks out (4)

Definition: sticks out · container + last letter

To jut is to project outwards from a surface, which is what sticking out means, and both are third-person verbs here.

  • Saucy thing → JUS A jus is a thin sauce, so it is a saucy thing indeed.
  • miniskirt → T The letter the word ends on.
  • admits — To admit something is to let it in, so these letters open up and take the next piece inside.
  • backside — The backside of a word is the letter sitting at the very end of it.

Someone risqué is showing off the back of a very short skirt.

'Saucy thing' plays as a flirt in the surface and as cookery in the wordplay — a jus is the pan juices poured over the meat.

19 across FANNY ADAMS

Nothing from lover, as many desperate to pick up date (5,5)

Definition: Nothing · charade + anagram

Fanny Adams, usually with 'sweet' in front, means nothing at all — the polite version of a much ruder phrase with the same initials.

  • lover → FAN A lover of a singer or a team is a fan.
  • as many → ASMANY Six letters of the shuffle, before the date joins them.
  • date → D D is the everyday abbreviation for date.
  • desperate — People in a desperate state are all over the place, and so are the letters standing beside the word.
  • to pick up — Picking a thing up gathers it in, so the letter named next joins the ones being shuffled.

A hopeful suitor gets no reply at all from the person they are trying to arrange an evening with.

'Sweet Fanny Adams' is British slang for nothing whatever, and it began as a grim Victorian joke about tinned meat named after a murder victim.

22 across SOD'S LAW

Typical bits of grass, garden getting trimmed (4,3)

Definition: Typical · charade + deletion

Sod's law is invoked at exactly the moment someone groans 'Typical!', so the exclamation names the principle by the reaction it produces.

  • bits of grass → SODS Squares of turf cut out of a field are sods.
  • garden → LAW The grassy part of a garden is the lawn, with its final letter cut off.
  • getting trimmed — A hedge getting trimmed comes back shorter, and so does this word — the letter on the end goes.

Somebody is out cutting turf and tidying the edges of the garden.

Sod's law is the British name for Murphy's law — whatever can go wrong will — and 'Typical!' is what people actually say when it does.

24 across MONITOR

Check dipstick in car? No time (7)

Definition: Check · container + deletion

To monitor something is to keep a check on it, watching it steadily to see how it changes.

  • car → MOOR A car is a motor, and the T has gone from it.
  • dipstick → NIT A dipstick is British slang for a fool, and so is a nit.
  • time → T T is the abbreviation for time, and that is the letter refused.
  • in — 'In' is the plainest container instruction there is: what is in something sits inside it.
  • No — Saying no to a thing refuses it entry, so the letter it names is not in the word.

A driver in a hurry checks the oil level before setting off.

'Dipstick' is the oil gauge in the surface and an idiot in the wordplay, which is a piece of slang a garage sentence can hide completely.

25 across POSTNATAL

Last panto rearranged after infant pulled out (9)

Definition: after infant pulled out · anagram

Postnatal means belonging to the time after a birth, which is the stretch that begins once the infant has been delivered.

  • Last panto → POSTNATAL These nine letters are exactly the ones required, in a different order.
  • rearranged — Something rearranged keeps all its parts and changes their arrangement, which is what happens to these letters.

The final pantomime of the season has been reorganised since a child dropped out of the cast.

'Pulled out' reads as a young performer leaving the show, and the definition needs it as a delivery — the baby is out and the clock starts.

26 across INEPT

Awkward to write about boring sex (5)

Definition: Awkward · container + reversal

Someone inept is clumsy and unskilled, awkward in the sense of being no good at the task rather than of feeling embarrassed.

  • sex → IT 'It' is the old euphemism, as in a film star having it.
  • write → NEP To write is to pen, turned about.
  • about — Turning about faces a word the other way, so its letters are read from the end back.
  • boring — To bore is to drive a hole into something, so these letters end up inside the other piece.

A clumsy novelist keeps writing about sex that bores everybody.

'Boring' looks like a verdict on the writing and is an instruction instead — one word bores its way into another.

27 across FOSTER PARENT

Guardian fellow sent a report for editing (6-6)

Definition: Guardian · anagram

A foster parent takes a child who is not theirs into their home and care, which is the job a guardian does.

  • fellow → F F is the standard abbreviation for fellow.
  • sent a report → SENTAREPORT Eleven letters of fodder, taken with the F.
  • editing — Editing a piece of copy moves its parts about, so the letters come out in a fresh order.

A man at the newspaper has filed his copy and the subeditors are working it over.

The capital G does the misdirecting: 'Guardian' reads as the paper the clue was printed in, and it means someone with legal care of a child.

1 down SCREEN OUT

Filter air – not at home (6,3)

Definition: Filter · charade

To screen something out is to filter it: a screen sifts what may pass and holds the rest back.

  • air → SCREEN To air a programme is to screen it — both are broadcasters' verbs.
  • not at home → OUT Someone not at home is out.

Purifying the air in a house while nobody is in it.

'Air' wants to be the stuff being filtered, the object of the sentence; the wordplay needs it as the verb a television schedule uses.

2 down UNDERDOG

Fail to warm up sufficiently before start of game – one’s likely to lose (8)

Definition: one’s likely to lose · charade + first letter

An underdog is the competitor everyone expects to be beaten, named by their prospects rather than by their form.

  • Fail to warm up sufficiently → UNDERDO To do food is to cook it, so to cook it too little is to underdo it.
  • game → G Only the initial letter of the word is wanted.
  • before — What stands before something stands ahead of it, so this chunk is written down first.
  • start of — The start of a word is the single letter it opens on, not the whole word.

A player skimps on the warm-up and walks out onto the pitch cold.

'Warm up' is doing two jobs: the surface has an athlete stretching, the wordplay has food that has not been cooked enough.

3 down KORAN

Before artist put on the canvas new religious work (5)

Definition: religious work · charade

The Koran is the scripture of Islam, and 'work' is the crossword's usual way of naming a book or composition without saying which kind.

  • put on the canvas → KO A boxer put on the canvas has been knocked out, which the record books write as KO.
  • artist → RA An artist may be a Royal Academician, RA after the name.
  • new → N N is the standard abbreviation for new.
  • Before — A thing set before another stands in front of it, so this chunk leads the assembly.

A painter is flattened in front of his easel before starting a fresh commission.

The canvas is two canvases — the painter's cloth in the surface, the floor of the ring in the wordplay — and 'artist' pulls hard towards the first.

4 down TOUCH DOWN

Feel sad? Come in (5,4)

Definition: Come in · charade

A plane comes in when it lands, and to touch down is exactly that moment of the wheels reaching the ground.

  • Feel → TOUCH To feel something is to touch it.
  • sad → DOWN Someone sad is down, low in spirits.

A miserable friend is standing on the doorstep and being waved inside.

An aircraft told to come in is being brought onto the runway, which is the sense the definition needs; the surface offers hospitality instead.

5 down HIDING

Beating from punch, getting cut before sound of the bell (6)

Definition: Beating · charade + deletion

A hiding is a thrashing, as in giving someone a hiding, so it matches 'Beating' as one noun for another.

  • punch → HI A punch is a hit, and the cut takes its last letter away.
  • sound of the bell → DING A bell goes ding.
  • getting cut — A word getting cut comes up short, losing the letter it ended on.
  • before — Standing before the other piece means standing ahead of it, so these letters come first.

A boxer takes a punch and is cut open just as the round ends.

Every word belongs to the ring — the punch, the cut, the bell — and the definition lives there too, so nothing in the sentence looks like an instruction.

6 down NEATH

Rugby Union club having straight rugby posts? (5)

Definition: Rugby Union club · charade

Neath RFC is a long-established Welsh rugby union club, so the answer is one particular club standing under the general description.

  • straight → NEAT A drink taken straight is taken neat, with nothing added to it.
  • rugby → The setter uses the word twice; here it only says which posts are meant, and hands over no letters.
  • posts? → H A set of posts is two uprights joined by a crossbar — the shape of the letter.

A rugby club is admired for the perfectly upright posts at either end of its pitch.

Neath is a town in south Wales whose side is one of the oldest in Welsh rugby, so the definition is a place name serving as a team.

7 down EGGCUP

Perhaps copper maintains good power – it might retain output from battery for consumer (6)

Definition: it might retain output from battery for consumer · charade + container

An eggcup holds a boiled egg upright at the table, and eggs are what battery hens produce for whoever eats them.

  • Perhaps → EG 'Perhaps' flags an example, which is written eg.
  • good → G G is the standard abbreviation for good, as on a school report.
  • copper → CU Copper's chemical symbol is Cu.
  • power → P P is the physicist's symbol for power.
  • maintains — To maintain a thing is to keep hold of it, so these letters close around the piece named next.

A police officer keeps a firm grip on power and on whatever the battery is putting out for the customer.

'Copper' is a policeman on the surface and the metal in the wordplay, and 'battery' pulls the same trick with electricity and hen sheds.

8 down ODDS-ON

Pretty certain con is set-up – sucker not Yankee (4-2)

Definition: Pretty certain · reversal + deletion + charade

A bet quoted odds-on is judged more likely to happen than not, and the phrase carries over to anything you would confidently expect.

  • con → OD A con is a do — a swindle — stood on its head.
  • sucker → DSON A Dyson is the British vacuum cleaner that sucks, one letter lighter.
  • Yankee → Y Yankee is Y in the radio alphabet, and that is the letter removed.
  • set-up — In a down clue, something set up has been stood on end, so its letters are read from the bottom.
  • not — Saying a thing is not something takes that named letter out of the word in front.

A confidence trick is exposed, and the mug caught up in it turns out not to be American.

Dyson is the British make of vacuum cleaner, which is the joke buried in 'sucker', and the radio alphabet supplies the Yankee that has to go.

14 down HEADWATER

Not initially bad weather for swimming in spring? (9)

Definition: spring · anagram + deletion

A headwater is the stream at a river's source, which is what a spring is — water rising at the top of the system.

  • bad weather → ADWEATHER Without the B it opens on, the phrase leaves exactly nine letters to be rearranged.
  • bad → B B is the initial letter that goes.
  • Not initially — 'Not initially' strikes out the letter the next word begins on.
  • swimming — Letters swimming have drifted loose and wash up in an order of their own.

The weather was fine enough at first for a swim, back in the spring.

'Spring' reads as the season right through the sentence, and the definition needs it as a place where water comes out of the ground.

16 down HEARTBEAT

Romeo during passion done in a flash (9)

Definition: a flash · container + charade

'In a heartbeat' is the idiom for doing something instantly, so a heartbeat stands for the flash of time it takes.

  • passion → HEAT Passion is heat, the metaphor English reaches for with strong feeling.
  • Romeo → R Romeo is R in the radio alphabet.
  • done in → BEAT Someone done in is beat — worn out.
  • during — What happens during something happens inside it, so this letter drops in among the others.

Romeo, caught up in a passionate affair, is worn out in no time at all.

Romeo is R in the radio alphabet, and 'done in' wants 'beat' in its slang sense of exhausted rather than the murderous one the surface hints at.

17 down MAINLINE

Ocean vessel docked for a major route (8)

Definition: a major route · charade + deletion

A mainline is the principal railway route between big cities, as against the branch lines that feed into it.

  • Ocean → MAIN The main is an old word for the open sea, as in the Spanish Main.
  • vessel → LINE A vessel is a liner, minus its final letter.
  • docked — A docked tail is a shortened one, so the word loses the letter at its end.

A ship has tied up at the quay after crossing the ocean.

'Docked' does both jobs at once, berthing the ship in the surface and shortening the liner in the wordplay, while 'ocean' asks for the poets' word for the sea.

18 down JOSEPH

Biblical figure formed from rib inspiring epistle (6)

Definition: Biblical figure · container

Joseph is a Bible name twice over — Jacob's son with the coat, and the carpenter of the nativity — so either one answers to 'Biblical figure'.

  • rib → JOSH To rib someone is to josh them, teasing rather than wounding.
  • epistle → EP Ep. is the standard short form of epistle.
  • inspiring — To inspire is to breathe in, so the word draws the piece named next inside itself.

A Bible character is made out of a rib while an apostle's letter is read aloud.

The rib is Adam's on the surface, which points hard at Eve; in the wordplay it is a verb, and to rib is to tease.

20 down SCROTA

They hold balls in opening of Royal Ascot racing (6)

Definition: They hold balls · anagram + first letter

Scrota is the plural of scrotum, the sac that holds the testicles, so the plural definition points at more than one owner.

  • Royal → R The initial letter of the word.
  • Ascot → ASCOT Five letters of fodder, taken with the R.
  • opening of — The opening of a word is the single letter it starts on.
  • racing — Letters racing are running about, and they cross the line in a different order.

The first race of the week is under way at Royal Ascot.

Royal Ascot is Britain's grandest and most formal race meeting, so 'opening of Royal Ascot' reads as the first day of it rather than as one letter.

21 down PLANET

Body in zone for running during training (6)

Definition: Body · container

A planet is a heavenly body, and 'body' alone is the astronomer's ordinary word for anything of that kind in orbit.

  • training → PT PT is physical training, what British schools call the gym lesson.
  • zone for running → LANE A runner's own zone on the track is their lane.
  • during — A thing done during a session is done in the middle of it, so these letters sit inside.

An athlete keeps strictly to their own lane during a training session.

'Body' is not the athlete's body the surface is describing but a heavenly one, which is the only word in the clue that leaves the running track.

23 down DISCO

Criticise old following Conservative Party (5)

Definition: Party · charade

A disco is a party where records are played for dancing, so 'party' is the social sense rather than the political one the surface offers.

  • Criticise → DIS To dis someone is to criticise or disrespect them.
  • Conservative → C C is the standard abbreviation for Conservative.
  • old → O O is the standard abbreviation for old.
  • following — What follows another piece is written after it, so this letter takes the later place.

A veteran Tory grandee is being attacked by his own side.

'Conservative Party' looks like one phrase and the split falls between its two words — the party at the end is the dancing kind.

24 down MELBA

Old singer, ace, supporting Spice Girl (5)

Definition: Old singer · charade

Nellie Melba was the reigning operatic soprano either side of 1900, so 'Old singer' places her by period rather than by voice.

  • Spice Girl → MELB Melanie Brown of the Spice Girls was billed as Mel B.
  • ace → A A is the standard abbreviation for ace.
  • supporting — In a down clue whatever is supporting the rest sits underneath it, so this letter comes last.

A great singer of the old days is reduced to opening for a Spice Girl.

Peach Melba and Melba toast are both named after Dame Nellie, which is how well known the singer the definition wants once was.
