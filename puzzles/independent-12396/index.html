Independent Cryptic Crossword No 12,396

Across

1 across SMARTS

American Intelligence stings (6)

Definition: American Intelligence · double definition

Two senses of one word: in the US it means brains or nous, and as a verb it is what a graze does.

  • American Intelligence → SMARTS In US usage the word means brains or nous, as in the street variety.
  • stings → SMARTS A grazed knee is said to do this: the verb for a sharp, lingering pain.

The capital I is the trap: 'American Intelligence' offers you the CIA, and the answer is only the everyday word for brains.

5 across ENCROACH

Intrude and hence, lacking boundaries, run over a child (8)

Definition: Intrude · charade + middle letters

To encroach is to intrude — both mean pushing past a boundary onto ground that is not yours.

  • hence, lacking boundaries → ENC Take the first and last letters off the little adverb and three letters remain.
  • run → R R is a run in cricket scoring, the abbreviation printed on any scorecard.
  • over → O An over is six balls in cricket and shortens to O, keeping the same sporting picture.
  • a → A The article is taken at face value as the single letter it looks like.
  • child → CH Ch. is the standard abbreviation for child, as printed on a family booking form.
  • lacking boundaries — A word's boundaries are its outermost letters, so a word lacking them is left with only its middle.

'run over a child' reads as a road accident, which is exactly the point: it is really three separate pieces of shorthand sitting in a row.

9 across LAND

Boy covering new ground (4)

Definition: ground · container

Ground and land are the same stuff underfoot — 'solid ground', 'dry land' — both plain nouns for the earth's surface.

  • Boy → LAD A boy is a lad, and here it is the outer word.
  • new → N N is the standard abbreviation for new, as on a product label.
  • covering — Something that covers goes over and around whatever it hides, so the first word wraps the letter that follows.

Four-letter containers are always tight: only one letter can possibly go in, so the work is deciding which word is the wrapper.

10 across IMPALEMENT

Device to protect a piercing (10)

Definition: piercing · container

Impalement is being run through on a spike, so it is a piercing in the most literal and unpleasant sense of that noun.

  • Device → IMPLEMENT A device is an implement, the tool sense, and it is the word that opens up.
  • a → A The article is taken at face value as the single letter it looks like.
  • to protect — To protect something is to put yourself all the way round it, which is where the loose letter ends up.

The surface offers body jewellery; the answer is the medieval punishment. Hiding a bare 'a' inside a nine-letter word is the move to learn here.

11 across HIS AND HERS

Welcome tools to conserve bit of harmony for a married couple? (3,3,4)

Definition: for a married couple? · charade + container + first letter

A whole-phrase idiom: matching towels, bathrobes or car keys owned by a couple, not two pronouns being read separately.

  • Welcome → HI A welcome is a greeting, and the shortest greeting there is runs to two letters.
  • tools → SANDERS Sanders are power tools for smoothing wood, and they open up to admit a letter.
  • bit of harmony → H Only the opening letter of the noun that follows is wanted.
  • to conserve — To conserve something is to keep it safely inside, so the word before closes around the letter after.
  • bit of — A bit of a word is just its opening letter, the smallest piece a clue can take from it.

Sanders are the tools, not a surname, and harmony is here purely to donate its H. The question mark owns up to a whole-phrase definition.

12 across STYX

Picked up pieces of wood in river with ferry (4)

Definition: river with ferry · homophone

The mythological river the ferryman Charon rows the dead across, so 'ferry' narrows the definition to one river in particular.

  • pieces of wood → STYX Pieces of wood are sticks, and said aloud that is what you write into the grid.
  • Picked up — Something picked up is something heard, so the clue wants the sound of the words rather than their spelling.

Charon ferries the dead across this river in Greek myth, which is why 'with ferry' is doing real work — it pins down which river is meant.

13 across HELSINKI

Man left to invest independent capital (8)

Definition: capital · charade

Definition by example: the answer is Finland's capital city, one instance of the class the single word names.

  • Man → HE A man is he, the pronoun standing in for the noun.
  • left → L L is left, the standard direction abbreviation.
  • invest → SINK To invest money in a scheme is to sink it into one.
  • independent → I I is independent, as printed beside the name of an MP who belongs to no party.

'Man left' wants to be the Isle of Man, a favourite crossword island, losing something. It is only a pronoun and a direction letter.

16 across INSTAL

Set up home next to salt supply (6)

Definition: Set up · charade + anagram

To instal something is to set it up in place — the sense used of software, of equipment, and of a new bishop.

  • home → IN Someone at home is in, the crossword's usual two letters for it.
  • salt → STAL The four letters of the seasoning, resettled.
  • next to — Next to means side by side, so the two pieces are simply laid against each other in the order given.
  • supply — Not the noun: this is the adverb from 'supple', said supp-lee, and letters handled flexibly bend into a new order.

The one-L spelling is the older form and still good in Chambers, which is what lets six letters do a job you would expect to need seven.

18 across LANDAU

Win gold carriage (6)

Definition: carriage · charade

Definition by example: a landau is one particular horse-drawn carriage, so the clue names the class and the answer supplies a member.

  • Win → LAND To land a contract or a job is to win it.
  • gold → AU Au is gold's chemical symbol, from the Latin aurum.

The answer is a four-wheeled horse-drawn carriage with a folding hood, the kind royal processions still use.

20 across TELECAST

Programme broadcast from the empty abandoned castle (8)

Definition: Programme broadcast · charade + outer letters + anagram

A telecast is a programme sent out over television, so the definition names the thing and the medium carrying it at once.

  • the empty → TE The three-letter article, emptied of its middle, keeps its two ends.
  • abandoned castle → LECAST The six letters of the building, resettled in a new order.
  • empty — A word emptied out keeps its walls and loses its insides, so only the first and last letters survive.
  • abandoned — Abandoned describes behaviour with no restraint left, and letters behaving that way will not stay where they were put.

'broadcast' looks like the shuffle instruction and is not — it belongs to the definition. The word doing the shuffling is 'abandoned'.

22 across RAPS

Knocks box over (4)

Definition: Knocks · reversal

Raps are sharp knocks — knuckles on a door — so noun and verb both line up with the plural definition.

  • box → RAPS To box is to spar, and the letters of that verb are read the other way round.
  • over — Turning a thing over shows you its far side, so the letters are read from the other end.

Three words and one mechanism, which is what makes it hard: 'box' has to be read as a verb, not as a container.

24 across HACKSAWING

Journalist spotted popular German cutting things up (10)

Definition: cutting things up · charade

The answer is cutting things up with a hacksaw, so the definition gives the general activity and the answer names the tool doing it.

  • Journalist → HACK A hack is a jobbing journalist, mildly insulting and very common in clues.
  • spotted → SAW Spotted in the sense of caught sight of is saw.
  • popular → IN Something popular is in, as in fashion.
  • German → G G is the dictionary abbreviation for German.

Nothing here tells you to shuffle, hide or turn anything: four plain synonyms laid end to end, which makes it the purest charade in the puzzle.

27 across CHRONICLES

Accounts from inveterate liar especially suspicious, initially (10)

Definition: Accounts · charade + first letters

A chronicle is a written account of events in time order, so the plural sits on 'Accounts' in its record-keeping sense rather than its financial one.

  • inveterate → CHRONIC An inveterate habit is a chronic one: long-standing and not shifting.
  • liar especially suspicious → LES Three words, three opening letters, taken in the order they appear.
  • initially — It asks for beginnings only, and it governs all three of the words in front of it rather than just the nearest.

A single 'initially' can govern a whole run of words — three of them here — and stopping one word short is where most solvers come unstuck.

28 across EURO

Oscar, with regret, rebuffed French bread (4)

Definition: French bread · charade + reversal

Bread is slang for money and the euro is France's currency, so the two words work together as one definition.

  • Oscar → O Oscar is O in the NATO phonetic alphabet.
  • regret → RUE To rue something is to regret it.
  • rebuffed — To rebuff is to turn something back, so the assembled letters are read in reverse.

'French bread' is the joke and the definition at once: bread is slang for money, so what is wanted is a currency, not a baguette.

29 across ANGLESEY

Island linked by bridge to mainland transformed Senegal close to Saly (8)

Definition: Island linked by bridge to mainland · anagram + last letter

The Welsh island joined to the mainland by the Menai Bridge, so the crossing is what narrows the definition to this one island.

  • Senegal → SENEGAL The country's name supplies seven of the eight letters needed.
  • close to Saly → Y Saly is a resort on the Senegalese coast, and only its last letter is taken.
  • transformed — Something transformed has come out in a different shape from the one it went in as, which is what happens to these letters.
  • close to — It points at the end of the word that follows, taking that word's final letter and nothing else.

The definition runs to six words, which is unusually long and therefore easy to miss: everything from 'Island' to 'mainland' is describing, not clueing.

30 across PELLET

Shot Swiss folk hero returning after exercise (6)

Definition: Shot · charade + reversal

A pellet is a small shot fired from an air rifle or a shotgun, so 'Shot' names the projectile itself.

  • exercise → PE PE is physical education, the school timetable's exercise lesson.
  • Swiss folk hero → LLET The apple-shooting hero of Swiss legend is Tell, and his name is turned round.
  • returning — Something returning comes back the way it went, so the name is read from its far end.
  • after — It fixes the order: the reversed name goes behind the two letters, so the exercise comes first.

William Tell shot an apple off his son's head, so 'Shot' as the definition keeps the surface picture running right to the end.

Down

2 down MIAMI

Setter's repeatedly turned over ace in US city (5)

Definition: US city · reversal + container

Definition by example: the answer is one Florida city among many, so the clue points at the class rather than naming it.

  • Setter's repeatedly → IMIM The setter speaking of himself is I'M, and the clue asks for that twice over.
  • ace → A An ace is A, the card's own letter, and it lands in the middle of the reversed pair.
  • repeatedly — Doing something repeatedly means doing it more than once, so the two letters are written out twice before anything else happens.
  • turned over — Turning a thing over shows you its other face, and letters shown that way are read from the far end back.
  • in — This is a placement instruction rather than joinery: the single letter drops inside the pair instead of joining at either end.

Whenever a clue mentions the setter, expect I, ME or I'M — it is one of the conventions that pays for itself every week.

3 down REDCAPS

Military police officers scraped around (7)

Definition: Military police officers · anagram

A redcap is a British Army military policeman, nicknamed for the red cover worn over the cap.

  • scraped → SCRAPED All seven letters of this one word are the material, with nothing added or dropped.
  • around — Here it means all over the place rather than encircling, and letters strewn about like that come to rest in a new order.

British military police are nicknamed for the scarlet covers on their caps, which is what the definition is pointing at.

4 down SHIED

Threw Eccles in outhouse (5)

Definition: Threw · container

To shy something is to fling it, so the past tense sits straight on 'Threw'; a horse shying away is the decoy sense.

  • outhouse → SHED An outhouse is a shed, and it is the word that opens up to take a letter.
  • Eccles → I Eccles is the setter of this puzzle, and a setter naming himself is I.
  • in — It places one letter inside the other word rather than beside it, which is what makes this a container and not a charade.

Setters slip their own pseudonym into the grid like this fairly often — the name printed above the puzzle is fair game as wordplay.

5 down EUPHEMISTICALLY

Suggestively, Brussels drug-dealer quietly replacing Conservative in charge with friend (15)

Definition: Suggestively · charade + substitution

Speaking euphemistically is speaking suggestively — hinting at the blunt thing rather than naming it, so both adverbs describe the same sideways manner.

  • Brussels → EU Brussels is where the European Union is headquartered, so the city stands for the union.
  • drug-dealer quietly replacing Conservative → PHEMIST A drug-dealer is a chemist in British usage — the high-street pharmacist — and P for quiet takes over its C for Conservative.
  • in charge → IC In charge shortens to i/c on a duty roster, the officer's abbreviation.
  • with friend → ALLY An ally is a friend, especially the political kind.
  • replacing — One letter stands in for another: the quiet man's letter moves into the slot the Conservative's held, and the rest of the word is untouched.

The joke is 'chemist' for drug-dealer, a British pharmacy rather than a cartel; P for quiet comes from the piano marking in written music.

6 down CELESTIAL

Surprisingly, Stella with ice is heavenly (9)

Definition: heavenly · anagram

Heavenly and celestial both mean 'of the heavens'; celestial is the technical one, used by astronomers of bodies in the sky.

  • Stella with ice → STELLAICE Nine letters between the name and the word after it, every one of them reused.
  • Surprisingly — Something surprising has not come out the way it was set down, which is the cue to resettle the letters.

Stella is a lager as well as a girl's name, so the surface pours a drink over ice while the letters quietly go somewhere else.

7 down OSMOSIS

Method for passing through universe is not constant (7)

Definition: Method for passing through · deletion + charade

Osmosis is the process by which a solvent crosses a semi-permeable membrane, so 'passing through' names the mechanism directly.

  • constant → C C is the physicist's constant, the speed of light — and it is the letter this clue takes out.
  • universe → OSMOS The universe is the cosmos, less the C just named.
  • is → IS The linking verb is taken literally, two letters tacked on the end.
  • not — It is the removal instruction: the word after it names something the wordplay then does without.

'universe is not constant' reads as cosmology and is the false path here; the science in the definition, water crossing a membrane, is entirely literal.

8 down CANNY

City supporting John Sharp (5)

Definition: Sharp · charade

Canny means shrewd or quick-witted: the 'sharp' of a sharp operator rather than the sharpness of a blade.

  • John → CAN The john and the can are both American slang for the lavatory.
  • City → NY NY is New York, the city so good they named it twice.
  • supporting — In a down clue, one thing supporting another sits underneath it, so the second piece goes below the first.

'John Sharp' reads as somebody's name, which is the trap — John is the lavatory and Sharp is the definition standing on its own.

14 down ERA

Old Queen Anne's first important date (3)

Definition: important date · charade + first letter

An era is the point from which a run of years is reckoned, so 'date' is meant in its calendar sense rather than as a span.

  • Old Queen → ER ER is Elizabeth Regina, and 'old' marks a queen who no longer reigns.
  • Anne's first → A The opening letter of the name is all that is wanted.
  • first — It takes only the opening letter of the name in front of it, not the whole word.

ER for the Queen is one of the conventions worth memorising outright — it is stamped on British postboxes. Queen Anne is only the surface.

15 down NOUGHTIES

Sufficient late-starting matches for a decade (9)

Definition: a decade · deletion + charade

What Britain calls the ten years from 2000, after the two zeros — noughts — standing in the middle of every one of those dates.

  • Sufficient → NOUGH Sufficient is enough, and beginning one letter in leaves five of its six.
  • matches → TIES Matches that finish level are ties, the sporting sense of the word.
  • late-starting — To start late is to not start at the beginning, so the word's opening letter is left behind.

The Noughties are the years 2000 to 2009, named after the noughts in the dates — 'nought' being the British word for zero.

17 down ASS

Idiot in Kansas oddly ignored (3)

Definition: Idiot · alternate letters

The animal is a donkey, and calling a person one has meant 'fool' in English for centuries — a transferred insult, not a synonym.

  • Kansas → ASS Count into the state's name and keep the second, fourth and sixth letters.
  • oddly ignored — Ignore the odd-numbered letters and the even-numbered ones are left, read in the order they already stand.

Kansas is doing nothing but supplying letters; the picture of an idiot in Kansas has no bearing at all on the mechanism.

19 down DESPOIL

I sloped off to strip (7)

Definition: strip · anagram

To despoil a place is to strip it of everything of value, so both verbs describe plundering rather than undressing.

  • I sloped → ISLOPED Seven letters, the pronoun included, all reused in a new order.
  • off — Milk that has gone off has spoiled out of its proper state, and letters off in that way will not keep their order.

'Sloped off' is a single idiom in the surface — someone sneaking away — and prising the two words apart is the move the clue is asking for.

21 down COWHEEL

Company push source of gelatine (7)

Definition: source of gelatine · charade

Boiled cow heel yields the collagen that sets as gelatine, a staple of old British kitchens, so the definition is plain fact.

  • Company → CO Co. is the abbreviation printed after a firm's name.
  • push → WHEEL To wheel a barrow or a bike along is to push it.

The answer is the foot of a cow, boiled in old British cookery for the jelly it gives up, so the definition is doing no work at all beyond the literal.

23 down ASHEN

Very pale, like bride-to-be? (5)

Definition: Very pale · charade

Ashen is the grey-white colour of ash, which is what a face turns when the blood drains out of it.

  • like → AS 'like' in the sense of 'in the manner of' is AS.
  • bride-to-be → HEN She is the hen of a hen party, the British night out before a wedding.

The question mark owns up to the stretch: a bride-to-be is a hen only inside the phrase 'hen night', and only in Britain.

25 down SYSOP

Extremely silly to soak network manager (5)

Definition: network manager · charade + outer letters

Short for system operator, the person who administers a networked system, so 'network manager' is that job described in plain words.

  • Extremely silly → SY The outermost letters of the adjective, in the order they stand.
  • to soak → SOP To sop is to soak up liquid, as bread sops gravy.
  • Extremely — The extremes of a word are its two end letters, so only the first and last of the adjective are taken.

The answer is short for system operator, the person who ran a bulletin board — a job title that survives from the dial-up era.

26 down NURSE

Treat back sores, running somewhat (5)

Definition: Treat · hidden word + reversal

To nurse an injury, or a grudge, is to treat and tend it, so the verb sits straight on the definition.

  • sores, running → NURSE Five consecutive letters straddle the comma between these two words, and the clue asks you to read them the other way.
  • back — Going back means retracing the same letters in the opposite direction to the one they are printed in.
  • somewhat — Somewhat means only part of what is written, which points at a run of letters inside the words rather than the words themselves.

Two instructions stack up: find the hidden run first, then turn it round. 'Treat back sores' reads as a medical complaint, which is the disguise.
