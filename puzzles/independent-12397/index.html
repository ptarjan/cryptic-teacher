1 across LADDISH

Macho director hosted by Hollywood Bowl? (7)

Definition: Macho · charade + container

LADDISH means macho in the beer-and-banter sense, so the adjective swaps straight in for the clue's word.

  • Hollywood → LA Hollywood is a district of Los Angeles, which crosswords shorten to the city's two initials.
  • director → D On a film credit the director is the standing single letter D.
  • Bowl → DISH A bowl on the table is a dish.
  • hosted by — A host takes a guest in and puts them up inside, so the letters either side close around whatever is being hosted.

A film director being wined and dined at a concert at the Hollywood Bowl.

The Hollywood Bowl is one Los Angeles venue, which is what makes the two words look like a single name; the setter splits them and uses each half separately.

5 across MEDUSAS

Our people getting into sea like jellyfish (7)

Definition: jellyfish · container

A medusa is the free-swimming bell stage of a jellyfish, so the plural covers several of the creatures.

  • sea → MED The Med is the everyday short name for the Mediterranean.
  • Our people → US Our people are us, and the word goes in exactly as written.
  • like → AS Like in the sense of in the manner of is as.
  • getting into — Getting into something puts you bodily inside it, so this piece is planted between the letters either side.

Holidaymakers wading into the sea and drifting about like jellyfish.

'Sea like' looks as though it is finishing a simile; it is two unrelated scraps of wordplay with the swimmers dropped in between them.

9 across VERTIGINOUS

Overusing it could make you dizzy (11)

Definition: dizzy · anagram

A VERTIGINOUS drop is a dizzy one, both adjectives describing the head swimming at a height.

  • Overusing it → VERTIGINOUS Eleven letters sit in those two words, and eleven is what the enumeration wants.
  • could make you — 'Could make' says the letters in front of it are raw material, capable of turning into something else.

A warning that too much of something will leave you giddy.

The clue is nearly all one sentence: the same two words that hand over the letters also describe what the answer does to a person.

10 across ORC

Tolkien creature has ring before Return of the King (3)

Definition: Tolkien creature · charade + reversal

ORCS are the goblin foot-soldiers Tolkien invented for Middle-earth, so the phrase names the species outright.

  • ring → O A ring drawn on the page is a circle, the letter O.
  • the King → RC Britain's king signs himself Charles Rex, CR, and that pair arrives here the other way about.
  • before — Before is positional: the piece named ahead of it is entered first, at the front.
  • Return of — A return is a coming back the way you came, so the letters named are written in the opposite order.

A Tolkien monster laying hands on a ring before the last film of the trilogy.

The Ring and The Return of the King are Tolkien's own titles, so the clue reads as a plot summary while both halves quietly do wordplay.

11 across SCUM

Character opening seedy clubs let me see dirty film (4)

Definition: dirty film · charade + first letter

SCUM is the filthy film that forms on the surface of a pond or a bath, a plain synonym once the film is not celluloid.

  • seedy → S Only the letter at the front of this word is wanted.
  • clubs → C C is clubs on a bridge scoresheet, one of the four card suits crosswords lean on.
  • let me see → UM The noise a person makes while thinking is written um.
  • Character opening — The character that opens a word is its very first letter, and that letter alone is taken.

A shady character breaking into a nightclub to watch a blue movie.

'Character' wants to be a person in the film the surface is about; it means a letter, and the 'dirty film' at the end is the greasy layer on standing water.

12 across CHAMELEON

Blender juiced each lemon (9)

Definition: Blender · anagram

A CHAMELEON shifts colour to melt into its background, so it is literally a thing that blends.

  • each lemon → CHAMELEON Nine letters between the two words, which is exactly the length wanted.
  • juiced — Fruit that has been juiced has been pulped, and pulped letters come out in a different order.

A kitchen gadget pulping citrus.

'Blender' reads as the appliance the lemons are heading into, which is the whole misdirection: it is the definition and no part of the machinery.

13 across COWPAT

Flat deposit previously held in stock (6)

Definition: Flat deposit previously held in stock · cryptic definition

The answer is the flat dropping a cow leaves in a field, matter that genuinely was held inside the stock beforehand.

  • Flat deposit → The surface offers money put down on a rented flat; read plainly it is something flat that has been deposited on the ground.
  • previously held in stock → A shop holds goods in stock, but stock is also livestock, and this was held inside the animal until the field received it.

An estate agent's ledger: a deposit on an apartment that used to sit in shares.

Stock is the hinge: shares in the surface picture, cattle in the real one. There is no wordplay to unpick, only the one sense giving way to the other.

15 across EN SUITE

European on vacation mentioned charming private facilities (2,5)

Definition: private facilities · charade + outer letters + homophone

An EN SUITE is the bathroom opening straight off a bedroom, reserved for whoever is in that room.

  • European on vacation → EN Empty the middle out of the word and its first and last letters are left holding the ends.
  • charming → SUITE Charming means sweet, and that noise said aloud is written the hotel way.
  • on vacation — A word on vacation has been emptied out, leaving only the letters standing at each end.
  • mentioned — Mentioned means said out loud, so what follows is a sound that gets respelled.

A holidaying European praising a charming private bathroom.

'European on vacation' is a whole picture in itself, which is what buries the instruction inside it: the vacation is an emptying, not a holiday.

18 across ANYWAYS

Crudely yawn and say "where was I?" (7)

Definition: where was I? · anagram

ANYWAYS is the colloquial marker you drop in to pick a story back up after wandering off it, which is what the question does.

  • yawn and say → ANYWAYS The words either side of 'and' supply all seven letters; the joiner itself contributes none.
  • Crudely — Done crudely, something is thrown together roughly, and letters thrown together lose the order they arrived in.

Someone yawning through their own story and losing the thread of it.

'Crudely' is doing two jobs at once: it shuffles the letters, and it warns that the answer is the rough, non-standard form of a very ordinary word.

20 across SILVER

A little utensil Verdi used as top conductor (6)

Definition: top conductor · hidden word

SILVER conducts electricity better than any other metal, so 'top' is a literal ranking rather than praise for a maestro.

  • utensil Verdi → SILVER The letters run on unbroken out of the end of one word and into the start of the next.
  • A little — A little of something is a short run taken from inside it rather than the whole of it.

A small piece of cutlery that Verdi once waved as a baton.

'Conductor' is the pivot: the surface wants Verdi on the podium, and the definition wants the metal that carries a current better than any other.

21 across BREAK A LEG

Cast member after being told this? (5,1,3)

Definition: Cast member after being told this? · cryptic definition

The answer is the traditional pre-show wish to an actor, and obeying it would put a member in a cast, which is how the clue reads both ways at once.

  • Cast member → The surface wants an actor in the cast of a play; the other reading is a limb, a member, sitting in plaster.
  • after being told this? → 'This' points at the answer itself, the thing said to a performer before curtain-up, which taken at its word lands them in that plaster.

An actor in a company, and whatever it is somebody has just wished them.

There is no wordplay to take apart here. The wish is theatre superstition, since saying good luck is held to invite the opposite, and the clue takes it literally.

23 across TOFU

Vegan food fight's ending with saucer being thrown back (4)

Definition: Vegan food · charade + last letter + reversal

TOFU is bean curd, the soya block that stands in for meat in vegan cooking.

  • fight's ending → T Take only the last letter of the word in front of it.
  • saucer → OFU A flying saucer is a UFO, and this one comes in back to front.
  • ending — The ending of a word is its final letter, and that is all that gets taken from it.
  • being thrown back — Thrown back means sent the way it came, so across the grid this piece is written right to left.

A canteen food fight finishing with crockery sailing back across the room.

The food fight and the flying plate are one picture, so the saucer looks like china; the saucer that matters is the kind that flies of its own accord.

25 across RIA

One described by "river area" (3)

Definition: One described by "river area" · &lit + container

The whole clue reads straight: a ria is a river's flooded lower valley, an arm of the sea, which the quoted phrase describes exactly.

  • river → R R is the map abbreviation for river.
  • One → I One as a Roman numeral is the single stroke I.
  • area → A A is the standing abbreviation for area.
  • described by — In geometry a figure is described about another, drawn right round the outside of it, so the first piece ends up enclosed.

A RIA is a drowned river valley open to the sea, so the quoted phrase is a straight definition as well as the source of the letters wrapped round the one.

26 across TENNIS ELBOW

Joint liability established by court appearances? (6,5)

Definition: liability · charade

A nagging injury that stops you playing properly is a liability in the everyday sense of a handicap, not the lawyer's sense the surface pushes.

  • court appearances? → TENNIS Appearing on court, in the sporting rather than the legal sense, means playing the racket game.
  • Joint → ELBOW The joint wanted is the hinge halfway down your arm.

A shared legal responsibility settled by turning up in court.

Every word points at the law — liability, established, court appearances — and none of it is legal; the court is the one with a net across the middle.

27 across ENLARGE

Increase in volume startled Al Green (7)

Definition: Increase in volume · anagram

To ENLARGE something is to make it bigger in bulk, volume in the geometric rather than the audio sense.

  • Al Green → ENLARGE The soul singer's two names hold exactly the seven letters needed.
  • startled — Something startled jumps and scatters, and scattered letters come to rest in a new order.

Turning the music up until the soul singer jumps.

'Increase in volume' sounds like reaching for the stereo, especially with a singer named next to it; volume here is bulk, the space a thing takes up.

28 across GOSPELS

John and the other three ultimately planning to dress up like colourful soldiers (7)

Definition: John and the other three · last letters

John is one of the four GOSPELS, so he plus the remaining three name the set exactly.

  • planning to dress up like colourful soldiers → GOSPELS Seven words in a row, each contributing the letter it ends with, taken in the order they are printed.
  • ultimately — Ultimately means at the very end, so each word gives up only the letter it finishes on.

A group deciding to turn up dressed as brightly coloured soldiers.

'John and the other three' pulls you towards the Beatles, and the costume in the surface is Sgt Pepper; the four actually meant are the evangelists.

1 down LIVE

Wicked's Defying Gravity performed on stage (4)

Definition: performed on stage · reversal

A LIVE show is one happening in front of you rather than recorded, which is what performing on stage means.

  • Wicked's → LIVE Wicked gives EVIL, which this down entry meets climbing back up.
  • Defying Gravity — Whatever defies gravity is heading upwards, and upwards in a down entry means reading the letters back up the column.

A song from the musical Wicked being belted out in the theatre.

Defying Gravity is a song from Wicked, so the two italicised titles read as one theatre sentence and only the first of them is doing any work.

2 down DIRECTORY

Awful politician takes cocaine in a bunch of addresses (9)

Definition: a bunch of addresses · charade + container

A DIRECTORY is the book that lists names beside their addresses, so it is a bunch of addresses bound together.

  • Awful → DIRE Something awful is dire.
  • cocaine → C C is the standing crossword abbreviation for cocaine.
  • politician → TORY A Tory is a member of the British Conservative party, one of the two politicians crosswords reach for.
  • takes — To take something is to let it inside you, so the surrounding letters open up and admit the piece named next.

A grim MP caught taking drugs while giving a run of speeches.

The addresses of the definition are the postal kind, not the ones a politician delivers, and the surface leans on the wrong sense the whole way.

3 down IDIOM

Phrase Methuselah had (flipping pompous way of referring to himself) (5)

Definition: Phrase · charade + reversal

An IDIOM is a set phrase whose meaning is not the sum of its words, so the two nouns swap freely.

  • Methuselah had → ID Methuselah is the setter of this puzzle, so he says I had the contracted way, I'D.
  • pompous way of referring to himself → IOM The self-important speaker calls himself MOI, French for me, and it goes in turned about.
  • flipping — To flip a thing is to turn it over, so the word it points at is entered the opposite way round.

The setter owning up, in an aside, to a rather grand way of talking about himself.

Methuselah is the pseudonym printed above the grid, and a setter naming himself in his own clue is a standing habit of the Independent's daily.

4 down HAIRCUT

A B-52 possibly Spoonerised Love Shack? (7)

Definition: A B-52 possibly · spoonerism

The B-52 is one particular style, so it names the general word only as an example, which is what 'possibly' flags.

  • Love → HAIR Love in the sense of care, which hands its opening sound over to the word after it.
  • Shack → CUT A shack is a hut, and it takes on the hard opening sound the first word gave up.
  • Spoonerised — Spooner swapped the opening sounds of two words, so say the pair aloud and trade the noises at their fronts.

The B-52's song Love Shack put through the Reverend Spooner.

A B-52 is the towering beehive style named after the bomber's nose cone, and the band named themselves after the hairdo, which is why their song supplies the sounds.

5 down MOORAGE

Where to stop vessel twisting space and time (7)

Definition: Where to stop vessel · charade + reversal

A MOORAGE is the place a boat is made fast, so the phrase names the spot rather than the act of stopping.

  • space → MOOR Space in the sense of ROOM, then turned about.
  • time → AGE An age is a long stretch of time.
  • twisting — A twist turns a thing round on itself, so the word it touches is entered the other way about.

A ship spinning through space and time, science fiction style.

'Vessel' belongs to the definition rather than the wordplay: it is a boat tying up, while the surface has you picturing a spacecraft.

6 down DISPERSAL

Aldi's reps freshened up spread (9)

Definition: spread · anagram

The DISPERSAL of seeds is their spread outwards, both nouns naming the same scattering.

  • Aldi's reps → DISPERSAL Nine letters go into the shake-up and nine come out, which is what the enumeration asks for.
  • freshened up — Something freshened up has been made over, and a word made over does not keep its letters in the order it was given.

Sales reps from the discount supermarket given a makeover.

Aldi is a German-owned chain of cut-price supermarkets, a fixture of the British high street, and that familiarity is what makes its reps read as staff rather than as letters.

7 down STOLE

Didn't take kindly to being hugged by solver, oddly (5)

Definition: Didn't take kindly · container + alternate letters

Taking something unkindly, that is without asking, is theft, so the negative phrase hides a plain past-tense verb.

  • solver, oddly → SLE Count into the word and keep the letters standing in odd positions, dropping the rest.
  • to → TO The little word is lifted straight out of the clue and dropped inside.
  • being hugged by — A hug closes arms right round its object, so the hugging word splits open and holds this piece inside.
  • oddly — Oddly points at the odd positions, so only the first, third and fifth letters of the word are kept.

Someone reacting badly to being embraced by a crossword solver.

'Didn't take kindly to' reads as one indignant phrase, but the 'to' on the end of it belongs to the wordplay, not the definition.

8 down SECONDED

Supported Davey rather than Miliband? (8)

Definition: Supported · charade

To SECOND a motion is to back it formally, the committee-room sense of support.

  • rather than Miliband? → SECOND Two politicians share the name, and Miliband had it in the public eye first, so pointing away from him says which of the pair is meant.
  • Davey → ED Ed Davey, leader of the Liberal Democrats, supplies his first name.

Backing one party leader in preference to another.

Both men are Eds: Ed Miliband led Labour from 2010, Ed Davey the Liberal Democrats from 2020, so naming one instead of the other is really giving him a number.

14 down PRANKSTER

Lover of capers quietly orders starters for the entire restaurant (9)

Definition: Lover of capers · charade + first letters

A caper is a playful escapade, so the one who loves them is the person forever pulling them on other people.

  • quietly → P P is piano in music, the mark telling a player to be quiet.
  • orders → RANKS Orders in the sense of grades or classes are ranks.
  • the entire restaurant → TER Three words, each handing over the letter it begins with, in the order they are printed.
  • starters for — A starter is what comes first, so each of the words after it gives up only the letter it opens with.

A diner who loves capers quietly ordering first courses for everyone in the place.

'Capers' does double duty in the picture: pickled flower buds on a restaurant menu, and escapades in the definition. 'Starters' plays the same trick.

16 down INEFFABLE

Good story moving leader more than words can say (9)

Definition: more than words can say · charade + cycling

Something INEFFABLE is beyond expression, which is what being more than words can say amounts to.

  • Good → INEF Good is FINE, whose front letter is shifted round to the end.
  • story → FABLE A fable is a short story with a moral.
  • moving leader — The leader is the letter at the front, and moving it sends it round to the back of its own word.

A good story that moves a party leader more than words can say.

The definition and the wordplay share the word moving: the story moves a leader emotionally in the surface, while the setter is moving a letter.

17 down JAMBOREE

Big gathering spread bad smell about base (8)

Definition: Big gathering · charade

A JAMBOREE is a large celebratory rally, the word Scouting took for its international meetings.

  • spread → JAM Jam is the spread you put on toast.
  • bad smell → BO BO is body odour, the two initials standing for the smell.
  • about → RE Re means concerning, the about at the top of a memo.
  • base → E e is the base of natural logarithms in mathematics.

A bad smell being spread around the bottom of something.

'Spread bad smell about' reads as one action, which is what disguises four separate scraps, and the base at the end is mathematical rather than architectural.

19 down SILENCE

Still seeing that heartless lie being swallowed (7)

Definition: Still · container + deletion

The still of the night is its SILENCE, the noun sense of still that survives mostly in that phrase.

  • seeing that → SINCE Seeing that you are late and since you are late are the same conjunction.
  • heartless lie → LE The word for an untruth loses the I at its heart, leaving the letters that stood either side.
  • heartless — A heartless word has had the letter at its very centre taken out of it.
  • being swallowed — Swallowing takes a thing right inside, so this piece disappears into the middle of the word before it.

Watching a small untruth get swallowed whole by the person told it.

'Still' is the trap. It wants to be the adverb meaning even now, carrying the sentence on, and it is a noun.

20 down SIGNING

New team member communicating digitally (7)

Definition: New team member · double definition

A club's new recruit is its latest signing, from the contract he signs; and signing is also holding a conversation in sign language.

  • New team member → the recruit a club has just put under contract, in the sports-page sense
  • communicating digitally → talking with the hands, digits having been fingers long before they were electronics

A club's latest recruit tapping away at his phone.

'Digitally' is the joke: not by phone or email but with the digits, the fingers, which is how sign language is done.

22 down EMAIL

Parliament embraces revolutionary way to contact MP? (5)

Definition: way to contact MP? · hidden word + reversal

An EMAIL is one of several ways of writing to a member, so the question mark marks a definition by example.

  • Parliament → EMAIL Five consecutive letters inside the word, read in the opposite order to the way they are printed.
  • embraces — To embrace is to hold a thing within your arms, so the letters are sitting inside the word, unbroken.
  • revolutionary — A revolution turns a thing full circle, so the run of letters is met the other way about.

The Commons taking up a modern way of letting voters reach their members.

'Revolutionary' looks like a person, a firebrand loose in Parliament, and the clue comes close to describing itself, since the word Parliament really does contain what you write to one.

23 down TRESS

Brief PM with wrong letter from EU? That's a bit of a shock (5)

Definition: a bit of a shock · substitution

A shock is a thick head of hair, and a TRESS is one long lock out of it, so the definition asks for part of the whole.

  • Brief PM → TRUSS Liz Truss lasted 49 days in Downing Street, the shortest premiership Britain has had.
  • wrong letter from EU? → E Of the two letters in EU, the U is already standing there, so the other one replaces it.
  • wrong — Wrong marks a swap: the letter sitting in place is the mistaken one, and the other letter offered takes its seat.

A short-lived Prime Minister mistyping a message from Brussels.

'Brief' is not an instruction to shorten anything: it describes how long the Prime Minister lasted, and every letter of her name stays put except the swapped one.

24 down OWLS

Those turning heads with hot walks now and then (4)

Definition: Those turning heads · alternate letters

OWLS can swivel their heads about 270 degrees, so the phrase names them while also meaning attracting attention.

  • hot walks → OWLS Start at the second letter and take every other one straight through both words.
  • now and then — Now and then means at intervals rather than continuously, and the interval here is every other letter.

Heads turning at the sight of somebody out walking in the heat.

'Turning' looks like an instruction to reverse something and is nothing of the sort; it belongs to the definition, where the turning is literal.
