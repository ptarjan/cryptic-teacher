8 across JUBILANT

Jack built new houses: a number on top of the world (8)

Definition: on top of the world · charade + anagram + container

Someone on top of the world is elated, and jubilant names that same unmixed high spirits — an idiom matched by a single adjective.

  • Jack → J J is the jack in a pack of cards
  • built → BUILT anagram fodder, five letters, and it splits open to take a guest
  • a number → AN the clue's 'a' plus n, the standard abbreviation for number
  • new — made new is made over: the word in front of it is rebuilt from the same letters in a different order.
  • houses — to house something is to give it a room inside you, so the rebuilt letters close around what follows.

Jack of the nursery rhyme puts up new houses, numbered, somewhere near the top of the world.

'The house that Jack built' is the rhyme the surface is leaning on, and it costs you the parsing: 'houses' is a verb here, not a row of buildings.

9 across CLEAR

Obvious Charlie’s King (5)

Definition: Obvious · charade

Clear means obvious — 'it's clear he's lying' — the sense of plain to anyone, not the sense of see-through.

  • Charlie → C Charlie is C in the NATO alphabet
  • King → LEAR Shakespeare's King Lear, the king setters reach for first

Somebody called Charlie has a king — a playing card, perhaps.

Both halves are conventions rather than synonyms, one from the NATO alphabet and one from Shakespeare. 'Charlie's King' also reads as Charles III, which is the misdirection.

10 across MANY

Removing a corner from wood stacks (4)

Definition: stacks · deletion

'Stacks' is the informal quantifier — stacks of room, stacks of time — and many is the plain word for that same abundance.

  • from wood → MAHOGANY mahogany is the wood being worked on
  • a corner → AHOG to hog something is to corner it — you corner a market by hogging it — so this is A + HOG, lifted out of the middle
  • Removing — removing is the bare instruction to take something out, and putting it first makes it read as carpentry rather than as an order.

A carpenter taking the corner off a piece of timber.

'Corner' is a verb here, not a shape: cornering a market is hogging it, and that is the word being pulled out of the plank.

11 across DARJEELING

Japanese fish served in wild leaves (10)

Definition: leaves · container + charade

Darjeeling is a black tea from the Indian hills, and a tea is named for the leaves it is made of — what you spoon into the pot.

  • wild → DARING daring is wild in the reckless sense — this is the word that opens up
  • Japanese → J J is the international code letter for Japan
  • fish → EEL the eel, three letters and two vowels, is the crossword's default fish
  • served in — serving something in a dish puts it inside the dish, so the pieces named before go within the word after.

A fish dish from Japan arriving on a bed of wild greens.

The Darjeeling Limited is one of the Wes Anderson films threaded through this grid — and the tea itself comes from the Indian hills, nowhere near Japan.

12 across MEMOIR

In 2000, Spain rejecting port’s account (6)

Definition: account · container + charade + reversal

A memoir is an account of a life, 'account' in the sense of a written telling of events rather than a bank one.

  • 2000 → MM 2000 in Roman numerals is a pair of Ms
  • Spain → E E is Spain's international vehicle code, from España
  • port → OIR Rio de Janeiro is the port, thrown back the way it came
  • In — the clue opens with 'In' as though setting a year, and it is really the instruction to put what follows inside the number.
  • rejecting — to reject something is to throw it back, so the port's letters arrive in the opposite order.

Spain in the millennium year, turning down a harbour town's version of events.

'Port' looks like the drink the Iberian surface has been setting up, and it is the harbour — a Brazilian one at that.

14 across NOVEMBER

Time period at the heart of Romeo and Juliet? (8)

Definition: Time period · middle letter

November is a month, and a month is a period of time — the definition names the category the answer is a member of.

  • Romeo and Juliet → NOVEMBER Romeo and Juliet are R and J in the NATO alphabet, and the letter sitting midway between J and R has its own name in that same alphabet
  • at the heart of — the heart of two things is the point midway between them, which here means midway along the alphabet from one letter to the other.

A stretch of time somewhere in the middle of Shakespeare's tragedy.

Shakespeare is the false trail: 'Romeo and Juliet?' looks like the play, and the question mark is the only signal that the pair are letters rather than lovers.

16 across ATHEIST

I don’t believe helium is found in a nucleus of matter (7)

Definition: I don’t believe · container + charade + middle letters

An atheist holds that there is no god, and the clue defines the person by putting their denial into their own mouth.

  • a → A the clue's own 'a' is written down as it stands
  • nucleus of matter → TT the two letters at the very centre of matter, which then split apart to make room
  • helium → HE He is helium's symbol on the periodic table
  • is → IS the clue's 'is' goes straight down while still working as the sentence's verb
  • found in — being found in something is being inside it, so the pieces named before this go within what comes after.
  • nucleus of — a nucleus is the dense centre, so this keeps the letters at the middle of the word and drops the rest.

Someone flatly refusing to accept a claim about where helium sits inside an atom.

A helium nucleus is exactly what an alpha particle is, so the physics in the surface is very nearly true — which is what lets the sentence read as one thought instead of two.

18 across INSTEAD

At home with special hot date? Rather! (7)

Definition: Rather · charade + anagram

Rather and instead both mean 'in place of that' — she stayed home instead, she stayed home rather — the substitution sense of each.

  • At home → IN being in is being at home — 'is she in?'
  • special → S s = special, as in special constable
  • date → TEAD the four letters of DATE, run together in another order
  • hot — heat is being applied to the word beside it: something hot runs and loses its shape, and these letters do not set in the order given.

An invitation: are you at home for something special, a hot date perhaps?

The exclamation mark makes 'Rather!' read as enthusiastic agreement, which is the sense the definition does not want. It wants the quiet one: in place of something else.

21 across WHITENER

Frenchman retired after shake with artificial milk (8)

Definition: artificial milk · charade + anagram + reversal

Whitener is the powder stirred into coffee when there is no milk, so 'artificial milk' names it by the thing it stands in for.

  • with → WHIT the four letters of WITH, shaken into a new order
  • Frenchman → ENER René is the setters' stock Frenchman, here written backwards
  • shake — shaking a thing rattles its parts loose, so the word beside it comes out in another order.
  • retired — someone retired has gone back, and the Frenchman's letters are written in the opposite direction.
  • after — 'after' puts the retired name behind the shaken letters rather than in front of them.

A retired Frenchman after a milkshake made with something out of a jar.

The fodder is 'with', a word so small and so grammatical that the eye passes over it as joinery instead of as material to be shuffled.

23 across ENDING

Finish mailing out 17 (6)

Definition: Finish · deletion

The ending of a book or a film is its finish — both are nouns for the last part of a thing, not for the act of stopping it.

  • mailing → SENDING to mail something is to send it — the word the letter comes off
  • 17 → S 17 down is a son, and s = son is the standard abbreviation, so a single letter is what leaves
  • out — 'out' following a word marks that word as the one to take away, the way 'count me out' leaves you absent.

The last of a mailout goes into the post.

Cross-references usually hand you a whole word to slot in. Here 17 down's answer shrinks to its one-letter abbreviation first, and only then is it taken away.

24 across STIR-FRYING

Cooking mash first then the ultimate in jelly-filled doughnut (4-6)

Definition: Cooking · anagram + container + last letter

Stir-frying is cooking food fast in a very hot pan, so the general word defines by naming the family this method belongs to.

  • first → STIRF the five letters of FIRST, mashed into another order
  • doughnut → RING a doughnut is a ring of dough, and it opens after its first letter
  • the ultimate in jelly → Y the last letter of jelly
  • mash — mashing food breaks it up and mixes it, so the word beside it is pulped into a new order.
  • the ultimate in — the ultimate one is the last one, so this takes the final letter of the word that follows.
  • filled — a filled doughnut has something pushed into the middle of it, which is where the single letter goes.

A recipe barked across a kitchen: mash first, then deal with the jam doughnut.

'Mash first' reads as step one of the recipe, and that is the trap: 'first' is the material and 'mash' is the instruction to jumble it.

26 across MIST

Film society hosted by Cambridge University (4)

Definition: Film · container

A mist is a thin film of water hanging in the air — 'film' in the sense of a fine layer, which the surface dresses up as cinema.

  • Cambridge University → MIT MIT stands in Cambridge, Massachusetts — the university in the other Cambridge
  • society → S s = society, the standard abbreviation
  • hosted by — a host takes a guest inside the house, so the letter before this sits within the letters after it.

A film society meeting in rooms lent by a Cambridge college.

Cambridge sends a British solver to the Cam and the colleges; the university wanted is MIT, in Cambridge, Massachusetts.

27 across LEWES

50 close to farms keep sheep in Sussex town (5)

Definition: Sussex town · container + last letter

Lewes is the county town of East Sussex, so the definition points at it as one instance of the county's towns.

  • 50 → L L is 50 in Roman numerals
  • sheep → EWE a ewe is a female sheep, and setters keep a flock of them
  • close to farms → S the last letter of farms
  • keep — keeping livestock is penning them, so the animal named after this word is shut inside the letters either side.
  • in — 'in' completes the penning: the sheep go within the other letters rather than alongside them.
  • close to — the close of a word is its final letter, so this takes only the S that farms ends on.

Sheep penned at the edge of the farms outside a Sussex town.

Lewes is the county town of East Sussex, said 'LOO-iss', and the flocks in the clue are the ones grazing the Downs above it.

28 across ASTEROID

Radio set played rock (8)

Definition: rock · anagram

An asteroid is a lump of rock orbiting the sun, and 'rock' is the plain informal word astronomers themselves use for one.

  • Radio set → RADIOSET anagram fodder, eight letters
  • played → anagram indicator
  • played — playing a piece of music is performing an arrangement of it, and the letters named are heard here in a different arrangement.

A radio playing rock music.

Every word of the surface belongs to music and the answer is a lump of space debris. Asteroid City is one more Wes Anderson film in a grid full of them.

1 down BUDAPEST

American friend’s a nuisance in European 2 (8)

Definition: European 2 · charade

Budapest is a city in Europe, and the 2 sends you to 2 down for the noun, so the definition reads 'European city'.

  • American friend → BUD bud is American for pal — 'hey, bud' — so the nationality is telling you which slang to use
  • a nuisance → APEST a pest is a nuisance, and the clue's own 'a' comes along in front of it

A friend over from the States is being a pain somewhere in Europe.

Wes Anderson's films run right through this grid, and The Grand Budapest Hotel takes a word each from here, 7 down and 22 down. The '2' is a cross-reference, not a number to convert.

2 down CITY

100,120 went off football team? (4)

Definition: football team · charade + deletion

Clubs named for their town are called City by their fans — Manchester City, Leicester City — so the word names a team by example.

  • 100 → C C is 100 in Roman numerals
  • 1 → I I is 1 in Roman numerals
  • 20 went → TY write 20 out as TWENTY, take WENT out of the middle of it and the ends close up
  • off — 'went off' sounds like an alarm going, but 'off' is a removal instruction: the word WENT is what goes away.

A numbered device goes off, and a football team is somehow involved.

One string of digits, three different treatments: two Roman numerals and then a number spelled out in English so it can be robbed. Asteroid City is the Wes Anderson film this half belongs to.

3 down WANDER

Wife more majestic once King George departs to ramble (6)

Definition: ramble · charade + deletion

To wander is to ramble — to go about without a route in mind, the walking sense of both verbs rather than the talking one.

  • Wife → W w = wife, the standard abbreviation
  • King George → GR Georgius Rex, the cypher a King George signs and stamps with
  • more majestic → ANDER GRANDER is more majestic, and once the cypher walks out this is what stands
  • once — 'once' marks the cypher as belonging to a former reign — a King George signed with it, not the monarch on today's post boxes.
  • departs — departing is leaving, so the royal pair named just before it walks out of the longer word.

A wife left grander than before now that King George has gone, taking herself off for a walk.

WANDER and 17 down together spell Wes Anderson, whose films are the theme of the grid. 'Once' is the setter playing fair: GR is a cypher from an earlier reign.

4 down STARING

Looking for our sun, perhaps, in edge of galaxy (7)

Definition: Looking · charade + first letter

Staring is looking, held still and too long — the -ing form matches, and both name an eye fixed on something.

  • our sun → STAR the sun is a star, one among billions
  • in → IN the clue's own 'in' is written down as it stands
  • galaxy → G the letter at the front edge of galaxy
  • perhaps — 'perhaps' warns that our sun is only an instance of the word wanted, not a synonym for it.
  • edge of — an edge is where a thing stops, so this hands over the outermost letter of the word beside it and nothing else.

An astronomer scanning the rim of the galaxy for a star like our own.

Every word belongs to astronomy, which is what hides the join: 'our sun, perhaps' asks for the category the sun belongs to, not for the sun.

5 down ACRE

Measure the holy soul (4)

Definition: Measure · middle letters

An acre is a unit of land area — 'Measure' as the noun for a unit, the way a furlong or a bushel is one.

  • holy → ACRE SACRED means holy, and its innermost four letters are what survive
  • soul — the soul of a thing is what sits deepest inside it, so this asks for the letters at the centre and throws the ends away.

Somebody measuring a soul, as though you could put a tape round one.

Nothing announces a word to work on: 'holy' has to be turned into SACRED before the instruction can bite, and that unstated step is where the clue hides.

6 down SEX-LIMITED

To secure vote, Reform lied with items like male pattern baldness? (3-7)

Definition: like male pattern baldness · anagram

A sex-limited trait is expressed in one sex only, and that pattern of hair loss shows in men, so the example defines the term.

  • To secure vote → X a vote is the cross you pencil on a ballot paper, and securing one gets you that X
  • lied with items → LIEDITEMS anagram fodder, nine letters
  • Reform → anagram indicator
  • Reform — a reform breaks an institution up and puts it back together in a different arrangement, which is what happens to the letters beside it.

A political party accused of lying about odds and ends, hair loss among them, to win a vote.

Reform is a British political party, which is why the capital letter passes as a name in the sentence while it is quietly the instruction underneath.

7 down GRANDE

Singer in terrible danger (6)

Definition: Singer · anagram

Ariana Grande is a pop singer, so the surname defines by example — one performer standing in for the category.

  • danger → DANGER anagram fodder, six letters
  • terrible → anagram indicator
  • terrible — something terrible is in a bad way, and a word in a bad way does not hold the order it was given in.

A performer caught up in something frightening.

Surname-only definitions are worth expecting from this paper. The theme is Wes Anderson, and this entry sits with 1 down and 22 down to cover The Grand Budapest Hotel.

13 down OVERTHROWS

Public arguments involving husband’s ‘extras’ (10)

Definition: extras · charade + container

In cricket, extras are runs not scored off the bat, and overthrows — runs taken when a fielder's return goes astray — are entered as exactly that.

  • Public → OVERT overt means done in the open, in public view
  • husband → H h = husband, the standard abbreviation
  • arguments → ROWS a row is an argument, the word rhyming with 'cow'
  • involving — involving someone is drawing them into the middle of the business, so the letter it introduces sits inside the rest.

A public quarrel that has dragged the husband into it.

The quotation marks around 'extras' are the setter admitting the word is doing specialist work — cricket, where a marital row is nowhere in sight.

15 down VAN

Hooligan ignores young bloke reversing vehicle (3)

Definition: vehicle · deletion + reversal

A van is a vehicle, in the plain delivery sense rather than the vanguard sense that shares the spelling.

  • Hooligan → VANDAL a vandal is a hooligan — the word the deletion works on
  • young bloke → DAL a lad is a young bloke, and turned round he is the three letters that go
  • ignores — to ignore something is to leave it out, so the letters it names are the ones not written down.
  • reversing — the young bloke's three letters arrive back to front, which is the only way they match the tail of the longer word.

A hooligan taking no notice of a young man backing a vehicle out.

The whole thing reads as a scene in a car park, where 'reversing vehicle' is one phrase; the split the solver needs comes a word earlier than the eye wants it.

17 down SON

Uplifting new Big Issue (3)

Definition: Issue · charade + reversal

'Issue' is the legal word for offspring — 'died without issue' — and a son is issue of that kind.

  • new → N n = new, the standard abbreviation
  • Big → OS OS is outsize, the label sewn into big clothing
  • Uplifting — an uplift in a down clue carries the letters back up the grid, so they are read from the bottom of the entry.

A cheering copy of the Big Issue bought on a street corner.

The Big Issue is the magazine sold on British high streets by homeless vendors, and that is the whole picture. Read as offspring, SON joins 3 down to make Wes Anderson.

19 down AGNOSTIC

Sceptic’s odd actions over the origin of God (8)

Definition: Sceptic · anagram + container + first letter

An agnostic holds that nothing can be known about God, so 'sceptic' fits in its everyday sense of a doubter rather than its philosophical one.

  • actions → ANOSTIC the seven letters of ACTIONS, put back in a different order
  • God → G the first letter of God, taken on its own
  • odd — odd means strange, and a strange version of a word is one whose letters have gone out of true.
  • over — going over a thing is covering it, so the rearranged letters wrap round the single letter that follows.
  • the origin of — the origin of a word is where it begins, so this hands over its first letter and nothing more.

A doubter's strange behaviour, and the question of where the idea of God began.

'Odd actions' reads as a description of the man himself, which is where the anagram hides: the word doing the shuffling is the one that sounds most like character.

20 down ARTISAN

As seen, Bogart is a naturally skilled worker (7)

Definition: skilled worker · hidden word

An artisan is a skilled manual worker, a maker of things by hand — a word now met mostly on bread packaging.

  • Bogart is a naturally → ARTISAN seven consecutive letters run across these four words, beginning inside the actor's surname
  • As seen — 'as seen' says the letters are already on the page to be looked at rather than built, so the job is reading across the words.

Humphrey Bogart described as a born craftsman.

Hidden answers hate being started inside a name — the eye reads 'Bogart' as a person and never as a run of letters, which buys the setter the first three.

22 down HOTELS

H-h-housing? (6)

Definition: housing · substitution

Hotels put people up for the night, so 'housing' names them by the service they provide rather than by what they are.

  • H-h → HOTELS in the NATO alphabet H is spoken as HOTEL, and the setter has written the letter twice — a capital and a lower case — so more than one is being asked for

Somebody stuttering over the word 'housing'.

The stammer is a disguise for a doubled letter: NATO's alphabet gives H a word of its own, and having two of them is what makes the answer plural. The Grand Budapest Hotel is the film.

23 down EIGHTH

What describes authority to abolish leader’s position? (6)

Definition: position · container + deletion

Eighth is where you come in a race or a queue, so 'position' names a place in an order — the question mark flagging one instance of it.

  • What → EH 'eh?' is what you say when you have not caught something — 'what?' written as a noise
  • authority → IGHT a right is an authority, an entitlement, and its leading letter is abolished
  • describes — geometry's sense of the word: to describe a circle is to draw it round something, so this puts one piece outside the other.
  • to abolish leader — abolishing the leader takes the front letter off the word it acts on, and the body carries on without its head.

A question about the sort of power that can do away with the top job.

'Describes' looks like the word introducing a definition and is the container instruction instead, which is why the clue seems to have no wordplay in it at all.

25 down RUSHMORE

Former Liverpool striker regularly worried about header from Mason Mount (8)

Definition: Mount · charade + alternate letters + first letter

Mount Rushmore is the South Dakota cliff carved with four presidents' heads, and the answer is the name that word introduces.

  • Former Liverpool striker → RUSH Ian Rush, the club's record goalscorer, from the 1980s
  • Mason → M the head of Mason, its first letter
  • worried → ORE every second letter of wOrRiEd, the rest falling away
  • about → surface only — 'worried about' is the sentence's own phrase and adds no letters
  • regularly — taking a word regularly means picking its letters at a steady interval — here every second one, starting with the second.
  • header from — a header is the top of a thing, so it hands over the first letter of the name after it, while heading is also what footballers do.

A footballer fretting over a header from Mason Mount.

Mason Mount is an England midfielder, so his name passes as one more player in a football sentence. Rushmore is another of the Wes Anderson films buried in this grid.
