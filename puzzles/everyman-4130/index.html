Observer Everyman Crossword No 4,130

Across

1 across AGASSI

American great Andre - super server incarnate, primarily (6)

Definition: American great Andre - super server incarnate, primarily · &lit + first letters

  • American great Andre → AGA the opening letters of the first three words, taken in the order they stand
  • super server incarnate → SSI the opening letters of the next three, still in order

The dash makes the clue look like a definition on one side and wordplay on the other. It is not split at all: every word serves both jobs at once, which is what an &lit does.

5 across DIRECT

Outspoken usher (6)

Definition: Outspoken · double definition

  • Outspoken → the blunt sense: saying the thing itself rather than working round to it
  • usher → the verb an attendant does, showing somebody to a seat or pointing them down the right corridor

Two words, no join and no wordplay. One is an adjective about plain speaking, the other a verb about showing somebody where to go.

8 across READING ROOM

Location of Gaol on rolling heath, as can be seen in library (7,4)

Definition: can be seen in library · charade + reversal

  • Location of Gaol → READING Oscar Wilde's ballad names its gaol by the Berkshire town it stood in, west of London
  • rolling heath → ROOM a heath is open moorland, and MOOR turned end for end gives these four letters

'Gaol' is the older British spelling of jail, and the prison is famous because Wilde was locked up there and wrote a ballad about it. The town's name does double duty.

11 across OVERBUILT

Louvre: bit ugly, with too much development (9)

Definition: with too much development · anagram

  • Louvre: bit → LOUVREBIT nine letters of fodder, the museum's name plus the short word after the colon

The colon and the capital make 'Louvre' look like a topic heading, as if the clue were about the museum's glass pyramid. It is nine letters of raw material and nothing more.

12 across ODOUR

Band with grim smell (5)

Definition: smell · charade

  • Band → O a band is a ring or a hoop, and a ring drawn on paper is the round letter
  • grim → DOUR dour means grim and unsmiling, said of a face or a mood

'Band with grim' reads as a gloomy group of musicians, which is the whole misdirection. O for a ring is worth keeping: it turns up as a band, a hoop, a duck and a love score.

13 across OFFBEAT

Eccentric very loudly getting into flipping Korean exercise (7)

Definition: Eccentric · container + reversal

  • flipping Korean exercise → OBEAT Tae Bo, the fitness workout built on Korean taekwondo, supplies five letters; turned round they read the other way
  • very loudly → FF ff is fortissimo in a musical score, the marking that tells a player to be very loud

The surface is a gym timetable, a long way from the definition. Keep ff: it is one of the most reliable pairs of letters in cryptics, and it is always music.

14 across PASSKEY

Recast, Peaky Blinders actors ultimately getting access to multiple places (7)

Definition: access to multiple places · anagram + last letters

  • Peaky → PEAKY five of the seven letters, taken from the first word of the title
  • Blinders actors ultimately → SS the closing letter of each of those two words, an S apiece

Peaky Blinders is the British drama about a Birmingham gang, and the italics mark it as a title. Only its first word is fodder; the second is there for its final letter alone.

15 across CURDLED

Sour, nasty, heartless, dead cruel (7)

Definition: Sour · anagram + deletion

  • heartless, dead → DD DEAD with its centre pair EA taken out leaves the two consonants at either end
  • cruel → CRUEL the other five letters of the fodder, taken exactly as they stand

The clue is a string of adjectives about a person, and 'dead cruel' reads as British intensifier — dead meaning very. That reading is what hides the pair of Ds.

17 across LAMPOON

Satirise nitwit hiding electrical unit (7)

Definition: Satirise · container

  • nitwit → LOON a loon is a fool, and the four letters open up to take something in
  • electrical unit → AMP an amp is an ampere, the unit of electric current

'Amp' for anything electrical is one of the standard crossword shorthands, and it is short enough to hide anywhere. The surface reads as an insult, not an instruction.

20 across ADIEU

Take care of gold-plated cube (5)

Definition: Take care · container

  • gold → AU Au is gold's symbol in the periodic table, from the Latin aurum
  • cube → DIE a die is the singular of dice, the numbered cube you throw

'Take care of' reads as one phrase meaning look after, and the definition stops a word earlier than you want it to. That single word is where the whole clue turns.

21 across SEMICOLON

Lion comes to represent Mark (9)

Definition: Mark · anagram

  • Lion comes → LIONCOMES nine letters of fodder, both words used whole

The winged lion really is the emblem of St Mark, so the surface is a true sentence about Venice. The capital M is doing all the hiding.

22 across UNDER A CLOUD

Our dad and uncle misbehaving in the doghouse (5,1,5)

Definition: in the doghouse · anagram

  • Our dad and uncle → OURDADUNCLE eleven letters of fodder; the 'and' is joinery and contributes nothing

'Misbehaving' reads as what dad and uncle are up to, which is the disguise: it is an instruction, not a description of the family.

23 across MERSEY

Duck leaving Rome? Yes, swimming in river (6)

Definition: river · anagram + deletion

  • Rome? Yes → ROMEYES seven letters to start from, before anything is taken away
  • Duck → O a batter out for no runs in cricket has made a duck, written as a nought, and that is the letter that goes

The duck is the piece of cricket the crossword borrowed: nought, and so the letter O. On the surface it is a bird flying out of Italy, which is why nobody counts the letters.

24 across LEERED

Looked unpleasantly flushed following return of slippery character (6)

Definition: Looked unpleasantly · charade + reversal

  • return of slippery character → LEE the slippery character is an eel, and it comes back the other way round
  • flushed → RED a flushed face has gone red, so the colour is what the word is for

'Slippery character' for an eel is the joke, and 'flushed' looks like embarrassment when all the clue wants from it is a colour.

2 down GARDEN FURNITURE

Harry nurtured a fringe that's brought out in summer (6,9)

Definition: that's brought out in summer · anagram

  • nurtured a fringe → NURTUREDAFRINGE fifteen letters of fodder, which is the whole enumeration

'Harry' reads as a name — a gardener, or the prince — and it is really a verb meaning to harass. That is the instruction, and the fifteen letters beside it are the material.

3 down SHAMBLE

Some characters from Lewisham blearily totter (7)

Definition: totter · hidden word

  • Lewisham blearily → SHAMBLE Lewisham is a district of south-east London; the letters wanted run straight on across the gap between these two words, in the order they already stand

'Blearily' looks like part of the definition — the way an unsteady walker moves — and it is only there for its letters. That is the usual shape of a hidden word: a phrase that reads like description.

4 down INITIATED

Everyman's description of restaurant trip - given starter of daikons - kicked off (9)

Definition: kicked off · charade + first letter

  • Everyman's description of restaurant trip → INITIATE Everyman is I, and his account of the meal reads IN IT I ATE — four words closed up into eight letters
  • starter of daikons → D the first letter of daikons, the long white radish

Everyman writes himself into his own puzzles, so 'Everyman's' is a first-person I. The word order is a poet's rather than a diner's, which is what stops the sentence sounding like wordplay.

5 down DIGIT

What to do to a whole (reportedly) number!? (5)

Definition: number · homophone

  • a whole (reportedly) → HOLE the word for something entire is said exactly like the word for a pit in the ground, and only the second spelling is any use here
  • What to do to → DIGIT the instruction for dealing with a pit in the ground — a verb and the pronoun after it, pushed together into one five-letter word

Say 'whole' aloud and you have the kind you dig. What you then do to it is two words that close up into one, and the exclamation mark is Everyman enjoying himself.

6 down RIOTOUS

Rowdy: summary of journey starting in S America, ending in N America? (7)

Definition: Rowdy · charade

  • starting in S America → RIO Rio de Janeiro, in Brazil, is the South American end of the trip, and a ticket prints it short
  • summary of journey → TO summarise any journey and it comes out as one place TO another; that joining word is all that is borrowed
  • ending in N America? → US the two letters an itinerary uses for the United States, at the North American end

The colon makes the first word look like a heading for what follows, when it is the definition standing on its own. The rest is an itinerary written the way a ticket would print it.

7 down COMMON KNOWLEDGE

It's generally understood what's possessed by Wombles (6,9)

Definition: It's generally understood · double definition

  • It's generally understood → the plain sense: a thing everybody already knows, so nobody has to be told it
  • what's possessed by Wombles → the Wombles of Elisabeth Beresford's stories live on Wimbledon Common and tidy up the litter left there, so the ground is theirs

The Wombles are burrowing creatures from a 1970s children's series, and the one thing everybody knows about them is where they live. Two definitions side by side, and no letters move.

9 down POLO

Game in which you need a horse or a car (4)

Definition: Game in which you need a horse · double definition

  • Game in which you need a horse → the mounted team game played with long mallets, four riders a side
  • a car → Volkswagen's small hatchback, a common sight on European roads for fifty years

'Or' looks like it is offering a choice of equipment within one game, and it is really the seam between two definitions of the same four letters.

10 down PRAY

I ask you for victim, did you say? (4)

Definition: I ask you · homophone

  • victim → PRAY an animal hunted by another is its prey, and that word said aloud is written the other way here

'I ask you' is also an exclamation of exasperation, which is how the surface wants it read. The definition needs the older sense instead, the one still alive in 'pray tell'.

14 down POLEMICAL

Belligerent, the writer's sent up European state cops (9)

Definition: Belligerent · charade + reversal + container

  • European → POLE a Pole is a native of Poland, one European among many
  • the writer's sent up → MI the writer of the puzzle is I, so 'the writer's' is I'M, and lifting it puts the two letters the other way about
  • state → CAL Cal is the standard short form of California, a state of the USA

The surface pretends to be a news headline about European police. 'The writer' is the setter talking about himself, which is a convention worth knowing: it also comes as I, ME or the setter's own name.

15 down CHAR

Burn arch in ruins (4)

Definition: Burn · anagram

  • arch → ARCH the fodder: four letters, the curved span of stone or brick

Only four letters move here, and 'in ruins' is what moves them. 'Burn arch' reads as a threat to a monument, which is the whole of the misdirection.

16 down LOURDES

Splash of colour desired in holy site (7)

Definition: holy site · hidden word

  • colour desired → LOURDES the letters run straight on from one word into the next, in the order they already stand

'Splash of colour' is such an ordinary phrase that it disappears into the surface, which is exactly what a hidden-word indicator wants to do.

18 down MICROBE

Nick covered in rodents: this might make you sick (7)

Definition: this might make you sick · container

  • rodents → MICE the plural of mouse, and the four letters that do the covering
  • Nick → ROB to nick is British slang for to steal, so a thief's verb sits under the capital letter

The capital N makes Nick look like a man's name, which is the trap. Rodents and a thief in one sentence buy the setter a very smooth surface.

19 down NINE

Square getting verbal refusal in Rheinland (4)

Definition: Square · homophone

  • refusal in Rheinland → NINE the German for no is nein, and a German saying it produces exactly the sound an English speaker writes another way

The Rhineland is the German region along the Rhine, which is how the clue tells you which language to refuse in. The two words are said identically and spelt nothing alike.

21 down STRAY

Holy man with beam? Get lost (5)

Definition: Get lost · charade

  • Holy man → ST St is the abbreviation for saint, printed in front of every canonised name
  • beam → RAY a beam of light is a ray, the sunshine sense rather than the timber one

'Get lost' is a rude dismissal on the surface and a literal wandering underneath. St for saint is one of the first abbreviations a solver should learn.
