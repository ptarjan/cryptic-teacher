Across

8 across THOROUGH

Disk installed in finished comprehensive (8)

Definition: comprehensive · container

comprehensive → THOROUGH: covering every part and leaving nothing out; a thorough search and a comprehensive search are the same search.

  • finished → THROUGH when you are through with something you have finished with it
  • Disk → O the round letter stands for any disk or circle
  • installed in — Something installed in a thing is fitted inside its body, so the disk goes within the word for finished, not next to it.

A disk being fitted into a machine that is already complete.

'Comprehensive' is the definition although it sits at the end looking like the noun — the school — and 'finished' is wordplay although it looks like an adjective describing the machine.

9 across MOUTH

Hum – to set trap (5)

Definition: trap · anagram

trap → MOUTH: British slang for the mouth, as in 'keep your trap shut', the word standing in for the opening itself.

  • Hum – to → HUMTO anagram fodder, five letters
  • set — Type is set when it is arranged into position, and letters set out afresh come up in a new order — a loose indicator that works by suggestion.

Humming a tune while quietly setting a snare.

The dash makes the first word look like a self-contained instruction and quietly separates it from the letters it belongs with. 'Trap' is slang for the thing you are told to shut.

10 across GREY

Horse led by Earl? (4)

Definition: Horse · double definition

Horse → GREY: a horse of that colour is simply called a grey; and Grey is the name that follows Earl in the famous tea.

  • Horse → a horse with a whitish, silver-flecked coat is named for that colour and nothing else
  • led by Earl → put the title in front and you have the bergamot tea, and the prime minister it was named for

An earl leading a horse along.

Horses take their name straight from their colour, and Earl Grey — the prime minister who carried the 1832 Reform Act — lent his to the bergamot tea.

11 across APPROACHED

Came near to rewriting ad hoc paper (10)

Definition: Came near · anagram

Came near → APPROACHED: to approach is to come nearer, and the past tense agrees — the walker approached, the walker came near.

  • ad hoc paper → ADHOCPAPER anagram fodder, ten letters
  • rewriting — Rewriting sets the same material down again in a different arrangement, which is exactly what happens to the ten letters after it.

Somebody who very nearly got round to redrafting a hastily thrown-together report.

'Came near to' reads as one phrase meaning almost managed it, and the split falls inside it: the definition stops after two words and 'to' is only joinery.

12 across VOLLEY

Very faint-hearted, not quite returning salvo (6)

Definition: salvo · charade + deletion + reversal

salvo → VOLLEY: a simultaneous discharge of many guns, which is what a salvo is, and both words serve equally for a burst of insults.

  • Very → V v is the standard abbreviation for very
  • faint-hearted → OLLEY faint-hearted is YELLOW; it loses its last letter and the rest is turned round
  • not quite — Not quite there means stopping just short of the end, so the word gives up its final letter.
  • returning — Something returning comes back along the way it went, so those letters are read from the far end backwards.

A coward who cannot quite bring himself to return fire.

'Returning salvo' reads as one military phrase, which is the trap: 'returning' belongs to the letters and 'salvo' is the definition sitting where a solver expects more wordplay.

14 across APPLAUSE

One American in multinational company gives a hand (8)

Definition: a hand · container + charade

a hand → APPLAUSE: 'give her a hand' asks a room for applause, so a hand is the clapping itself.

  • multinational company → APPLE the Californian technology giant, among the largest firms in the world
  • One → A a stands for one, as 'a hundred' is one hundred
  • American → US US is the everyday short form for American
  • in — It puts the two short pieces inside the company's name rather than in front of it.

A single American working somewhere inside a global corporation.

'Gives a hand' reads as lending assistance, and the hand wanted is the one an audience gives: the noise made by clapping, not any help offered.

16 across FLIGHTY

Irresponsible, filthy cracks about gang leader (7)

Definition: Irresponsible · anagram + first letter

Irresponsible → FLIGHTY: a flighty person flits from one thing to the next and cannot be depended on, irresponsibility of the giddy sort.

  • filthy → FILTHY anagram fodder, six letters
  • gang → G the letter at the head of gang
  • cracks — A cracked thing has broken into pieces, and letters broken apart can be reassembled in any order at all.
  • about — It sets the broken letters round the outside of the single letter, so that one ends up among them.
  • leader — A leader stands at the head of the group, so only the first letter of the word before it comes across.

Somebody making dirty jokes at the expense of the head of a gang.

'Cracks' is a noun in the surface — the jokes themselves — and a verb in the wordplay. Flighty is the old word for a mind that will not settle to anything for long.

18 across CONSOLE

Cheer gamer’s device (7)

Definition: Cheer · double definition

Cheer → CONSOLE in the sense of comforting the bereaved; and a console is the box a gamer plays on, named for a cabinet of controls.

  • Cheer → in the sense of comforting somebody who is upset, not of shouting encouragement from a stand
  • gamer’s device → the box under the television that video games are played on

Cheering on the machine a gamer plays with.

'Cheer' is not the crowd's roar but the quiet kind, comforting somebody. The two meanings come from different places: the Latin for comfort, and the cabinet of controls an organ or a ship's bridge has.

21 across ESOTERIC

One of the Trumps hiding small toe-curling secret (8)

Definition: secret · container + anagram

secret → ESOTERIC: knowledge that is esoteric is kept among the initiated few, secret in the sense of withheld rather than hidden.

  • One of the Trumps → ERIC Eric is one of Donald Trump's sons
  • small → S S is the size label for small
  • toe → OTE the three letters of toe, curled into a different order
  • hiding — Something hidden is tucked inside where it cannot be seen, so the name closes over the pieces that follow it.
  • curling — Letters that curl are bent out of the straight line they came in, so the three in front come out in another order.

A member of the Trump family sitting on a small, cringe-making secret.

'Toe-curling' is one word of embarrassment on the surface and comes apart into a body part and an instruction. The family name is the famous one; the first name is what the letters need.

23 across CODIFY

Order of fish provided by end of day (6)

Definition: Order · charade + last letter

Order → CODIFY: to codify is to arrange rules into an orderly code, order in the sense of putting things in order rather than commanding.

  • fish → COD the everyday North Atlantic food fish
  • provided → IF if means provided that — 'if you agree' is 'provided you agree'
  • day → Y the last letter of day
  • end of — The end of a word is its final letter, so only the last letter of what follows is taken.

A fish supper ordered and delivered at the close of the working day.

'Order of fish' reads as one request at a counter, and the first word is the whole definition on its own: to codify is to set rules into an orderly system.

24 across DAMP-COURSE

House safeguard that makes for heavy going? (4-6)

Definition: House safeguard · double definition

House safeguard → DAMP-COURSE: the damp-proof layer keeping rising damp out of a wall; and a damp course in racing is soft ground, which is heavy going.

  • House safeguard → the impermeable layer built into a wall near ground level to stop damp rising through the brickwork
  • that makes for heavy going → 'the going' is the state of the ground at a British racecourse, and wet ground is called heavy

A protective feature of a building that turns everything into hard work.

Two entirely different courses share the letters: the builder's damp-proof layer, and a racecourse that has taken rain. 'Heavy going' does double duty as racing term and as ordinary slog.

26 across BASS

Mean-sounding voice (4)

Definition: voice · homophone

voice → BASS: the lowest of the singing voices, the part written at the bottom of the stave.

  • Mean → BASS mean here is the old moral sense — base motives, a base betrayal — and that word is what you say aloud
  • sounding — It asks for the noise a word makes rather than its spelling, so what the ear gets is written down another way.

A voice that comes across as unkind.

'Mean' is neither miserly nor unkind here but base, the old word for low and contemptible, and base is said exactly like the lowest of the singing voices.

27 across EARTH

Near the boundless land (5)

Definition: land · middle letters

land → EARTH: the ground itself, the stuff you dig; land and earth both name what is underfoot rather than the planet overhead.

  • Near the → EARTH the two words are run together and the letter at each outer edge is deleted
  • boundless — A bound is a limit, so a boundless phrase has lost the letters standing at its two limits, the very first and the very last.

A spot lying close to a country that stretches away without limit.

'Boundless' looks like an adjective describing the land ahead and is quietly an instruction. The definition wants the soil sense rather than the planet — good earth, scorched earth.

28 across ETRUSCAN

Centaurs cast out of a pre-Roman state (8)

Definition: of a pre-Roman state · anagram

of a pre-Roman state → ETRUSCAN: belonging to Etruria, the civilisation in Tuscany that Rome absorbed — Etruscan tombs, Etruscan pottery.

  • Centaurs → CENTAURS anagram fodder, eight letters — exactly the length the grid wants
  • cast out — Something cast out is flung from where it was sitting, and letters flung about land in a different arrangement.

Mythical half-horses being driven out of an ancient kingdom.

Centaurs belong to Greek myth rather than Italian archaeology, and 'cast out' passes as banishment while telling you to rearrange. Nothing is added or dropped: eight letters in, eight out.

Down

1 down CHARCOAL

What drawers might hold: chain, bow and ring on a length (8)

Definition: What drawers might hold · charade

What drawers might hold → CHARCOAL: drawers here are people who draw, and a stick of charcoal is the classic thing in a sketcher's hand.

  • chain → CH ch is the surveyor's chain, the old 22-yard unit
  • bow → ARC a bow is bent into a curve, and a stretch of curve is an arc
  • ring → O the round letter does duty for any ring or circle
  • a → A the clue's own indefinite article is taken across as a letter
  • length → L l is the standard symbol for length in maths and physics
  • on — In a down clue one piece sits on another by resting above it, so everything before this word stacks on top of everything after.

A jewellery box inventory: a chain, a bow and a ring threaded onto a cord.

'Drawers' is the joke, and it means people who draw rather than the sliding boxes or the underwear. Two of the five pieces are units of measurement dressed up as things on a necklace.

2 down BRAY

Harsh sound of underwear rubbing against somebody’s rear (4)

Definition: Harsh sound · charade + last letter

Harsh sound → BRAY: the rough grating cry of a donkey, used of any loud ugly noise a person makes.

  • underwear → BRA the three-letter item of underwear everyone thinks of first
  • somebody’s → Y the last letter of somebody
  • rubbing against — Two things rubbing against each other are in contact, so the pieces are laid side by side with nothing between them.
  • rear — A person's rear is their back end, and a word's rear is its final letter — only that one is wanted.

The scratchy noise made by underclothes chafing against a person's backside.

The surface is deliberately grubby, which is what lets 'rear' pass as anatomy instead of as an instruction. A bray is the donkey's call, and by extension any coarse blast of laughter.

3 down SUGARY

Player supporting the Guardian, perversely, is sweet? (6)

Definition: sweet · charade + reversal

sweet → SUGARY: tasting of sugar, the plain adjective for anything sweetened past the point of subtlety.

  • the Guardian → SU a Guardian setter can write his own paper as US, and the reversal turns those two letters
  • Player → GARY Gary Player, the South African golfer, so the surname hands over the first name
  • supporting — To support something is to hold it up from below, so in a down clue the supporting piece is written underneath.
  • perversely — Perverse means contrary, going against the way a thing was handed to you, so those letters are set down the other way round.

A sportsman coming out, contrarily, in support of the newspaper.

'Player' reads as a role and is a surname: Gary Player won nine majors. The paper referring to itself as 'us' is a habit worth knowing — a Guardian clue can clue US that way.

4 down WHIPSAW

Cat saying ‘tree cutter’? (7)

Definition: tree cutter · charade

tree cutter → WHIPSAW: a narrow saw with a springy blade, worked by two people to cut through timber.

  • Cat → WHIP the cat-o'-nine-tails, the navy's old lash, known simply as the cat
  • saying → SAW a saw is a proverb or maxim — 'full of wise saws and modern instances'

A cat that appears to have come out with the words 'tree cutter'.

The quotation marks make it look as though an animal is talking, which is what stops the two opening words being read as two separate pieces of vocabulary laid end to end.

5 down AMMO

Brief shots of mother, topless doing a twirl (4)

Definition: Brief shots · deletion + reversal

Brief shots → AMMO: the clipped everyday word for ammunition, shots in the sense of rounds to fire rather than photographs.

  • mother → MOMMA momma is the familiar American word for mother
  • topless — In a down clue the top is the first letter, and a topless word has had that letter taken off.
  • doing a twirl — A twirl spins something completely round, so what is left is read from the far end back.

A quick photo session: mum bare-topped, caught mid-pirouette.

'Shots' works as photographs across the surface and as rounds in the definition, and 'Brief' is doing a real job: the answer is the clipped form of a much longer word.

6 down OUTCLASSED

Student group in exposed surroundings put in the shade (10)

Definition: put in the shade · container

put in the shade → OUTCLASSED: to be put in the shade is to be so far surpassed that nobody looks at you, beaten by a better class of thing.

  • exposed → OUTED to out somebody is to expose them publicly
  • Student group → CLASS a class is the group of students taught together
  • in — It places the student group within the other word rather than beside it, which is what makes this an insertion.
  • surroundings — Surroundings are everything lying round about, so the exposing word closes on both sides of what it takes in.

A student society standing about in bleak open country.

'Exposed' has to be read as a verb rather than as an adjective describing bare countryside, and 'put in the shade' is figurative: beaten by something better, not sheltered from the sun.

7 down WHEELS

Car is revolutionary (6)

Definition: Car is revolutionary · cryptic definition

Car is revolutionary → WHEELS: slang for a car, and wheels are the parts of it that literally revolve.

  • Car → the surface offers a motor vehicle, and slang has long named one by the round things it rolls along on
  • is revolutionary → reads as ground-breaking; the sense wanted is the literal one, going round and round

A radically new motor car.

'Revolutionary' promises innovation and delivers rotation. Slang then takes the turning parts for the whole vehicle, which is how six letters cover both halves of the sentence at once.

13 down LIGHT OPERA

Elgar too hip, perhaps, to drop round for this type of work (5,5)

Definition: this type of work · anagram + deletion

this type of work → LIGHT OPERA: a musical work in the tuneful comic vein of Gilbert and Sullivan, opera with the weight taken out.

  • Elgar too hip → ELGARTOOHIP anagram fodder, eleven letters — one too many for the grid
  • round → O a round is a circle, and it is the letter dropped from the fodder
  • perhaps — It offers a possibility rather than a fact: the letters in front of it could come out some other way round.
  • drop — To drop something is to let go of it, so the letter named next falls out of the mix before anything is rearranged.

Elgar being far too fashionable, perhaps, to pop round and take the job on.

Elgar wrote nothing of the sort, and the joke is that he is much too grand for it. 'Drop round' is a friendly visit in the surface and a discarded letter in the wordplay.

15 down POO

Hapless Republican’s abandoned passed motion (3)

Definition: passed motion · deletion

passed motion → POO: motion is the polite clinical word for a bowel movement, so one that has been passed is the thing itself.

  • Hapless → POOR hapless is unlucky, a sense this word carries alongside its money one — it is what 'you ... thing' means said in sympathy
  • Republican → R R is the standard abbreviation for Republican, as on a US election map
  • abandoned — What is abandoned is walked away from and left behind, so that letter goes missing from the word before it.

An unlucky Republican walking away from a motion that had just been carried.

Every word of the surface is parliamentary — motions are passed, members abandon them — while the definition uses the nursery-and-doctor sense of motion instead.

17 down TOR

Hill’s tale uncovered (3)

Definition: Hill · middle letters

Hill → TOR: the word for a rocky hill or the crag on top of one, standard in the West Country and on Dartmoor.

  • tale → TOR a tale is a STORY, and deleting the letters that cover its two ends leaves the middle three
  • uncovered — Take the cover off a word and the letters wrapping each end come away, leaving what was underneath them.

A story about a hill that has finally been brought to light.

A tor is the bare rocky outcrop crowning a hill on English moorland — Glastonbury Tor, the granite tors of Dartmoor — and 'uncovered' passes as revelation while working as an instruction.

19 down LIFESPAN

Duration of biography’s going to pot (8)

Definition: Duration · charade

Duration → LIFESPAN: the length of time something lasts from beginning to end, which is what a duration measures.

  • biography’s → LIFES a biography is a life, and the possessive s comes along with it
  • pot → PAN pots and pans — a pan is a cooking pot

A life story that is falling apart.

'Going to pot' is a single idiom on the surface, meaning ruin, and two separate jobs in the wordplay: a phrase that merely joins, and a word for a cooking vessel.

20 down SCARPER

Mark for each bolt (7)

Definition: bolt · charade

bolt → SCARPER: British slang for running away in a hurry, from the Italian scappare, the same sudden flight as bolting.

  • Mark → SCAR a scar is the mark a healed wound leaves
  • for each → PER per means for each — miles per hour

An inspector putting a mark against every bolt.

'Bolt' looks like the threaded fastener the surface is counting and is wanted as the verb: to bolt is to run off suddenly. Scarper is British slang for exactly that exit.

22 down SHAKES

Foreign alcoholic drinks poured over Henry’s cold drinks (6)

Definition: cold drinks · container

cold drinks → SHAKES: milkshakes, the thick cold drinks a diner serves, shortened to shakes in ordinary speech.

  • Foreign alcoholic → SAKES sake is the Japanese rice wine, and the clue asks for more than one of them
  • Henry → H H is the symbol for the henry, the unit of electrical inductance
  • poured over — Pour a drink over somebody and it covers him, so his letter finishes up inside the word for the drinks.

Imported spirits being tipped over the cold drinks Henry had just poured.

Sake is the one Japanese word most solvers have met on a menu, and Henry is the electrical unit rather than the man the surface paints standing there getting drenched.

23 down CHEERS

Cold? Here’s fishy toast (6)

Definition: toast · charade + anagram

toast → CHEERS: the word said while raising a glass, the standard British toast, standing for the salute itself.

  • Cold → C C is the letter on a cold tap
  • Here’s → HEERS the five letters of here's, shuffled
  • fishy — Something fishy is not as it should be, and letters that are not as they should be have been put in the wrong order.

Somebody shivering is offered a slice of toast that smells of fish.

'Here's' looks like the setter handing something over and is the fodder itself, while 'toast' is the raised glass rather than the thing under the fish.

25 down OCHE

Player’s behind this aspect of polo cheating? (4)

Definition: Player’s behind this · hidden word

Player’s behind this → OCHE: the oche is the throwing line in darts, the mark a player's feet must stay behind.

  • polo cheating → OCHE the letters run consecutively across the join between the two words, in the order given
  • aspect of — An aspect is one part of a bigger thing, so a stretch of what follows is wanted rather than its meaning.

Some sharp practice going on in a game of polo.

The oche is the line a darts player has to stand behind when throwing, so the opening words are literal. Polo is there only to make 'cheating' sound like a sporting scandal.

26 down BUST

Police work that often gets support (4)

Definition: Police work · double definition

Police work → BUST: a bust is a police raid; and the bust is the bosom, which is what a bra supports.

  • Police work → a raid, in the sense of the drugs squad turning a place over
  • that often gets support → the part of a woman's figure a bra is designed to hold up

The kind of policing that tends to be well backed by the public.

'Gets support' reads as public approval for the police and means underwiring instead. The third sense pulls the same trick: a sculpted head and shoulders gets support from a plinth.
