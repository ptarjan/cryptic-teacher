Across

1 across LEWIS CARROLL

Writer of 7D is well positioned to secure motor part? Not quite! (5,7)

Definition: Writer of 7D · anagram + container + deletion

Lewis Carroll coined the portmanteau word in Through the Looking-Glass, so he is literally the writer of 7 down's entry.

  • is well → ISWELL anagram fodder, six letters
  • motor → CAR a motor is a car
  • part → ROL an actor's part is a role, docked of its last letter
  • positioned — Putting letters into position is only a job worth naming if they are not standing in the right one yet.
  • to secure — Securing something means closing on it and holding it fast, so one group of letters shuts around another.
  • Not quite! — The exclamation says the word just before it stops a fraction short of the mark, so its final letter goes.

Somebody stands in just the right spot to grab hold of a car component, though not quite the right one.

The definition points across the grid: 7 down names the kind of word this writer invented, blending two words into one. 'Motor part' reads as a single phrase and is two separate pieces.

8 across ORIGAMI

Japanese craft fit with sails, following old comrade to Marseille (7)

Definition: Japanese craft · charade

Origami is the Japanese art of folding paper, a craft in the sense of a handed-down skill rather than a vessel.

  • old → O o is the standard abbreviation for old
  • fit with sails → RIG to rig a vessel is to fit it out with sails
  • comrade to Marseille → AMI in Marseille a comrade is an ami
  • following — A piece described as following another is set down behind it, so these letters take second place.

An old friend down in Marseille is busy fitting a boat out with sails.

'Craft' points at boats all the way through the surface — the rigging, the French port — and in the definition it means a practised skill with paper.

9 across GENOMES

Instructions Ken discovered inside garden ornaments (7)

Definition: Instructions · container + middle letter

A genome is an organism's complete set of genetic instructions, so the plural covers several such sets of directions.

  • garden ornaments → GNOMES the plaster gnome is the classic British garden ornament
  • Ken → E KEN with the letters covering it at either end taken away
  • discovered — Read as dis-covered rather than found, it strips the covering off, and a short word's covering is the letter at each end.
  • inside — The word says the piece is placed within, so the ornaments close around it.

A man called Ken turns up hiding among the gnomes on somebody's lawn.

'Discovered' is the setter's pun on dis-covered: not found, but having the cover taken off. Garden gnomes are a fixture of British suburban lawns.

11 across TAUREAN

Go round Scotsman, we hear, bullish sort (7)

Definition: bullish sort · charade + homophone

A Taurean is a person born under Taurus, the bull, so the 'sort' is an astrological label rather than a temperament.

  • Go round → TOUR to go round a country is to tour it
  • Scotsman → IAN Ian is the Scottish form of John and the crossword's usual Scotsman
  • Go round Scotsman, we hear → TAUREAN run together and said aloud, those two pieces come out spelt another way
  • we hear — The pieces reach us as noise, so what gets written down is a spelling of the sound rather than of the words themselves.

You are sent round a Scotsman who is, by all accounts, the bullish type.

Taureans are born under the bull, which is where 'bullish' comes from — nothing to do with rising markets.

12 across STOMATA

Buys Tom a table with hidden leaf sections (7)

Definition: leaf sections · hidden word

Stomata are the microscopic pores set into a leaf's surface, parts of the leaf itself — 'sections' put loosely.

  • Buys Tom a table → STOMATA seven letters run consecutively across these four words, starting one letter into the first
  • hidden — Something hidden is present without showing itself, so the letters are sitting unbroken inside the words around them.

Someone buys Tom a table with extra leaves tucked away inside it.

The surface's leaf is a table's fold-out panel; the definition's leaf is the botanical one. Stomata are the pores a plant breathes and sweats through.

13 across AVERT

Head off from party, cycling plastered essentially (5)

Definition: Head off · charade + cycling + middle letter

To avert something is to head it off — to turn it aside before it can arrive — the same prevention sense in both.

  • party → AVER a party is a rave, whose R rides round to the back
  • plastered → T the central letter of the nine in 'plastered'
  • cycling — Cycling sends the letter at one end round to the other while the rest keep their order, the way a bicycle chain comes back round.
  • essentially — The essence of a word is its very middle, so the central letter is the one taken.

Leaving a party early on a bike, thoroughly drunk.

'Head off' is the prevent sense, not the departure sense the surface is selling, and 'plastered' is drunk on the surface but only a nine-letter word to be measured in the wordplay.

14 across IMPORTERS

1A’s impressive supporters, they bring a lot in (9)

Definition: they bring a lot in · charade + deletion

Importers are the traders who bring goods into a country in bulk, which is what 'they bring a lot in' describes.

  • impressive → IMP only the front three letters survive the blend
  • supporters → ORTERS only the back six letters survive, from the fourth on
  • 1A’s — The writer at 1 across gave English the blended word, so the possessive is an instruction to telescope the two words that follow into one.

The writer at 1 across has impressive supporters, and they bring plenty in.

A portmanteau word runs the head of one word into the tail of another, which is the device behind this clue and 15 down as well.

16 across TEMPLATES

Casual worker behind schedule, a little slow to find pro-forma documents (9)

Definition: pro-forma documents · charade + first letter

A template is a ready-made pattern to work from, which is what a pro-forma document is: a form laid out in advance to be copied.

  • Casual worker → TEMP a temp is a worker taken on for a short spell
  • behind schedule → LATE running behind schedule is being late
  • slow → S the letter the word starts with
  • a little — Asking for a little of a word takes the smallest piece there is, the single letter it opens with.

An agency temp is running late and is a bit slow with the paperwork.

Every word of the surface belongs to one office scene, which is what hides a three-part build. Pro-forma documents are blank layouts kept ready to be filled in.

19 across SO FAR

Afro’s styled up to a point (2,3)

Definition: up to a point · anagram

So far means to a limited extent and no further, which is precisely what the idiom 'up to a point' says.

  • Afro’s → AFROS anagram fodder, five letters counting the S
  • styled — Hair that has been styled has been worked into a new arrangement, and the letters get the same treatment.

An Afro styled to a certain height and no further.

The apostrophe-s is part of the fodder rather than a possessive, which makes it five letters and not four. 'Up to a point' is an idiom meaning partly, and all of it is the definition.

21 across ALLEGED

Put forward member in new deal (7)

Definition: Put forward · container + anagram

To allege something is to put it forward as a claim without proving it, and both words are past tense assertions.

  • deal → DEAL anagram fodder, four letters
  • member → LEG a leg is a limb, a member of the body
  • in — The member goes in rather than alongside, so its letters are dropped within the other piece.
  • new — Something made new is remade out of the same material, so the letters come back in another order.

A club member is put up for something under the terms of a fresh agreement.

'Put forward' means asserted rather than nominated, and it is past tense, which the answer's ending has to match. 'Member' is the anatomical sense: a limb.

23 across PEEPERS

Goes around empty palace with King and surveyors (7)

Definition: surveyors · container + charade + outer letters

Peepers are those who look others over, so 'surveyors' is the plain ones-who-survey sense rather than the land-measuring profession.

  • Goes → PEES to go, in the nursery sense, is to pee
  • palace → PE the outside letters of the word with its middle gone
  • King → R R is Rex, Latin for king, as on the postboxes
  • around — One piece goes around the other, closing on it from both sides at once.
  • empty — An empty building keeps only its walls, so the word keeps only its outer letters and the middle is cleared out.

Somebody doing the rounds of a deserted palace in the company of the king.

Peepers is old slang for the eyes as well as for people who pry, and the palace is emptied of everything except its walls.

24 across SITCOMS

Shows model company manuscript (7)

Definition: Shows · charade

Sitcoms are television programmes, so 'Shows' is the everyday broadcasting sense with the plural matching.

  • model → SIT to model for a painter is to sit for them
  • company → CO co is the abbreviation tacked onto a firm's name
  • manuscript → MS ms is the standard abbreviation for a manuscript

A manuscript from an exemplary firm is put on display.

'Model' is wanted as a verb — to model for an artist is to sit — not as the noun the surface offers. Sitcoms are situation comedies, built on one recurring setting.

25 across REPLETE

Charged salesman to lease convertible, ultimately (7)

Definition: Charged · charade + last letter

Replete means filled to capacity, the sense 'charged' carries in a charged battery or a phrase charged with meaning.

  • salesman → REP a rep is a sales representative
  • lease → LET to lease a property out is to let it
  • convertible → E the final letter of the word
  • ultimately — The ultimate thing is the last one reached, so the word's final letter is what gets taken.

A salesman is charged with leasing out a convertible when all is said and done.

'Convertible' is the word most likely to be read as an anagram instruction, and here it is a car whose last letter is wanted. 'Charged' means filled, not accused.

26 across HABERDASHERY

Shop has rye bread replaced - bit of hair stuck in it (12)

Definition: Shop · anagram + first letter

A haberdashery is the shop that sells sewing sundries — buttons, thread, ribbon — so 'Shop' is a plain singular synonym.

  • has rye bread → HASRYEBREAD anagram fodder, eleven letters
  • hair → H the letter the word begins with
  • replaced — Putting things back in place implies they were lifted out first, and they need not go back where they were.
  • bit of — A bit of a word is a fragment rather than the whole, and the fragment a setter means is the letter it begins with.
  • stuck in it — The phrase says the stray letter is lodged somewhere among the others rather than tacked on at either end.

A shop where the rye bread has been swapped for something else and a stray hair has got into it.

Haberdashery is the British shop for buttons, ribbons and sewing sundries; in America the same word sells menswear. The dash carries no instruction and only breaks the sentence.

Down

1 down LEISURE

Holiday time in lieu is every so often uncertain? No! (7)

Definition: Holiday time · charade + alternate letters

Leisure is the free time a holiday is made of — the time itself, not the activities that fill it.

  • lieu is → LEI the first, third and fifth letters of these two words
  • uncertain → SURE flatly contradicted, 'uncertain' becomes its opposite
  • every so often — Something done every so often happens at intervals rather than continuously, so the letters are picked up at every second step.
  • No! — The flat contradiction turns the word before it into its opposite, and it is the opposite whose letters are wanted.

An employee insisting that the time off they are owed for extra hours worked is never in any doubt.

Time in lieu is hours owed back for overtime, which makes the surface sound like an office grumble; 'every so often' is doing the counting instead.

2 down WEAKEST

Bridge partner devours cake less the icing - most unhealthy (7)

Definition: most unhealthy · container + deletion

The weakest of a group is the one in poorest health, a superlative answering the superlative 'most unhealthy'.

  • Bridge partner → WEST the four seats at a bridge table are North, South, East and West, and West partners East
  • cake → AKE CAKE with the letter on top removed
  • devours — What is devoured ends up inside the devourer, so one word swallows the other whole.
  • less the icing — Icing goes on top of a cake, and in a down clue the top is the front, so the leading letter comes off.

A card player at the bridge table wolfing down somebody else's cake once the icing has been scraped off.

Bridge's four seats are named for compass points, so 'partner' is asking for a direction rather than a person, and the dash stands in for an equals sign.

3 down SCIENTIST

Perfume containing iodine first for one qualified in olfactology? (9)

Definition: one qualified in olfactology? · container + charade

Someone qualified in the study of smell is a scientist — the definition names one branch as an example of the whole class.

  • Perfume → SCENT a perfume is a scent
  • iodine → I I is iodine's chemical symbol
  • first → IST first written as a numeral keeps these three letters: 1ST
  • containing — A perfume containing an ingredient holds it within, so the letter is planted inside the other word's letters.

A perfume laced with iodine, handed to somebody holding a qualification in the study of smell.

Olfactology is the study of smell, and the question mark admits the definition names only one specialism out of many. 'First' is asking for the tail of 1ST.

4 down ARGUS

Ancient overseer refined sugar (5)

Definition: Ancient overseer · anagram

Argus, the hundred-eyed giant posted to guard Io, is the classical byword for a watchful overseer.

  • sugar → SUGAR anagram fodder, five letters
  • refined — Refining puts a substance through a process it comes out of rearranged, and the letters go the same way.

An overseer from long ago is credited with refining the sugar.

Argus of Greek myth had a hundred eyes and was set to watch over Io, which is why an all-seeing watchman counts as ancient.

5 down RANCOUR

Upwardly mobile officer overheard hostility (7)

Definition: hostility · homophone

Rancour is settled, long-nursed bitterness — hostility of the sort that is felt rather than fought out.

  • Upwardly mobile officer → RANCOUR a ranker is an officer commissioned up from the ordinary ranks, and both words are said the same way
  • overheard — Something overheard reaches you as sound alone, so what you write down need not be spelt the way the speaker meant it.

An officer climbing quickly through the ranks is caught being unpleasant about somebody.

'Upwardly' looks like the reversal instruction a down clue invites, and it is describing a career instead. A ranker is a soldier promoted out of the enlisted ranks.

6 down LIMEADE

Fizzy drink somehow emailed (7)

Definition: Fizzy drink · anagram

Limeade is a lime cordial sold carbonated, so 'fizzy drink' names it by its type rather than by a synonym.

  • emailed → EMAILED anagram fodder, seven letters
  • somehow — Leaving the manner of arrival unstated leaves the order of the letters unstated too, so they can be rebuilt.

A drink that has arrived, by some unexplained route, in your inbox.

Limeade is the lime version of lemonade. The clue runs to four words, so the one spare adverb has nowhere to hide and has to be the instruction.

7 down PORTMANTEAUS

Cases of fortified wine fellow starts to unceremoniously swill after evening meal (12)

Definition: Cases · charade + first letters

A portmanteau is a large travelling case that opens into two equal compartments, so 'cases' is a plain plural synonym.

  • fortified wine → PORT port is the fortified wine of the Douro
  • fellow → MAN a fellow is a man
  • evening meal → TEA across much of Britain the evening meal is called tea
  • unceremoniously swill → US the opening letters of these two words
  • starts to — The phrase points at where the following words start, so only the letter each one opens with is taken.
  • after — Setting one piece after another puts it behind, so those letters follow what has been built already.

Crates of port, a bloke, and a thoroughly undignified drinking session once the evening meal is out of the way.

'Tea' for the evening meal is everyday British usage rather than the drink. The definition is the luggage sense: a portmanteau is a stiff travelling case hinged to open into two halves.

10 down SEAMSTRESSES

Sewers and streams broadcast - ssssssssssss? (12)

Definition: Sewers · anagram + charade

Seamstresses sew for a living, so 'sewers' in its ones-who-sew sense — not the drainage sense — is a plain plural synonym.

  • streams → STREAMS anagram fodder, seven letters
  • ssssssssssss? → ESSES a written-out row of the letter S, and the plural of ess is esses
  • broadcast — To broadcast is to fling seed wide across a field, which is where these letters end up before you gather them.

Drains and streams giving off one long hiss between them.

'Sewers' wants to be the drains the rest of the surface is about, and it means people who sew. The hiss spelt out is a row of esses, and esses is a word.

15 down PASSPORTS

Means of identifying 1A’s passionless spoilsports (9)

Definition: Means of identifying · charade + deletion

A passport is the document proving who you are at a border, so the plural is the standard means of identifying travellers.

  • passionless → PASS the opening four letters are all that is kept
  • spoilsports → PORTS the last five letters are all that is kept
  • 1A’s — The possessive credits the blend to the writer at 1 across, whose invention it was, and asks for the next two words to be fused into one.

Killjoys with no passion left in them, belonging to the writer at 1 across.

What is left of 'spoilsports' is where ships tie up, so the two surviving halves both mean something on their own before they are joined.

17 down MILITIA

Civilian fighters lost in battle getting one fired (7)

Definition: Civilian fighters · container + charade

A militia is a fighting force raised from ordinary civilians rather than professional soldiers, so it is exactly civilians who fight.

  • lost in battle → MIA MIA is the services' shorthand for missing in action
  • one → I the Roman numeral for one
  • fired → LIT a fire that has been fired is lit
  • getting — Getting something is taking it in, so these letters are received inside the word that gets them.

Volunteers go missing in a battle and somebody is sacked over it.

MIA is the military's own shorthand, and 'one fired' looks like a single dismissal while splitting into two separate pieces.

18 down LUGHOLE

Hearing part where water drains when spending penny (7)

Definition: Hearing part · deletion

A lughole is British slang for an ear — 'lug' being a dialect word for one — so it is the part you hear with.

  • penny → P p is the abbreviation for a penny in British money
  • where water drains → LUGHOLE a PLUGHOLE hands over that letter from the front
  • spending — Money spent is money gone, so the coin's letter leaves the word rather than joining it.

A remark about where the water goes when you nip off to the loo.

'Spending a penny' is the British euphemism for a trip to the lavatory, which is what makes the surface hang together; the wordplay only wants the coin's letter gone.

19 down SHEEPLE

Banshee pledging to give sanctuary to those easily led (7)

Definition: those easily led · hidden word

Sheeple are people who follow the herd unthinkingly, the blend saying in one word what 'easily led' says in three.

  • Banshee pledging → SHEEPLE seven consecutive letters straddle the join between these two words
  • to give sanctuary to — Giving sanctuary is sheltering someone within your own walls, so the letters are being kept inside the words that offer it.

A wailing banshee promises shelter to the gullible.

Sheeple is a modern coinage — sheep spliced into people — for a crowd that follows without asking questions.

20 down FRENEMY

Fine fellow from Fontainebleau Bard’s sparring partner? (7)

Definition: sparring partner? · charade

A frenemy is friend and enemy at once, which is what a sparring partner is: someone you trade blows with in friendship.

  • Fine → F F is the pencil grade meaning fine
  • fellow from Fontainebleau → RENE René is a French man's name and Fontainebleau is a French town
  • Bard’s → MY Bard is the setter of this puzzle, so what belongs to Bard is his own to call mine

A fine Frenchman from Fontainebleau, described as the setter's sparring partner.

The setter signs himself Bard, so 'Bard's' is him saying 'my' — worth remembering whenever a byline turns up inside a clue.

22 down DOSED

Regularly drop speed (prescribed) (5)

Definition: prescribed · alternate letters

To be dosed is to be given a measured amount of medicine, which is what a prescription orders up.

  • drop speed → DOSED the first, third, fifth, seventh and ninth letters of these two words
  • Regularly — Letters taken at a regular interval means every second one, counted from the start and never varying.

Someone taking amphetamines at regular intervals, and on doctor's orders at that.

'Speed' is the drug on the surface and merely a word to count through in the wordplay, while the single bracketed word carries the whole definition.
