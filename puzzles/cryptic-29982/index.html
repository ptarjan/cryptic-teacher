1 across PULSAR

Endless effect of gravity – time to leave white dwarf, say, for something denser (6)

Definition: something denser · charade + deletion

A pulsar is a spinning neutron star, whose matter is packed far tighter than a white dwarf's, so it is literally the denser object.

  • Endless effect of gravity → PUL what gravity does to everything near it is pull, and that word arrives here one letter short at the back
  • time to leave white dwarf, say → SAR a white dwarf is one sort of star, 'say' owning up to the example, and the T that stands for time is taken out of it
  • Endless — Something endless has no end, so the word it governs is written without its final letter.
  • to leave — A piece told to leave walks out of the word it was sitting in, and the remaining letters close up behind it.

An astronomer describing how gravity crushes a dying white dwarf into something heavier still.

'Say' is the flag for a definition by example: the white dwarf is not the thing being defined, only one kind of star. The dash keeps the astronomy sentence upright and nothing more.

4 across CATARRH

A lot of delay blocking courthouse discharge (7)

Definition: discharge · container + deletion

Catarrh is the thick mucus a head cold produces, and discharge is the clinical noun for anything a body puts out.

  • courthouse → CH CH is the map-maker's shorthand for a court house, the building where British cases are heard
  • A → A the article stands for itself, one letter
  • lot of delay → TARR to tarry is to delay, and most of that word is all the clue wants
  • lot of — A lot of a word is most but not all of it, so the final letter stays behind.
  • blocking — Something blocking a gap is wedged into the middle of it, which is where these letters go.

A long hold-up jamming the entrance to the court building.

'Discharge' is medical here rather than legal, though the courthouse standing in front of it works hard to keep you in a courtroom. CH is the abbreviation British maps use for a court house.

9 across DAMP SQUIB

Cephalopod fresh from the sea mostly branded at first a disappointment (4,5)

Definition: a disappointment · charade + deletion + first letter

A damp squib is a firework too wet to fire, and the phrase now stands for anything that fizzles instead of delivering.

  • fresh from the sea → DAMP anything just out of the water is still wet
  • Cephalopod → SQUI a squid is the ten-armed cephalopod, here losing its tail letter
  • branded → B the opening letter of branded
  • mostly — Mostly means nearly all of it, so the creature's name turns up with its final letter missing.
  • at first — A word taken at first surrenders only the letter it starts with.

A squid landed straight off the boat and written off by the buyers as a letdown.

The clue runs as one sentence about seafood, so 'fresh from the sea' hides in plain sight as more description of the catch when it is clueing a word of its own.

10 across TIGON

Leave in middle of stint, being cross (5)

Definition: cross · container + middle letters

A tigon is the offspring of a tiger and a lioness, so 'cross' is the breeder's noun for a hybrid rather than a mood.

  • middle of stint → TIN the outer letters of STINT come off and three remain
  • Leave → GO to go is to leave
  • in — 'In' puts the word before it inside the letters that follow, rather than alongside them.
  • middle of — The middle of a five-letter word is the three letters left once you take one off each end.

Walking out halfway through a shift in a thoroughly bad temper.

'Being cross' reads as bad temper and turns out to be zoology: a tiger father and a lioness mother produce this cub, and the word for the pairing is the breeder's.

11 across LATTE

Drink and dish uncovered (5)

Definition: Drink · deletion

A latte is the tall milky coffee, so 'drink' names it by the broadest category it belongs to.

  • dish → PLATTER a platter is the big flat dish food gets served on
  • uncovered — Uncovered means stripped of whatever wraps it, so the first and last letters of the word come away.

A drink and a dish carried out with the lids taken off.

'Uncovered' does the work of a knife: the outside of the word comes away and the middle stays. The 'and' is joinery, not a second helping.

12 across TEST MATCH

Team backed tense striker for international (4,5)

Definition: international · charade + reversal

A Test match is cricket played between two countries, and in British sport an 'international' is exactly that, a fixture between national sides.

  • Team → TES a set is a group of people who go about together, written here the other way round
  • tense → T T is the grammarian's abbreviation for tense
  • striker → MATCH a match is the little stick you strike to make a flame
  • backed — A thing backed goes the other way, and in an across entry that means its letters are written from right to left.

A squad rallying behind an edgy centre-forward who has been picked for his country.

'Striker' is a footballer in the surface and a thing you strike in the wordplay, and the two sports never meet: the answer belongs to cricket.

13 across STAND-IN

Temporary appointment? Reputation no good (5-2)

Definition: Temporary appointment · deletion

A stand-in takes another person's place for a while, so a temporary appointment is what the word names — the substitute, not the paperwork.

  • Reputation → STANDING your standing is the reputation you hold with other people
  • good → G is the abbreviation for good, and G is the letter that goes
  • no — 'No' in front of a thing says it is absent, so the letter standing for it never gets written.

Somebody turned down for a short-term post because their reputation is poor.

Reputation and standing are the same idea in different registers, which hides the fact that one of them is being spelled out in full before a letter is taken off it.

15 across SQUAWK

Sound of complaint seven days after crew loses diamonds (6)

Definition: Sound of complaint · charade + deletion

A squawk is the harsh cry a startled bird makes, and the word doubles as slang for a loud complaint about being hard done by.

  • crew loses diamonds → SQUA a squad is a crew, and D is the card player's diamonds, lifted out of it
  • seven days → WK seven days make a week, shortened to two letters in any diary
  • after — 'After' puts the piece named before it at the back, behind the piece named next.
  • loses — What a word loses is gone from it, so that letter is taken out and not replaced.

A voice raised in protest a week after a gang was relieved of its jewels.

Diamonds are the card suit here rather than the gems the surface is waving about. D for diamonds is worth keeping: bridge and whist hand crosswords all four suits.

17 across ALUMNA

Former student extracting iodine from metallic oxide (6)

Definition: Former student · deletion

An alumna is a woman who studied at a school or university and has since left, which is what 'former student' says in Latin dress.

  • metallic oxide → ALUMINA alumina is the oxide of aluminium, the white powder bauxite is refined into
  • iodine → I is iodine on the periodic table, and I is the letter pulled out
  • extracting — Extracting a thing pulls it out and leaves the rest behind, so the letter named goes missing from the word that follows.

A graduate in a laboratory, pulling iodine out of a metal oxide.

Chemistry supplies both halves of the trick: I is iodine's symbol, and the oxide is a single letter away from a word about universities.

19 across HURRY UP

Husband all set to consume spicy dish, dismissing starter – look lively (5,2)

Definition: look lively · charade + container + deletion

Look lively is the British shout at somebody dawdling, and the answer is the same order given in plainer words.

  • Husband → H H is the standard abbreviation for husband, as on a form
  • spicy dish, dismissing starter → URRY a curry is the spicy dish, and its first letter — the starter of the meal — is the one sent away
  • all set → UP when everything is up and running it is all set
  • to consume — To consume is to swallow whole, so the piece named next disappears inside the letters already found.
  • dismissing — Something dismissed is sent away, so the letter it names never reaches the grid.

A husband ready to tuck into a curry, minus the first course.

'Starter' is the first course in the surface and the first letter in the wordplay, and the whole clue passes as one sentence about a man and his dinner.

22 across SINISTRAL

Crime is a burden, one ignored on the Left? (9)

Definition: on the Left · charade + deletion

Sinistral means situated on or favouring the left side — the left-handed sense — straight from Latin sinister, meaning left.

  • Crime → SIN a sin is a crime against a moral law rather than a statute
  • is → IS the word is used just as it stands, two letters
  • a burden, one ignored → TRAL a trial is a burden to be borne, and the I that stands for one is left out of it
  • ignored — What is ignored is passed over and never written down, so that letter is missing from the word it arrived in.

A jibe that crime is a load which the political left prefers not to notice.

'The Left' is capitalised to look political, and the word wanted is the anatomist's, describing a left-handed person or a shell that coils the other way.

24 across APPAL

Attraction in avoiding source of electric shock (5)

Definition: shock · deletion + first letter

To appal is to shock — to fill somebody with dismay — and both words work as transitive verbs taking the same object.

  • Attraction → APPEAL the appeal of something is its power of attraction
  • source of electric → the letter electric begins with is the one dropped
  • avoiding — Avoiding a thing means not taking it, so the letter named is left out of the word it belongs to.
  • source of — The source of a word is where it starts, which is its first letter and nothing else.

Being drawn to the idea of keeping well away from a live wire.

The sentence ends on 'electric shock', so the definition looks as though it stopped three words earlier; the shock at the end is the one the answer means.

26 across EXTRA

Additional symbol of affection beginning to torment in time (5)

Definition: Additional · container + first letter

Extra means one more than expected, the plain adjective in 'an extra chair', which is what additional says.

  • time → ERA an era is a stretch of time
  • symbol of affection → X X is the kiss at the bottom of a letter
  • torment → T the opening letter of torment
  • beginning to — The beginning of a word is the letter it opens with, and that is all this one gives up.
  • in — 'In' drops what has been found so far inside the word that follows it.

One more kiss that is starting to torment somebody as the years pass.

X for a kiss is the one you sign a card with, and 'in time' is a container instruction dressed as a phrase about things happening eventually.

27 across NITPICKER

One criticising UK currency, keeping it personal to begin with (9)

Definition: One criticising · container + first letter

A nitpicker picks over tiny faults, so 'one criticising' names the person by what they spend their time doing.

  • UK currency → NICKER a nicker is a pound in British slang, as in 'twenty nicker'
  • it → IT the word is taken as it stands, two letters
  • personal → P the first letter of personal
  • keeping — To keep something is to hold on to it, so the word doing the keeping wraps round the letters named next.
  • to begin with — Taken to begin with, a word hands over only the letter at its front.

A fault-finder complaining about sterling while making the whole thing about themselves.

'Nicker' is British slang for a pound, singular and plural alike, and 'keeping it personal' passes as a phrase while doubling as a pair of instructions.

28 across TECHNIC

Private eye capturing Chinese backing popular applied science (7)

Definition: applied science · container + reversal

Technic is the noun for the practical application of science, the body of method a craft or an industry works by.

  • Private eye → TEC a tec is old slang for a detective, clipped out of the word detective itself
  • Chinese → CH Ch. is the dictionary's abbreviation for Chinese
  • popular → NI something in is popular — in fashion — and here those two letters are written backwards
  • capturing — What is captured is held inside its captor, so these letters are trapped in the middle of the word before them.
  • backing — Something backing is going the other way, and in an across entry that means the letters read right to left.

A detective arresting a Chinese national who had backed something popular.

'Private eye' has meant a detective since the pulp magazines, and 'backing popular' reads as supporting a hit while quietly telling you to turn two letters round.

29 across AGORAS

A delightful time, welcoming soldiers in public areas (6)

Definition: public areas · charade + container

An agora was the open marketplace at the heart of a Greek city, the public square where the citizens gathered.

  • A → A the article, taken as one letter
  • delightful time → GAS a gas is a thoroughly good time, the sense in 'the party was a gas'
  • soldiers → OR OR is Other Ranks, the British army's name for everyone below officer level
  • welcoming — A welcome brings somebody in through the door, so the letters named next go inside the word that welcomes them.

A wonderful afternoon that made room for the troops.

OR for Other Ranks is army shorthand a British solver meets constantly, and 'a gas' for a good time is the 1960s slang the surface leans on entirely.

1 down PEDALOS

Pleasure boats? Sod pleasure – not certain at sea (7)

Definition: Pleasure boats · anagram + deletion

Pedalos are the little pedal-driven craft hired out on holiday lakes and beaches, boats kept for pleasure and nothing else.

  • Sod → SOD three letters of raw material, taken as they stand
  • pleasure → PLEASURE eight more letters, though four of them are about to go
  • certain → SURE certain means sure, and that word comes straight back out of the eight letters before it
  • not — 'Not' cancels the word after it, so those letters are struck out of the material instead of being used.
  • at sea — Anything at sea is adrift with no fixed bearing, and letters adrift come ashore in a different order.

Somebody dismissing the whole idea of enjoyment, and none too confident once out on the water.

The setter uses 'pleasure' twice, once in the definition and once as material to be cut down, which is what lets the second one read as an exasperated aside.

2 down LIMIT

Rather drunk, though maintaining motorway’s maximum speed, perhaps (5)

Definition: maximum speed, perhaps · container

A speed limit is a maximum, and 'perhaps' admits that limits cap plenty of other things besides speed.

  • Rather drunk → LIT lit is the slang for somebody who has had a few — lit up
  • motorway’s → MI Britain's first motorway is the M1, and the numeral is written in the grid as the capital letter it looks like
  • though → surface only: it holds the sentence together and hands over no letters
  • maintaining — To maintain something is to keep hold of it, so the word doing the maintaining keeps the next piece inside itself.

A driver who has had a few but is still keeping to the speed the motorway allows.

The M1 is Britain's oldest motorway, and setters spell its number with the letter the numeral resembles. 'Though' is pure glue, propping up a picture of a driver behaving two ways at once.

3 down AMSTERDAM

Morning vapour surrounding road in European city (9)

Definition: European city · charade + container

Amsterdam is the Dutch capital, so 'European city' defines it by category, naming the class it is one instance of.

  • Morning → AM AM is before noon on a clock face, the hours of the morning
  • vapour → STEAM steam is the vapour water turns into
  • road → RD RD is how road is shortened on any street sign
  • surrounding — What surrounds a thing closes round it on both sides, so the letters named next end up buried inside the longer word.

Mist hanging over a road at first light somewhere on the continent.

'Morning' and 'vapour' read as one weather picture, which is what stops them looking like two separate pieces of a build. RD is the abbreviation every British street sign uses.

4 down CUBISTS

Artists in request for more during economies (7)

Definition: Artists · container

Cubists are the painters of the Braque and Picasso school, so 'artists' names them by the wider class they belong to.

  • economies → CUTS cuts are what a government makes when it economises
  • request for more → BIS bis is what a French or Italian audience shouts for an encore — again
  • during — Something happening during another thing sits inside its span, so this piece lands in the middle of the word named next.

Painters calling for a repeat performance in the middle of a round of budget savings.

'Bis' is the continental call for an encore, printed in scores where an English one says repeat. 'In' joins definition to wordplay and does no other work.

5 down TOTEM

Venerated symbol, feature of racetrack, followed by millions (5)

Definition: Venerated symbol · charade

A totem is the carved emblem a clan treats as sacred, which is precisely a symbol that gets venerated.

  • feature of racetrack → TOTE the Tote is the state-run betting pool at British racecourses, the machine that pays out on the horses
  • millions → M M is the abbreviation for million on any set of accounts
  • followed by — What follows comes afterwards, so the letter named next is written at the end of the piece already found.

Something worshipped at the racecourse, with millions of pounds riding on it.

The Tote is Britain's on-course betting pool, the totalisator, and its name is the only piece of racing knowledge this clue asks for.

6 down RIGHT AWAY

Imposition of socialism would mean this immediately? (5,4)

Definition: immediately · cryptic definition

Right away is the everyday phrase for at once, and the clue wants it read a second time as the political right being sent away.

  • Imposition of socialism → socialism sits on the left of politics, so bringing it in is what would clear out the wing opposite
  • would mean this → 'this' points at the answer itself, so the phrase has to be read as a political result rather than as an answer to 'when?'

A grumble that a socialist government would arrive and change everything on the spot.

There is no wordplay to take apart: the phrase is read once as a moment in time and once as a piece of politics, with the wing opposite socialism sent packing.

7 down HONSHU

Hard working? Largely avoid Asian island (6)

Definition: Asian island · charade + deletion

Honshu is Japan's largest island, the one holding Tokyo, so 'Asian island' names it by the category it is an instance of.

  • Hard → H H is the grade stamped on a hard pencil
  • working → ON a machine that is on is working
  • avoid → SHU to shun is to avoid, with the end left off
  • Largely — Largely means for the most part but not the whole of it, so the last letter of the word it governs is dropped.

Asking whether being industrious is mostly something to be dodged.

The question mark after 'Hard working?' invites you to read those two words as one description of a person, and they are separate pieces: a pencil grade and a switch position.

8 down BUTTON

Target working feature of control panel (6)

Definition: feature of control panel · charade

A button is the thing you press on a control panel, so the definition points at it by where it lives rather than by a synonym.

  • Target → BUTT the butt is the mound an archer shoots at, and the butt of a joke takes the same hit
  • working → ON a machine that is on is working

A target in working order, described as part of the machinery in front of you.

A butt is the bank of earth an archer aims at, which is where the butt of a joke gets its sense as well — nothing to do with the end of anything.

14 down APLANATIC

Capital, after editing, to include a note describing certain lenses (9)

Definition: describing certain lenses · anagram

An aplanatic lens is one corrected so that it focuses without spherical aberration, an optician's adjective for that particular fix.

  • Capital → CAPITAL seven letters of raw material — the word itself, not a city
  • a → A the article, one more letter joining the material
  • note → N n is the abbreviation for note in a dictionary or on a score
  • after editing — Editing rewrites whatever it touches, and letters that have been through it no longer stand in their old order.
  • to include — What is included joins the material being worked on rather than sitting outside it.

A capital rewritten to squeeze in a footnote about optics.

'Capital' looks like a city waiting to be named and is the raw letters themselves, which is the step that hides the whole build. N for note is the abbreviation dictionaries use.

16 down UPREARING

Roughly repair gun inclining to shoot high? (9)

Definition: inclining to shoot high · anagram

To uprear is to raise or rear up, so something uprearing is tilting skywards, which is a barrel climbing above the level.

  • repair gun → REPAIRGUN nine letters of raw material, the two words run together
  • Roughly — Something done roughly is done without care for the proper order, which is what becomes of these letters.

A makeshift fix on a weapon that now points well above the target.

'Roughly' opens the clue looking like part of the repair job — a rough repair — and it is the instruction that scrambles everything after it.

18 down AARONIC

Articles with satirical humour, ousting leader of Jewish priesthood (7)

Definition: of Jewish priesthood · charade + deletion

Aaronic means belonging to Aaron's line, the hereditary priesthood of the Hebrew Bible, so the definition is the adjective's plain sense.

  • Articles → AA A is the indefinite article, and the plural asks for two of them
  • satirical humour, ousting leader → RONIC irony is satirical humour, and the leading letter of that word is shown the door
  • ousting — To oust is to throw out, so the letter named next is expelled from the word it was heading.

A magazine of satirical pieces getting rid of its editor.

Aaron was Moses's brother and the first high priest, so everything belonging to that priesthood is named after him. 'Leader' here means a front letter, not the man in charge.

19 down HELOTS

Chap’s imprisoning a good many slaves (6)

Definition: slaves · container

Helots were Sparta's state-owned labourers, tied to the land and treated as property, which is what 'slaves' names them as.

  • Chap’s → HES a chap is a he, and the 's of the surface supplies the S
  • a good many → LOT a lot of something is a good many of it
  • imprisoning — Anything imprisoned is shut inside walls, so these letters are locked up in the middle of the word before them.

A bloke locking up a large number of people.

The apostrophe-s does real work here instead of showing possession. Helots were the serfs of ancient Sparta, owned by the state rather than by any individual master.

20 down PYLORUS

Part of gut grumbling softly, upset, having received nothing (7)

Definition: Part of gut · charade + reversal + container

The pylorus is the valve at the stomach's exit into the small intestine, so 'part of gut' names it by the tract it sits in.

  • softly → P p is the musician's mark for piano, the instruction to play softly
  • grumbling → YLRUS somebody grumbling is surly, and that word is written here the other way up
  • nothing → O O is the round nothing of a scoreboard
  • upset — Upset is upside down, and in a down entry that turns the letters so they read from the bottom of the grid upwards.
  • having received — What is received is taken in, so the letter named next is swallowed by the piece just built.

The digestive sense of 'grumbling' is the decoy — a stomach rumbling away — while the word is actually being used for a sour temper.

21 down ASPECT

Way of viewing a group regularly infiltrated by police constable (6)

Definition: Way of viewing · container

An aspect is the side of a thing you happen to be looking at, the sense in 'seen from another aspect'.

  • a group → ASET a set is a group of people who belong together, and the article comes along with it
  • police constable → PC PC is the rank of an ordinary British uniformed officer, shortened on every charge sheet
  • regularly infiltrated by — An infiltrator slips inside unnoticed, and doing it regularly means the two letters go in at even spacing rather than side by side.

A gathering that the police keep planting an officer inside.

'Regularly' normally asks for alternate letters to be picked out of a word; here it describes how two letters go in, one gap apart. The article at the front is part of the material.

Somebody butting in politely enough on a chatterbox.
