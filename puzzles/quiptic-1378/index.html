Across

1 across SLIGHT

Back of glass lamp is flimsy (6)

Definition: flimsy · charade + last letter

Slight means thin and insubstantial, as a slight partition or a slight frame is, which is what calling something flimsy says of it.

  • glass → S the closing letter of 'glass', taken because only its rear is asked for
  • lamp → LIGHT a lamp is a light, the ordinary word for the thing on the table
  • Back of — The back of a word is its final letter, so only the rear end of the word that follows is wanted.

The rear of a glass lamp turns out to be poorly made.

'Back of' looks like part of the picture — the rear of the lamp — and it is an instruction about letters instead.

4 across BESIDES

Broadcast lesser-known tracks as well (7)

Definition: as well · homophone

'As well' means in addition, and besides carries that same additive sense — 'besides, there's the cost' — rather than its other meaning of alongside.

  • lesser-known tracks → BESIDES the flip side of a single carried the song nobody bought the record for, and those two syllables said aloud can be written a second way
  • Broadcast — What is broadcast goes out over the air and arrives as sound, so what follows is wanted for the noise it makes rather than the way it is written.

An instruction to the radio station to play the obscure old songs too.

'Broadcast' is a favourite anagram indicator and here it means the other thing, said aloud. A seven-inch single had its hit on one face and a filler on the other.

9 across REDUNDANT

No longer needed and turned around (9)

Definition: No longer needed · anagram

A redundant worker or part is one there is no further need for, so 'no longer needed' is the phrase the workplace uses for it.

  • and turned → ANDTURNED nine letters of raw material, the little conjunction counted in
  • around — Sending something around moves it out of its place and back by another route, so these letters arrive in a fresh order.

Something nobody wants any more, and spun about for good measure.

'And' looks like the joint between the two halves of the clue, and it is material itself — leave it out and the letter count comes up three short.

10 across HOSTA

Crush a plant (5)

Definition: plant · charade

A hosta is the broad-leaved shade plant of countless gardens, so 'plant' names it by class.

  • Crush → HOST a crush is a dense pack of people, and a host is that same multitude
  • a → A the clue's own indefinite article, taken as the single letter it already is

Somebody stamping a plant flat.

'Crush' reads as the verb, stamp it flat, and the noun is wanted: the crowd sense, as in a crush at the doors.

11 across NESTS

Loch contains third of otters’ dens (5)

Definition: dens · container + third letter

Nests are the homes animals build for themselves and their young, which is what dens are — the same lair sense, and plural to match.

  • Loch → NESS the most famous loch in Scotland, the one with the monster
  • otters’ → T counting three letters in from the start of the word lands on this one
  • contains — What contains something has it inside itself, so the letter named next goes within the word before it.
  • third of — 'Third of' counts three letters into the word that follows and takes only the one it lands on.

A loch holding a third of the local otters' homes.

'Contains third of' reads as a fraction, a third of all the dens, and it is really two separate instructions about letters standing side by side.

12 across PRE-WASHED

E Shepard working over introduction to Winnie-the-Pooh laid initial coat of paint? (3-6)

Definition: laid initial coat of paint? · anagram + first letter

Something pre-washed has had that preliminary wash of colour laid on first, and the question mark flags how far the setter is stretching the everyday word.

  • E Shepard → ESHEPARD eight letters of raw material: E. H. Shepard drew the original illustrations for A. A. Milne's bear
  • Winnie-the-Pooh → W the opening letter of the bear's name, joining the material to be shuffled
  • working over — To give something a working over is to knock it thoroughly out of shape, which is what becomes of the order of these letters.
  • introduction to — An introduction comes at the very front, so only the opening letter of the name that follows is taken.

Shepard labouring over the opening of Winnie-the-Pooh, having put down a first coat of paint.

A wash is a thin coat of paint brushed on before the proper one, which is the sense the definition turns on, and the hyphen falls after three letters.

13 across SHINGLE

One holding hot pebbles (7)

Definition: pebbles · container

Shingle is the mass of water-worn stones that makes up such a beach, so 'pebbles' names exactly what it consists of.

  • One → SINGLE a single is one of anything — one room, one ticket, one record
  • hot → H h is the letter on the hot tap
  • holding — What holds a thing keeps it inside itself, so the letter named next sits within the word before it.

Someone carrying a handful of warm stones.

Shingle is the coarse pebble covering of a beach, the stuff that crunches underfoot at Brighton, and 'One' is not the numeral but the word for a solitary thing.

15 across NOSTRIL

Opening part of journo’s trilogy (7)

Definition: Opening · hidden word

A nostril is one of the two openings of the nose, so 'opening' defines it by what it is: a hole letting something through.

  • journo’s trilogy → NOSTRIL seven consecutive letters beginning four into the first word and stepping over the apostrophe into the second
  • part of — 'Part of' says what is wanted is a stretch already sitting inside the words that follow, in the order they are given.

The first instalment of a journalist's three-book series.

'Opening' reads as the start of that trilogy and means an aperture instead. The apostrophe is stepped straight over, which is where the run of letters usually gets lost.

17 across ROOSTER

Chicken roll includes last slice of tomato (7)

Definition: Chicken · container + last letter

A rooster is a male chicken, the farmyard cock, so 'chicken' names it by species: one member of the kind.

  • roll → ROSTER a roll of names, the list read out at a meeting, is a roster
  • tomato → O the closing letter of the word
  • includes — What includes something has it in among its own parts, so the letter named next goes inside the word before it.
  • last slice of — A slice off the end of a word is one letter, and 'last' says take the one at the back.

A chicken roll made up with the end slice of a tomato in it.

Every word of the surface is food — chicken, roll, tomato — and not one of them is doing food work: the bird is alive and the roll is a list of names.

19 across CURRENT

Dog tore into moving water (7)

Definition: moving water · charade

A current is the body of water moving steadily in one direction through a river or a sea, so 'moving water' says what it is.

  • Dog → CUR a cur is a mongrel, the crossword's usual scruffy dog
  • tore → RENT rent is the past tense of rend: cloth that was torn was rent

A dog ripping its way into a river.

'Tore into' reads as one phrase, the dog laying about something, and 'into' is only the joint between the wordplay and the definition.

21 across ANCESTRAL

Family ran castle badly (9)

Definition: Family · anagram

Ancestral means belonging to one's forebears, and 'family' does the same adjectival job: the family seat is the ancestral seat.

  • ran castle → RANCASTLE nine letters of raw material, the verb and the building together
  • badly — A thing run badly is not kept in proper order, so the letters beside it come out in disarray.

A family that managed its castle poorly.

'Family' is doing adjective work — the family home, the family silver — which is how it sits at the front looking like the subject of the sentence.

23 across NEWER

Norse jug more recently discovered (5)

Definition: more recently discovered · charade

Newer means of a later date, so between two finds the newer one is whichever came to light more recently.

  • Norse → N N is the dictionary's short form for Norse, the label stamped on Old Norse word origins
  • jug → EWER an ewer is the wide-lipped jug that stood beside a washbasin

A Viking pitcher dug up later than the rest of the hoard.

EWER is one of those words crosswords keep in circulation long after the object left the house, and it turns up wherever a clue says jug or pitcher.

25 across HOLED

Reported part of ship scuttled (5)

Definition: scuttled · homophone

A scuttled ship has had its hull opened up to let the sea in, so it has been holed, and both words sit in the past tense.

  • part of ship → HOLED the cargo space below a ship's deck is its hold, and that syllable said aloud can be spelt a second way
  • Reported — What is reported is passed on by word of mouth, so the words beside it are wanted for the way they sound.

News coming in that part of a ship has been deliberately sunk.

To scuttle a vessel is to sink it on purpose by breaching the hull, which is the state the definition is describing rather than any hurried movement.

26 across BARCELONA

Ban Charlie dancing alone in the city (9)

Definition: the city · charade + anagram

Barcelona is the Catalan capital on Spain's east coast, so 'the city' defines it by class: one particular example of one.

  • Ban → BAR to bar somebody from a place is to ban them
  • Charlie → C Charlie is C in the radio alphabet, alongside Alpha and Bravo
  • alone → ELONA five letters of raw material, shuffled out of their given order
  • dancing — Dancers keep changing places with one another, so the letters told to dance finish somewhere other than where they started.

Charlie barred from the club and dancing by himself somewhere in town.

The surface is one picture of a lone reveller, and the definition is only the last two words; the place meant is Spain's second-largest, on the Mediterranean.

27 across SUSPECT

Dodgy extract of forest ceps usually sent back (7)

Definition: Dodgy · hidden word + reversal

Something dodgy is not to be trusted, and suspect wiring or a suspect motive is exactly that — the adjective, not the noun for a person.

  • forest ceps usually → SUSPECT seven consecutive letters spanning the three words, read from the right-hand end back towards the left
  • extract of — To extract is to pull something out of what it is sitting in, so the letters wanted are already there in the words that follow.
  • sent back — In an across entry, something sent back is read the other way along the row, from the right-hand end towards the left.

A dubious mushroom extract that customers usually send back.

Ceps are the fat brown wild mushrooms prized in French cooking, and every word of the sentence keeps that food-complaint picture going except the first.

28 across STAPLE

Secure essential food stuff (6)

Definition: Secure · double definition

To staple is to fasten with such a clip, and a staple is the basic foodstuff a diet depends on, so the two senses share only a spelling.

  • Secure → the verb: to fasten sheets of paper together with a little wire clip
  • essential food stuff → the noun: the basic food a whole diet rests on, rice or maize or bread

An instruction to lock down the food supplies nobody can do without.

'Secure' reads as an adjective describing that food, safely stored away, and the sense wanted is the office verb done with a wire clip.

1 down SPRINGS

Suddenly starts special syndicates (7)

Definition: Suddenly starts · charade

To spring is to move all at once and without warning, so a thing that springs starts suddenly rather than merely beginning.

  • special → SP sp is the standard short form of special, as chalked on a menu board
  • syndicates → RINGS a ring is a syndicate of people running something crooked — a drugs ring, a betting ring

Somebody abruptly sets up a few special consortia.

'Special' looks like an adjective attached to the syndicates beside it, and it is a two-letter abbreviation standing entirely on its own.

2 down INDUS

River shrouded by Berlin dusk (5)

Definition: River · hidden word

The Indus is the great river of Pakistan, so 'river' defines it by class: one named instance of the thing.

  • Berlin dusk → INDUS five consecutive letters running out of the middle of the first word and into the second
  • shrouded by — A shroud wraps right round whatever it covers, so the words after it are concealing what is wanted inside themselves.

Nightfall coming down over Berlin, with a river somewhere in the gloom.

Berlin and dusk together paint a German evening, and the river hidden in them runs through Pakistan — the picture points at the wrong continent entirely.

3 down HINDSIGHT

Hid things carelessly in retrospect (9)

Definition: retrospect · anagram

Retrospect is the backward look taken at events once they are over, the same understanding-after-the-fact that hindsight names.

  • Hid things → HIDTHINGS nine letters of raw material, the verb and its object run together
  • carelessly — Anything done carelessly is done without attention to where things end up, so these letters land out of their proper places.

Someone who stashed their possessions sloppily, looking back on it later.

'Hid' invites you to hunt for something buried inside the clue, which is what a concealed answer would ask for; the two words are raw material to be shuffled.

4 down BITTERN

Bird found in freezing north (7)

Definition: Bird · charade

A bittern is a heron of the reedbeds, famous for its foghorn boom, so 'bird' defines it by class.

  • freezing → BITTER a bitter wind is a freezing one, the word English keeps for cold that hurts
  • north → N n is the compass abbreviation for north, as printed on any map

A bird you would only come across in the icy far north.

'Found in' looks like a container instruction, one thing sitting inside another, and it is only joinery saying the definition equals what comes next.

5 down SCHWA

Vowel sound endings in Thomas’ poetic triumph wow Swansea (5)

Definition: Vowel sound · last letters

A schwa is the neutral unstressed vowel written ə, so it is a vowel sound rather than any vowel letter.

  • Thomas’ → S the closing letter of the poet's name
  • poetic → C the closing letter of 'poetic'
  • triumph → H the closing letter of 'triumph'
  • wow → W the closing letter of 'wow', which is the letter it opens with as well
  • Swansea → A the closing letter of the Welsh city where Dylan Thomas was born and grew up
  • endings in — An ending is the letter a word finishes on, and 'in' points at the list following, so every word there gives up its last one.

A poetic triumph of Thomas's leaving his home town delighted.

Dylan Thomas came from Swansea, which is what holds the surface together. A schwa is the flat unstressed uh at the end of 'sofa', the commonest vowel in English speech.

6 down DISCHARGE

Free police officers before accusation (9)

Definition: Free · charade

To discharge somebody is to release them from custody or from a duty, which is 'free' used as a verb: to set at liberty.

  • police officers → DIS a DI is a detective inspector in a British force, and the plural of that rank adds the s
  • accusation → CHARGE the formal accusation read out when somebody is booked is a charge
  • before — 'Before' fixes the running order: what it follows is placed in front of what comes after it.

Setting some policemen at liberty before anyone gets round to accusing them.

'Free' reads as an adjective describing those officers, which is the wrong part of speech: the word wanted is the verb, what a court does to a defendant.

7 down SCANDAL

Shoe absorbs cold mud (7)

Definition: mud · container

Mud in the sense of damaging talk — mud-slinging, a name dragged through it — is the same currency as a scandal, so the two swap.

  • Shoe → SANDAL the open summer shoe held on by straps
  • cold → C c is the letter on the cold tap and the weather map's short form
  • absorbs — What a thing absorbs is taken into it and kept there, so the letter named next vanishes inside the word before it.

A sandal soaking up the cold wet dirt of a puddle.

'Mud' is the slung sort, the stuff a reputation gets dragged through, rather than anything on the path — which is where the shoe is trying to send you.

8 down SAMPLE

Portion is small enough (6)

Definition: Portion · charade

A sample is the small portion taken to stand for the whole — the spoonful at the deli counter — so 'portion' is its plain sense.

  • small → S s is the size printed on a clothing label
  • enough → AMPLE an ample supply is more than enough, a plain synonym

A helping quite big enough for anybody.

'Small enough' looks like a single measurement of the portion, and the setter has split it down the middle into two unrelated jobs.

14 down ISOSCELES

Cossie designed by the French with two sides the same (9)

Definition: with two sides the same · anagram

An isosceles triangle is the one whose two sides are of equal length, so the phrase gives its defining property rather than a synonym.

  • Cossie → COSSIE six letters of raw material; a cossie is what British beachgoers call a swimming costume
  • the French → LES 'the' in French, in the form used before a plural noun
  • designed — Something designed has been deliberately laid out afresh, so these letters are set down in an order somebody else chose.

A swimsuit run up by French designers, cut identically on both sides.

Nothing in the sentence mentions triangles: it reads as a fashion credit, and the French definite article is a piece of crossword vocabulary worth keeping by you.

16 down STRINGENT

Tight temporary splint restrains band (9)

Definition: Tight · container

Stringent rules are tightly drawn and rigidly enforced, which is 'tight' in its figurative sense: a tight ship, tight controls.

  • temporary splint → STENT a stent is the little tube a surgeon leaves in an artery to hold it open
  • band → RING a plain band of metal worn on a finger
  • restrains — What restrains a thing holds it in and stops it getting out, so the piece named next is trapped inside the word before it.

A splint fitted for the time being, stopping a bandage from slipping.

'Tight' is the strict sense — tight rules, tight security — not anything physically taut, and 'band' is the metal kind rather than the musical one.

17 down REACHES

Stretches out concerning pains (7)

Definition: Stretches out · charade

To reach is to stretch a hand out towards something, so the plural verb matches the definition's own stretching sense.

  • concerning → RE re heads a memo to mean 'concerning', and crosswords lean on it constantly
  • pains → ACHES the dull pains left by a cold or a long walk are aches

A body stretching itself out to ease a set of nagging pains.

RE is the crossword's workhorse for 'concerning', the word at the head of a memo, and it opens more answers than any other pair of letters.

18 down RAREBIT

Underdone piece in cheese toastie (7)

Definition: cheese toastie · charade

Rarebit is that dish of melted cheese on toast, still on pub menus as Welsh rarebit, which is what 'cheese toastie' describes in modern words.

  • Underdone → RARE a steak served underdone is a rare one
  • piece → BIT a bit is a piece of something — a bit of cake, a bit of string

An undercooked scrap of something found in a toasted cheese sandwich.

Welsh rarebit is seasoned melted cheese poured over toast, a British supper dish, and 'in' is doing nothing but joining the clue's two halves.

19 down CELERY

Trader caught with unknown vegetable (6)

Definition: vegetable · homophone + charade

Celery is the pale ribbed stalk eaten raw with cheese or chopped into a soup base, so 'vegetable' names it by class.

  • Trader → CELER somebody who trades is a seller, and those two syllables said aloud can be written another way
  • unknown → Y y is the algebra teacher's unknown quantity, alongside x and z
  • caught — To catch what somebody says is to hear it, so the word before is wanted for its sound — a loose signal, and the only pointer to sound the clue offers.

A market trader stopped by the police with an unidentified vegetable on him.

The unknown vegetable is a policeman's problem in the surface and a single letter in the wordplay, tacked onto the end of a spoken 'seller'.

20 down TERRACE

Express hesitation in outline for bank (7)

Definition: bank · container

A terrace is a flat bank of ground raised or cut out of a hillside, so 'bank' means the ridge of earth and nothing financial.

  • Express hesitation → ER the noise a speaker makes while groping for the next word, written down as two letters
  • outline → TRACE to outline a shape on paper is to trace it
  • in — 'In' is the plainest of the insertion instructions: the letters just named are dropped inside the word that follows.

Someone voicing their doubts about a sketch drawn up for a bank.

'Bank' sends everybody to money and the sense wanted is a level bank of earth cut into a slope — the same word gardeners and hillside farmers use.

22 down SIDLE

Finally dismiss lazy creep (5)

Definition: creep · charade + last letter

To sidle is to edge along sideways hoping not to be noticed, which is 'creep' in its verb sense of moving unseen.

  • dismiss → S the closing letter of the word
  • lazy → IDLE an idle worker is a lazy one, straight synonym
  • Finally — 'Finally' asks for what comes at the very end, so only the closing letter of the next word is taken.

Sacking somebody bone-idle at long last.

'Creep' reads as the noun a sacked colleague might be called, and the verb is wanted: to move sideways and furtively along a wall.

24 down WHOOP

Which person starts to organise party cheer? (5)

Definition: cheer · charade + first letters

A whoop is the loud cry of delight let out at good news, so 'cheer' means a shout of approval rather than comfort or good spirits.

  • Which person → WHO the question word that asks exactly that
  • organise party → OP the opening letter of each of the two words, taken in order
  • starts to — A word's start is its first letter, and the plural reading takes one from the front of each word that follows.

Somebody asking who is going to start getting the party cheer organised.

The whole sentence reads as one question about who will get things going, and only the final word is the definition — everything before it is instructions.
