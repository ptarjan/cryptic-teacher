Independent Cryptic Crossword No 12,441

Across

1 across ISOBAR

One boozer drinking like this is sign of pressure (6)

Definition: sign of pressure · container + charade

An isobar is the line on a weather map joining places of equal air pressure, so 'sign of pressure' names it as a marking on a chart.

  • One → I one is the single stroke I
  • like this → SO 'so' is the small word for in this way
  • boozer → BAR a boozer is a bar — the premises, not the drinker
  • drinking — a drinker takes what is in the glass right inside himself, so the short word ends up between the two that surround it

'Boozer' means the drinker on the surface and the pub in the wordplay, which is the whole trick of the sentence.

5 across TAVERNAS

Time state reviewed hospital food outlets (8)

Definition: food outlets · charade + reversal

A taverna is a small Greek restaurant serving food and wine, so 'food outlets' is the flat trade description of what they are.

  • Time → T t is the standard abbreviation for time
  • state → AVER to state something formally is to aver it
  • hospital → NAS a sanatorium is a SAN, turned round
  • reviewed — to review is to go back over something already looked at, and going back over these letters reads them the other way

'State' sits between Time and hospital looking like a place on a map, and it is a verb. SAN for a hospital is worth banking — it turns up constantly.

9 across MONGOLIA

I moon gal travelling around foreign land (8)

Definition: foreign land · anagram

Mongolia is the landlocked republic between Russia and China, so 'foreign land' defines it by example — one country standing for any country abroad.

  • I moon gal → MONGOLIA the eight letters of I MOON GAL in a new order
  • travelling around — someone who wanders about does not end up where they set off, and neither do these letters

Hoskins builds his fodder out of a mooning gal, and a picture that vivid is exactly what stops you counting the letters in front of you.

10 across LOLITA

Short lad steals old literary novel (6)

Definition: novel · container + charade + deletion

Nabokov published Lolita in 1955, so 'novel' defines it by example — one famous title standing in for the whole class of thing.

  • lad → LA LAD with its last letter cut off
  • old → O o is the usual abbreviation for old
  • literary → LIT lit is the standard short form for literature
  • Short — cut short means stopping before the end, so the word turns up one letter shy
  • steals — a thief takes a thing into his own keeping, so the word doing the stealing closes round what it takes

'Old literary novel' reads as one description of a dusty book, and the seam falls right at the end of it: only the last word is the definition.

11 across REFRESH

Revive soldiers hard peasant knocked over (7)

Definition: Revive · charade + reversal

To refresh someone is to restore their energy, which is what reviving a flagging person means — both point at liveliness returning, not at the dead.

  • soldiers → RE the Royal Engineers, RE, are the crossword's standard soldiers
  • peasant → FRES a medieval peasant is a SERF, tipped over
  • hard → H H is the pencil grade for hard
  • knocked over — a thing knocked over lies the other way about, so its letters come out end first

RE for soldiers and H for hard are two of the most reused abbreviations in cryptics — the Royal Engineers appear whenever troops or sappers are mentioned.

13 across ATTEND

Minister’s bill blocked by abstaining English knight (6)

Definition: Minister · container + charade

To attend a patient is to minister to them — the old sense of tending to someone's needs, rather than preaching or governing.

  • bill → AD a bill pasted on a wall is an ad
  • abstaining → TT someone teetotal is TT
  • English → E E is the usual abbreviation for English
  • knight → N N is the knight in chess notation
  • blocked by — a pipe that is blocked has been stopped up from within, so what follows lodges in the middle

'Minister' is a verb here, neither a churchman nor a cabinet member, and the apostrophe-s that follows it means no more than 'is'.

14 across UGLI

Uniform infantryman stuffed with large fruit (4)

Definition: fruit · container + charade

The ugli is a Jamaican hybrid of grapefruit and tangerine sold under that trade name, so 'fruit' names it by category.

  • Uniform → U U is Uniform in the radio alphabet
  • infantryman → GI an American infantryman is a GI
  • large → L L is the clothing label for large
  • stuffed with — a bird stuffed for the oven is packed from inside, so the letter goes into the middle of the word

Two military words in a row and not a soldier in the answer: the whole parade dissolves into three abbreviations and a citrus.

16 across SOLUTIONAL

Allusion to changes related to an answer? (10)

Definition: related to an answer · anagram

Solutional is the adjective made from solution, so it describes whatever pertains to an answer; outside cave geology the word is barely used.

  • Allusion to → SOLUTIONAL the ten letters of ALLUSION TO rearranged
  • changes — what changes does not stay as it was, and here it is the order of the letters that will not stay put

The clue quietly describes itself: an allusion changed into something to do with an answer is what every cryptic clue in the grid is doing.

17 across ANKLE SOCKS

Criminal knocks sale for hosiery items (5,5)

Definition: hosiery items · anagram

Ankle socks are the short ones stopping just above the shoe, and hosiery is the trade's collective word for socks, stockings and tights.

  • knocks sale → ANKLESOCKS the ten letters of KNOCKS SALE in a different order
  • Criminal — a criminal will not keep to the rules, and letters described that way will not keep to the order they were given

'Criminal knocks' reads as a burglary report, so the first word passes for a description of the thief instead of the instruction it really is.

21 across SCAG

Horse and colt beset by flipping flatus (4)

Definition: Horse · container + reversal

Horse and scag are both street names for heroin, so the definition trades one dealer's word for another instead of naming an animal.

  • flatus → SAG flatus is wind, GAS, turned round
  • colt → C a racecard marks a colt with a small c
  • beset by — to be beset is to have something closing in from every side, so whatever is beset sits inside
  • flipping — to flip a thing is to turn it end over end, so the word comes out the other way round

The paddock full of horses is there so that the definition looks like one of them, and 'flipping' passes for mild swearing about the smell.

23 across RIBEYE

Cut grass over one trimmed part of garden (6)

Definition: Cut · container + charade + deletion

A ribeye is the steak taken from the rib section, and butchers call any such joint a cut, so the definition names it by trade category.

  • grass → RYE rye is a grass grown as a crop
  • one → I one is the single stroke I
  • part of garden → BE a BED is part of a garden, with its last letter trimmed off
  • over — a sheet laid over a thing covers it from both sides, so the grass closes round what follows
  • trimmed — a trimmed hedge has had its end taken off, and so has this word

Every word belongs to the garden and the clue reads as an afternoon's mowing, right up to the point where 'Cut' turns out to be butchery.

24 across TROLLOP

Tumble, being tackled by excellent hooker (7)

Definition: hooker · container

A trollop is a disreputable woman, and 'hooker' is the blunt slang for a prostitute — the same insult in newer dress, not the front-row forward.

  • excellent → TOP top marks, top drawer — top means excellent
  • Tumble → ROLL to tumble down a slope is to roll
  • tackled by — a player who is tackled gets wrapped up by whoever tackles him, so the other word closes around this one

Everything except the definition is rugby, and the definition is the one word that looks most like rugby of all.

26 across DEEPER

Dutch goalie misses header further down pitch (6)

Definition: further down pitch · charade + deletion

A deeper note sits lower on the scale, so 'further down pitch' is the musical reading — the football pitch of the surface is another word entirely.

  • Dutch → D D is the standard abbreviation for Dutch
  • goalie → EEPER a goalie is a KEEPER, and it is the K at the front that goes missing
  • misses header — the header is the letter at the top of the word, and missing it means starting one letter late

A goalkeeper missing a header is ordinary match commentary, and the football runs on one word past the point where the definition has quietly begun.

27 across BURP GUNS

Pubs rung up to get shooters from America (4,4)

Definition: shooters from America · anagram

A burp gun is GI slang for a submachine gun, named for the belching noise of its rapid fire — 'shooters' being the blunt word for firearms.

  • Pubs rung → BURPGUNS the eight letters of PUBS RUNG in a new order
  • up — a thing that is up has been stirred out of its settled state, so the letters in front of it will not stay as they were

'Rung up' reads as a phone call round the pubs, which hides the fact that only the last of those words is the instruction.

28 across ASTRODON

A way old academic will describe rare dinosaur (8)

Definition: dinosaur · container + charade

Astrodon was a Cretaceous sauropod named for its star-shaped teeth, so 'dinosaur' defines it by class; the name is the obscure half.

  • A → A the clue's own A begins the word
  • way → ST a street is a way, and ST is its abbreviation
  • rare → R r is the dictionary abbreviation for rare
  • old → O o is the usual abbreviation for old
  • academic → DON a university teacher is a don
  • will describe — to describe a circle in geometry is to draw it round something, so these letters are laid around the one that follows

'Describe' is geometry rather than English here — the sense in which a compass describes an arc — and the elderly academic keeps you reading it as speech.

29 across EXCESS

Surplus of clothing size to be picked up (6)

Definition: Surplus · homophone

An excess is the amount over what was needed, which is the leftover a surplus names — though excess also carries a sense of too much that surplus lacks.

  • clothing size → EXCESS the smallest label size is XS, and those two letters said aloud run together as the answer
  • picked up — to pick something up is to catch it by ear, so what matters is the sound of the letters and not their spelling

The size on a garment label is written, never spoken, so the clue's whole trick is asking you to say two ordinary letters out loud.

Down

2 down SNOT RAG

Debtor's not raging about one wiping bill (4,3)

Definition: one wiping bill · hidden word

A snot rag is blunt slang for a handkerchief, and 'bill' lends a bird's beak to a nose, so the phrase names the thing by the job it does.

  • Debtor's not raging → SNOTRAG the letters run straight on from the S of Debtor's through to the RAG in raging
  • about — words written about a subject run all the way round it, and these ones have the answer lying inside them

A bill is a bird's beak, which is what this gets wiped on; the debtor's temper is decoration over letters that were never disturbed at all.

3 down BOGOF

Offer shop might have on crapper loaf regularly (5)

Definition: Offer shop might have · charade + alternate letters

BOGOF is the retail trade's acronym for 'buy one get one free', so the definition points at a supermarket promotion by its shop-floor label.

  • crapper → BOG a bog is the coarse British word for a lavatory
  • loaf → OF the second and fourth letters of lOaF
  • regularly — at steady intervals means every second letter rather than all of them — take them at that spacing

The answer is an acronym you only meet on a shelf label — buy one get one free — and the surface is one long lavatory joke pointing away from the shop.

4 down RELIEFS

Help head to save outstanding sculptures (7)

Definition: outstanding sculptures · charade + first letter

A relief is a carving whose figures project from a flat background, so 'outstanding sculptures' describes them by standing proud of the surface.

  • Help → RELIEF aid sent to a disaster area is relief
  • save → S the letter standing at the head of save
  • head — the head of a word is the letter it begins with, so only that one letter is taken

'Outstanding' is doing literal work — these figures stand out from the flat stone behind them — while the surface offers it as praise.

5 down TEA

Old volunteers consuming ecstasy and grass (3)

Definition: grass · container

Grass and tea are both old street names for cannabis, so the definition swaps one piece of slang for another rather than naming a drink.

  • Old volunteers → TA the Territorial Army, TA, is the old name for the Army Reserve
  • ecstasy → E E is the tabloid abbreviation for ecstasy
  • consuming — to consume is to take something right inside you, so the letter lands within the word rather than beside it

Stop reading 'grass' as lawn and the whole clue is drugs: the answer has meant cannabis since the jazz age.

6 down VALIANT

One can served up worker filled with Courage (7)

Definition: filled with Courage · charade + reversal

Someone valiant is brimming with bravery, so 'filled with Courage' is that in plain English — the capital C only dresses it as the brewer.

  • One can → VALI one is I and a can is a LAV, and I LAV read upwards is VALI
  • worker → ANT the crossword's tireless worker is the ant
  • served up — what is served up in a down clue comes back towards the top, so those letters climb instead of descending

Courage is a brewery as well as a virtue, so the capital letter does pub-sign duty in a clue already full of cans and lavatories.

7 down RELATIONS

It is people like your mother-in-law ... (9)

Definition: It · double definition

One sense is the family — a mother-in-law is a relation by marriage; the other is the polite plural for sex, the same coyness as 'it'.

  • It → the coy old pronoun standing in for sex, avoiding the plain plural word for it
  • people like your mother-in-law → the plural noun for family you acquire by marriage rather than by blood

The ellipsis carries the sentence on into 8 down, which is what lets a clue this short read as half a grumble about the in-laws.

8 down ANTENNA

... Nanna upset about ultimately flat English dish? (7)

Definition: dish · anagram + container + last letter

An antenna is the aerial that catches a broadcast, and the domestic one is a satellite dish, so the question mark flags a definition by example.

  • Nanna → NANNA five letters to be rearranged, and they end up split either side of what goes in
  • ultimately flat → T the last letter of flaT
  • English → E E is the usual abbreviation for English
  • upset — a household upset leaves everything out of its usual place, and so does an upset word
  • about — a coat worn about you goes round the outside, so the jumbled letters wrap the pair that follow
  • ultimately — ultimately means at the very end, so it asks for the final letter of the word beside it

Reading on from 7 down, a grandmother in a temper about a flat English dinner is a complete picture — and every word of it is machinery.

12 down HELLCAT

Fury? That guy will coast on occasion! (7)

Definition: Fury · charade + alternate letters

A hellcat is a savagely bad-tempered woman, and the capitalised Furies of myth were avenging females, so the word answers to either reading.

  • That guy will → HELL that guy will is HE'LL, and an apostrophe is never written into the grid
  • coast → CAT the first, third and fifth letters of CoAsT
  • on occasion — on occasion means now and then rather than throughout, so the letters are taken now and then — every other one

The exclamation mark sells 'coast on occasion' as a lazy afternoon by the sea, which is where the every-other-letter instruction hides.

15 down ILL TEMPER

Idiotic PM, I tell old head of state, creates irritability (3,6)

Definition: irritability · anagram

Ill temper is a settled disposition to snap at people, which is what irritability names — the state, not one outburst of it.

  • PM, I tell → PMITELL seven of the nine letters, taken exactly as printed
  • old head of state → ER the late Queen signed herself ER, Elizabeth Regina — the head of state before this one
  • Idiotic — an idiotic arrangement is one nobody sensible would have chosen, so the order these letters arrive in cannot be trusted

The comma makes the fodder sound like a lecture delivered to a prime minister. ER is worth banking: any old queen in a clue may be asking for it.

18 down NAILERS

We might beat The Hammers in Linares, surprisingly (7)

Definition: We might beat The Hammers · anagram

'The Nailers' is Belper Town's nickname, from the town's nail trade, so the boast sets one hammering side against West Ham.

  • Linares → NAILERS the seven letters of LINARES rearranged
  • surprisingly — a surprise is what nobody saw coming, and these letters do not come out in the order anyone expects

Linares is a Spanish town famous for its chess tournament, which keeps the sporting surface plausible while its letters do all the work.

19 down SMEARED

Grease-covered slice of meat dons burnt on the outside (7)

Definition: Grease-covered · container + first letter

Something smeared has had a greasy substance wiped across it, so 'grease-covered' names the state the wiping leaves behind.

  • burnt on the outside → SEARED meat browned in a hot pan is seared — caught on the outside only
  • slice of meat → M a slice off the front of MEAT is its first letter
  • dons — to don a coat is to put it on over yourself, so the single letter ends up wearing the longer word around it

Both halves of the wordplay talk cooking, so nothing announces itself as an instruction; 'dons' is the only word doing a job rather than describing a kitchen.

20 down SCOURGE

Whip a person who causes great suffering (7)

Definition: Whip · double definition

The literal sense is the whip used for flogging, and the figurative one moved from the lash to whoever wields it — a tyrant or a plague on others.

  • Whip → the knotted lash used for flogging
  • a person who causes great suffering → Attila earned a famous epithet meaning 'affliction sent by God' — this half names the affliction itself, not the instrument

Two definitions with no join between them, which is why the clue reads as one smooth phrase; only the length tells you nothing else is coming.

22 down APPENDS

A quiet writer vacuous Duchess puts on? (7)

Definition: puts on · charade + outer letters

To append is to tack something onto the end of what is already there, which is one plain sense of 'puts on' — adding rather than wearing.

  • A → A the clue's own A opens the answer
  • quiet → P p is piano, the musician's mark for quiet
  • writer → PEN the writer here is the implement rather than the author
  • Duchess → DS DucheS emptied of everything between its ends
  • vacuous — vacuous means empty-headed, and a word emptied out keeps nothing but the two letters at its ends

'Puts on' invites you to watch a duchess dressing, when the answer wants the dry office sense of adding a paragraph at the bottom.

25 down LOGIC

Reason I will get into record by contralto (5)

Definition: Reason · container + charade

Logic is the discipline of valid inference, and reason in the sense of sound thinking is a plain synonym for it — not reason meaning a cause.

  • record → LOG a ship's daily record is its log
  • I → I the clue's I is used as the letter itself
  • contralto → C vocal parts are marked S, C, T and B — C is the contralto
  • get into — to get into a place is to end up inside it, so the letter lands within the pair rather than after them

'Reason' is the noun, not the verb, and the surface sounds like a singer muscling onto somebody's recording — which is the picture 'get into' was chosen for.

27 down BIN

Case of red, say, one put in case of brown (3)

Definition: Case of red, say · container + outer letters

A bin is the rack a wine is laid down in, so a container of red is one example of what bins hold, which is what the 'say' flags.

  • brown → BN the two letters at the ends of BROWN
  • one → I one is the single stroke I
  • put in — to put a thing in is to place it inside something else, so the letter sits within the word
  • case of — a case is what encloses a thing, so this asks only for the letters at the two ends

'Case' does two jobs a few words apart — wine storage in the definition, a word's outer shell in the wordplay — and 'say' marks the definition as an example.
