Observer Everyman Crossword No 4,090

Across

1 across CRAM

March curtailed, flipping study intensely (4)

Definition: study intensely · deletion + reversal

To cram is to study intensely in one short burst — the night-before revision every student has done at least once.

  • March → MARC The month written out, then docked of its final letter.
  • curtailed — A curtailed animal has had its tail docked, so the word gives up the letter it ends on.
  • flipping — Flipping a thing turns it over end for end, so the letters are read back the other way.

A march cut short, and somebody hunched over their books in a temper.

'Flipping' is a mild British oath in the surface, which is exactly what disguises it as an instruction, and 'March' is the calendar month rather than a protest.

3 across GULF OF ADEN

Loud gale found smashing bay (4,2,4)

Definition: bay · anagram

The Gulf of Aden is the arm of sea between Yemen and Somalia, and a gulf is a bay — the definition gives the general shape, the answer one instance.

  • Loud → F F is forte, the musician's mark for loud.
  • gale found → GALEFOUND Nine letters of fodder, joined to the F.
  • smashing — Smash something and every piece is still there but nothing is where it was, which is what happens to these letters.

A roaring gale found battering a bay.

'Found' looks like a verb reporting the storm and is fodder instead, which leaves a one-word definition stranded at the end where nobody looks for it.

9 across UTRECHT

Primarily – unsurprisingly – this Randstad eco city has trams! (7)

Definition: unsurprisingly – this Randstad eco city has trams · &lit + first letters

Utrecht is one of the four Randstad cities, bike- and tram-ridden and proud of it, so the sentence that spells it describes it too.

  • unsurprisingly – this → UT The opening letters of those two words, in the order printed.
  • Randstad eco city has trams → RECHT Five more opening letters, one from each remaining word.
  • Primarily — Primarily points at what comes first, so only the opening letter of each word that follows is wanted.

A smug boast that a green Dutch city is of course full of trams.

The Randstad is the Dutch conurbation taking in Amsterdam, Rotterdam, The Hague and this city, so read straight the sentence is true of the place it also spells out.

11 across NOUGHTS

Pair of characters from 2001 (7)

Definition: Pair of characters from 2001 · cryptic definition

Noughts are zeros, and the digits 2-0-0-1 hold exactly two of them, so the clue names the pair sitting in the middle of the year.

  • characters from 2001 → The surface offers Kubrick's film and the people and machines in it — HAL, Bowman, Poole.
  • Pair of → Type the year out and there are four characters on the page; two of them are identical, and those two are what the clue wants.

Two of the cast of Kubrick's space film.

'Characters' means typed symbols rather than people, and the year 2001 is written with two zeros side by side in the middle — and a zero is a nought.

12 across HARBOUR MASTER

Nurse schooner, perhaps port overseen by me (7,6)

Definition: port overseen by me · charade

A harbour master is the officer in charge of a port, so a port overseen by them is exactly the job description.

  • Nurse → HARBOUR You nurse a grudge or you harbour one; the two verbs are interchangeable.
  • schooner → MASTER A schooner is a sailing vessel counted by its masts — a two-master.
  • perhaps — Perhaps flags an example offered for the general thing, and it is stretched here: the vessel named is one of the sort counted by its masts.

Somebody nursing a schooner of port and keeping an eye on the water.

'Schooner' is a glass in the surface and a ship in the wordplay, and 'port' is the drink rather than the dock. The definition is spoken in the answer's own voice.

14 across REPRO

Carbon copy, fireproof (a bit) (5)

Definition: Carbon copy · hidden word

A repro is a reproduction, a facsimile of an original, and a carbon copy is the same idea from the typewriter age.

  • fireproof → REPRO Five consecutive letters lie inside this word, starting at its third.
  • a bit — Only a bit of the word is wanted, and a bit of a word is a run of its letters taken just as they stand.

An exact duplicate that will stand a certain amount of fire.

The bracket does two jobs at once — it reads as the setter qualifying 'fireproof', and it is the instruction to take only part of the word.

15 across OMINOUSLY

‘Mousy’ lion revived in alarming fashion (9)

Definition: in alarming fashion · anagram

Ominously means in a way that hints something bad is coming, which is alarming, and the adverb slots straight into the definition's 'in ... fashion' shape.

  • ‘Mousy’ lion → MOUSYLION Nine letters of fodder, exactly the length wanted.
  • revived — Something revived is put back together and set going again, and a thing rebuilt need not go back the way it came apart.

A timid lion brought back to life.

The quotation marks round 'Mousy' invite you to hunt for a second meaning and carry none — they are there to make the lion sound like a character in a story.

16 across PENNILESS

Poor, like a ‘Dreadful’ description of gruesome Victoriana? (9)

Definition: Poor · charade

Penniless means without a coin to your name, so 'Poor' is a plain synonym taken at its strongest: not short of money but out of it altogether.

  • description of gruesome Victoriana → PENNI Victorian shockers were sold as penny dreadfuls, cheap sensational fiction at a penny an issue, and the coin's name gives up its Y to a joining I.
  • like a ‘Dreadful’ → LESS The clue quotes only the second half of that title, with no coin in front of it, and the ending supplies a word for being without something.

The shabby, blood-soaked magazines the Victorians bought by the armful.

A penny dreadful is a cheap Victorian horror serial. Print 'Dreadful' on its own, as the clue does, and the penny has gone missing — which is the joke and the spelling in one move.

18 across KYOTO

Love to yak emptily going round city (5)

Definition: city · charade + outer letters + reversal

Kyoto is the old imperial capital of Japan, so 'city' is the bare category and the answer one very famous instance of it.

  • Love → O Love is a score of nothing in tennis, and nothing is written as this letter.
  • to → TO The clue's own word, taken exactly as it stands.
  • yak emptily → YK The animal's name with its middle taken out, leaving the two letters on the outside.
  • emptily — Emptied of its contents a word keeps only its outside, and the middle letters are the filling that goes.
  • going round — Going round is turning about, so the assembled letters are read from the far end back towards the front.

A fondness for empty chatter doing the rounds.

'Yak' is the chattering, not the ox, and 'Love' is the tennis nothing rather than affection. The three pieces are built in clue order and only then turned round.

20 across GHETTO BLASTER

Dancing, gather bottles and music-maker (6,7)

Definition: music-maker · anagram

A ghetto blaster is a big portable stereo, and 'music-maker' is loose but fair: what it makes is the noise, not the composition.

  • gather bottles → GATHERBOTTLES Thirteen letters of fodder, which is exactly the length wanted.
  • Dancing — Dancers do not hold their positions, so letters set dancing end up somewhere other than where they started.

Clearing up after a party: collect the empties, and the stereo.

'Dancing' reads as part of the party and is the shuffling instruction instead. Counting the fodder against the enumeration is the check to make before trusting an anagram this long.

23 across SPOILER

Aerodynamic device that might come with an alert (7)

Definition: Aerodynamic device · double definition

On a car it is the wing that spoils airflow and kills lift; in a review it is the plot given away, the thing a 'spoiler alert' warns of.

  • Aerodynamic device → The car reading: the wing on a boot lid that presses the back of the car down.
  • that might come with an alert → The other reading: the fact about a plot that a warning gets put in front of.

A gadget on a car that comes with its own warning light.

Nothing is taken apart here, and the second half is a definition by association rather than a synonym — the setter leaning on a phrase everybody who reads reviews online now knows.

24 across CELLIST

One takes a bow before and after concert (7)

Definition: One takes a bow before and after concert · cryptic definition

A cellist plays with a bow and, as a performer, bows to the audience, so the clue is true of them in two senses at once.

  • takes a bow before → Before the music the bow is an object: a stick strung with horsehair, picked up and drawn across the strings.
  • after concert → After the music the bow is an action: a bend from the waist while the audience claps.

A performer bowing at the start of the concert as well as at the end.

The two bows are not even pronounced the same — the stick rhymes with 'go', the bend with 'now' — and the italics on 'and' are there to insist that you take both.

25 across UNIMPOSING

After university, Frenchman acting affectedly: not impressive (10)

Definition: not impressive · charade

Unimposing means lacking presence or grandeur, so 'not impressive' is the phrase read straight, with the answer's own un- doing the negating.

  • university → UNI The everyday clipping of the word, and a standing short form in crosswords.
  • Frenchman → M M is Monsieur, the French form of address as abbreviated on an envelope.
  • acting affectedly → POSING To pose is to strike an attitude for effect.
  • After — After says where the rest goes: everything named next follows the university instead of coming before it.

A French graduate showing off, and impressing nobody at all.

Everything before the colon is wordplay and everything after it defines. M for Monsieur is the piece worth owning: 'Frenchman' shrinks to that single letter in crosswords all the time.

26 across CHAS

Disrespectfully, Your Majesty, two or three are needed for a dance (4)

Definition: Your Majesty · double definition

Chas is the tabloid shortening of Charles, so it addresses the King familiarly; and cha is the dance syllable, doubled or tripled into cha-cha and cha-cha-cha.

  • Your Majesty → The reigning British king is Charles III, and this is how a tabloid headline would address him.
  • two or three are needed for a dance → The Latin American dance is named by repeating one syllable — twice in one version, three times in the other.
  • Disrespectfully — Calling the King by a clipped nickname rather than his title is the disrespect being asked for, so the formal address is to be shortened and made familiar.

Somebody being rude about the King in the middle of a conversation about ballroom.

The same four letters are read twice over: a familiar form of the King's name, and the syllable a dance is built from. The comma after 'Majesty' is what makes it look like one sentence addressed to him.

Down

1 down COUGH DROPS

Cold rough pods crushed for help when you’re down with something … (5,5)

Definition: help when you’re down with something · anagram

A cough drop is the medicated sweet you suck when a cold has laid you up, so it really is help when you are down with something.

  • Cold → C C is the standing short form for cold, as on a tap.
  • rough pods → ROUGHPODS Nine letters of fodder, joined to the C in front before the shuffle.
  • crushed — Something crushed has lost its shape altogether, so the letters it was made of can be reset in any order at all.

Frost-bitten seed pods being pounded up into some sort of folk remedy.

'Cold' is weather in the surface and a single letter in the wordplay, and the ellipsis hands the sentence on to 2 down, which carries on the idea of things falling.

2 down AIRDROP

… or rapid deployment in some things coming down? (7)

Definition: some things coming down · anagram

An airdrop is a load of supplies parachuted out of a plane, so what it names is precisely things on their way down.

  • or rapid → ORRAPID Seven letters of fodder, the two words taken exactly as printed.
  • deployment — A deployment moves troops or kit into new positions, and letters deployed end up somewhere other than where they were given.

Supplies shoved out of an aircraft in a hurry.

'Rapid deployment' is army jargon and the surface wants you to read it whole; the setter has split it in two, keeping 'rapid' as letters and turning 'deployment' into the instruction.

4 down UTTER

Complete state (5)

Definition: Complete · double definition

Utter means complete or absolute, as in utter nonsense, and to utter is to state something aloud — one word doing duty as adjective and verb.

  • Complete → The adjective sense: total, absolute, with nothing held back.
  • state → The verb sense: to say a thing out loud, as a witness does in court.

Two words, two senses, nothing to take apart — and the only cover a clue this short can carry is that 'state' looks like a noun.

5 down FANTASIAS

Cool: Eliot embracing ‘the East’ in freeform works (9)

Definition: freeform works · charade + container

A fantasia is a piece of music that follows no fixed form, so 'freeform works' is the plain description of the genre.

  • Cool → FAN The thing you switch on to cool a room, and the verb to cool air.
  • Eliot → TS The poet's two initials, T and S.
  • ‘the East’ → ASIA The East as a continent, which is why the setter puts it in quotes.
  • embracing — To embrace something is to get your arms right round it, so the pair of initials opens up and takes the other word inside.

T. S. Eliot with his arms round the Orient, and sounding cool about it.

The colon after 'Cool' makes the word look like a heading; it is a one-word clue to the first chunk. Eliot really did quote the Upanishads, which is what makes the surface hold together.

6 down FRUIT COCKTAIL

Dessert: tomato on a rusty nail? (5,8)

Definition: Dessert · charade

Fruit cocktail is the tinned mixture of chopped fruit served at the end of a meal, so 'Dessert' names the course it belongs to.

  • tomato → FRUIT A tomato is botanically one of these, whatever the kitchen does with it.
  • a rusty nail → COCKTAIL A Rusty Nail is whisky with Drambuie — the drink, not the ironmongery.

A tomato served on an old nail, which is nobody's idea of pudding.

Both halves turn on a word read in the wrong register: 'tomato' as botany rather than salad, 'a rusty nail' as a bar order rather than a hazard.

7 down DEHORNS

Removes antlers from deer, regularly cropped, first to last? (7)

Definition: Removes antlers from · charade + alternate letters + cycling

To dehorn an animal is to take its horns off, and a deer's horns are its antlers, so the definition is the operation itself.

  • deer → DE Every other letter of the animal, starting with the one it begins on.
  • cropped → SHORN Cropped hair is shorn hair; this is the word as it stands, before anything moves.
  • regularly — Taken regularly means taken at a fixed step, so every second letter is picked up and the ones between are dropped.
  • first to last — The phrase is positional rather than descriptive: whichever letter stands at the front goes round to the back and the rest close up.

A deer given a routine trim, from one end of it to the other.

'First to last' sounds like a description of the deer's length and is an instruction: SHORN hands over the S at its front and takes it back at its end.

8 down NOSH

Grub meaning peace won’t come? (4)

Definition: Grub · charade

Nosh is informal British English for food, the same casual register as grub — you would use either in a canteen and neither on a menu.

  • won’t come → NO A flat refusal: ask whether it is coming and this is the answer.
  • peace → SH The noise you make when you want quiet, spelt the way comic books spell it.

Food arriving is the signal that the quiet is over.

The two pieces spell the surface's own joke: put together they read as a refusal to hush. 'Grub' sits alone at the front doing the defining.

10 down CROCODILE CLIP

Cut around nasty old icicle: it grips tightly (9,4)

Definition: it grips tightly · container + anagram

A crocodile clip is the spring clip with toothed jaws used on test leads, and those jaws snap shut and hold, which is gripping tightly.

  • Cut → CROP To crop is to cut; this is the word that splits open to make room.
  • old icicle → CODILECLI The nine letters of those two words, reset in a new order.
  • around — Going around a thing means enclosing it, so the outer word splits open and what follows sits in the gap.
  • nasty — A nasty state of affairs is one that has gone wrong, and letters gone wrong are letters no longer in their proper order.

Hacking away at a horrible old icicle that has frozen onto something.

'Cut' is a word to be broken open rather than an instruction to shorten anything, and nine letters go into the gap it leaves — which is why nothing here is a straight anagram.

13 down HYPOCRITES

Charlatans’ trendier analyses heard (10)

Definition: Charlatans · charade + homophone

A hypocrite professes what they do not believe and a charlatan passes a pose off as the real thing — the same pretence, one moral, one professional.

  • trendier → HYPO Trendier is hipper, and said aloud the front of that word comes out spelt quite differently.
  • analyses → CRITES Crits are critiques, art-school shorthand for a written analysis, and they land on the ear as the tail of a longer word.
  • heard — What is heard reaches the ear and not the eye, so the words are to be judged by their sound and written down again.

Quacks offering their more fashionable readings of things, reported second-hand.

'Charlatans' at the front is the definition; the rest is two words said aloud. In a non-rhotic British accent the R ending the first is not sounded, so the pair runs together as one long word.

15 down OVERTURES

Offers introductory music (9)

Definition: Offers · double definition

Overtures are opening approaches — you make overtures to somebody — and an overture is also the orchestral piece that opens an opera.

  • Offers → The plural noun: the first tentative approaches you make towards somebody.
  • introductory music → The orchestral piece played before the curtain goes up.

An advertisement promising some music to start you off.

Both halves are ordinary senses of the same word, and the only cover the setter has is that 'Offers' reads as a verb introducing the rest of the sentence.

17 down NEGRONI

One’s mixed with gin and drop of rosso (7)

Definition: One’s mixed with gin and drop of rosso · &lit + anagram + first letter

The whole clue is the drink's own recipe: equal measures with gin among them and red vermouth alongside, which is what a Negroni is.

  • One → ONE The word itself, three letters of fodder.
  • gin → GIN Three more letters of fodder, the spirit named outright.
  • drop of rosso → R Rosso is Italian for red, as in red vermouth, and a drop of a word is the letter it starts on.
  • mixed — Mixed is a drinks word in the surface and a shuffling word in the wordplay: mixed letters are letters no longer in the order given.

Somebody at the drinks trolley building a cocktail out of gin and a splash of red vermouth.

Read straight the sentence is a recipe — one measure of gin, a drop of red vermouth — and read as letters it is the same words shuffled, so the clue defines and spells with one set of words.

19 down OSTRICH

Bad borscht; no starter; Everyman hiding: he avoids problems (7)

Definition: he avoids problems · anagram + deletion

An ostrich is proverbially the creature that hides its head in the sand, so figuratively it is the person who will not face trouble.

  • borscht → ORSCHT The beetroot soup of Eastern Europe, with the letter it starts on left off.
  • Everyman → I Everyman is the name this setter signs, so the word stands for the setter, written as the one-letter pronoun.
  • Bad — Food gone bad has broken down into something else, and letters gone bad no longer sit in the order they were served in.
  • no starter — No starter means the first course is missing, and a word's first course is the letter it begins on.
  • hiding — Something hiding has gone in among other things, so the extra letter is tucked into the mixture rather than added at either end.

A rotten bowl of beetroot soup, no first course, and the setter keeping out of sight.

Everyman signing into a clue as I is a house habit worth learning. The definition is a whole clause because it rests on the bird's reputation for burying its head rather than on a synonym.

21 down BACON

Philosopher’s preparin’ dish for Paul Hollywood, you say? (5)

Definition: Philosopher · homophone

The answer is the surname of Francis Bacon, the Elizabethan philosopher of the scientific method, so the definition names him by his trade.

  • preparin’ dish for Paul Hollywood → BACON Paul Hollywood judges The Great British Bake Off, so a dish prepared for him is bakin' — and the clue has dropped the G for you already.
  • you say — 'You say' hands the clue over to the ear: you are being told what the words sound like, not how they are spelt.

A contestant getting a dish ready for the Bake Off judge.

'Philosopher's' looks possessive and is the definition with an 'is' hooked on: Francis Bacon, the Elizabethan who argued that science should proceed by experiment.

22 down ESAU

Biblical brother covered in Worcestershire sauce (4)

Definition: Biblical brother · hidden word

Esau is Jacob's elder twin in Genesis, the one who sold his birthright, so 'Biblical brother' names him by the relationship his whole story turns on.

  • Worcestershire sauce → ESAU Four letters run consecutively across the join between these two words.
  • covered in — To be covered in something is to lie under it out of sight, so the name is buried inside the words that follow rather than spelt out by them.

A brother out of Genesis smothered in brown sauce.

Worcestershire sauce is the dark British condiment, and the letters run across the gap between the two words — the hardest place in a clue to notice a name.
