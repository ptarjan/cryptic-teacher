1 across PROCURE

Manage to obtain expert remedy (7)

Definition: Manage to obtain · charade

To procure something is to succeed in getting hold of it, usually with some effort, which is exactly what 'manage to obtain' spells out.

  • expert → PRO a pro is somebody who does the thing for a living, so an expert at it
  • remedy → CURE the remedy that puts an illness right

Somebody managing to get hold of a first-rate treatment.

'Manage' on its own looks like the definition — to cope, to run a business — but the definition runs on through 'to obtain', and the two wordplay words are the last two.

5 across WARLOCK

Fight to secure wizard (7)

Definition: wizard · charade

A warlock is a male witch, so 'wizard' is the everyday word for somebody in the same line of magic.

  • Fight → WAR the fight that nations get into
  • secure → LOCK to secure a door is to lock it

A battle fought to capture a magician.

'To secure' looks like joinery — a fight in order to win something — and it is the second half of the build, the verb meaning to fasten.

10 across CARMEN

The opera for petrolheads? (6)

Definition: The opera · charade

Carmen is Bizet's opera about the cigarette girl of Seville, so 'the opera' defines it by the class one famous instance belongs to.

  • petrolheads → CAR petrol goes into the vehicle these enthusiasts are mad about
  • petrolheads → MEN the heads in question are the blokes doing the enthusing

The one opera a car fanatic would happily sit through.

A petrolhead is British slang for somebody obsessed with cars and driving, and the question mark is the setter admitting he has quietly made that crowd male.

11 across ENTIRETY

Eternity may be represented as wholeness (8)

Definition: wholeness · anagram

The entirety of a thing is the whole of it taken together, so 'wholeness' is the abstract noun for that same completeness.

  • Eternity → ENTIRETY the eight letters of the fodder, resettled
  • may be represented — To represent a thing is to set it out in some other form, and the other form here keeps every letter while changing their order.

Forever standing in as a picture of completeness.

'May be represented as' reads like a line of philosophy, one abstraction standing in for another, and it is a plain instruction to shuffle.

12 across GUV

Gaffer seen in good sort of light (3)

Definition: Gaffer · charade

Guv is British working slang for the boss, short for governor, and a gaffer is the same man on a building site or a film crew.

  • good → G standard abbreviation
  • sort of light → UV ultraviolet, the light just beyond the violet end of the spectrum

The boss being shown in a flattering light.

'Seen in a good light' is the whole surface joke, the boss looking his best, while the sentence is really asking for a named kind of light.

13 across THRONE

Disconcerted in hearing having elevated seat (6)

Definition: elevated seat · homophone

A throne is the raised ceremonial chair a monarch sits on, so 'elevated seat' describes it by its height and its job at once.

  • Disconcerted → THRONE somebody disconcerted is thrown by what has happened, and that word said aloud is written down another way
  • in hearing — Something taken in hearing is judged by ear alone, so the spelling is free to change as long as the noise survives.

Somebody unsettled at a hearing by where they had been seated.

'Hearing' does two jobs: on the surface it is a tribunal you attend, and in the wordplay it is the ear that lets one spelling stand in for another.

14 across ONRUSHES

Charges and unhorses criminal (8)

Definition: Charges · anagram

An onrush is a sudden surge forward, so 'charges' names the same headlong movements in the plural.

  • unhorses → ONRUSHES those eight letters put into a new order
  • criminal — A criminal is somebody who will not keep to the rules, and the letters he is standing next to will not keep to their order either.

A cavalry attack that knocks a villain clean out of the saddle.

'Criminal' sits where an adjective describing the villain would sit, which is what makes the sentence read as a jousting report rather than as an order to shuffle.

15 across BRIEF

A lawyer, but not for long? (5)

Definition: A lawyer · double definition

In British slang your brief is the barrister or solicitor speaking for you, and brief also means lasting only a short while.

  • A lawyer → the slang a defendant uses at the police station when asking for representation
  • not for long → the ordinary adjective for something that is over quickly

A lawyer who is not going to be around very long.

The two senses arrive from opposite directions: the short one from the Latin brevis, the legal one from the written brief that a solicitor hands up to counsel.

16 across QUALIFIED

Given a certificate with some reservations (9)

Definition: Given a certificate · double definition

Pass the exams and you are qualified; agree only partly and your approval is qualified, hedged about with conditions.

  • Given a certificate → the sense that comes with letters after your name
  • with some reservations → praise or agreement with conditions attached to it

Somebody handed their certificate, though not without a few doubts.

One word covers the exam pass and the hedged agreement, and the older sense is the bridge: to qualify a statement is to put limits on it.

19 across AYE AYE SIR

Two words of compliance in song, or three such at sea (3,3,3)

Definition: three such at sea · charade + container

A sailor acknowledges an order with three words of compliance, the last of them the officer's title, which is what 'at sea' pins down.

  • song → AIR an air is a tune, and it opens up after its first letter
  • Two words of compliance → YEAYES yea and yes, two ways of saying you agree, laid end to end
  • in — 'In' puts one thing inside another, so the pair of assents is dropped into the middle of the word for a song.

Agreeing twice over in a song, and three times over on board ship.

Both halves of the clue talk about words of compliance, which is the disguise: the two being counted in the wordplay are not the three the sailor says.

21 across LEARN

Major Shakespeare role, new, to memorise (5)

Definition: to memorise · charade

To learn a part is to get it by heart, so 'to memorise' is the plain verb for what an actor does with a script.

  • Major Shakespeare role → LEAR the old king who divides his kingdom, one of the longest parts in the canon
  • new → N standard abbreviation

A big Shakespearean part, freshly cast, waiting to be got by heart.

The commas do the joining, and the definition sits at the end where the surface makes it look like the purpose of the sentence rather than its answer.

24 across MUNCHKIN

Small child to eat with family (8)

Definition: Small child · charade

The Munchkins are the little people of Oz, and the name has passed into ordinary affectionate speech for any small child.

  • to eat → MUNCH to munch is to chew steadily away at something
  • family → KIN your kin are your relatives

A little one having supper with the relatives.

The surface is an ordinary family meal, which is why 'to eat' reads as the reason for the gathering rather than as a chunk of the answer.

26 across ENDIVE

Salad plant: I have the last bit first (6)

Definition: Salad plant · charade

Endive is the curly-leaved chicory relative sold for salads, so 'salad plant' names it by the class it belongs to.

  • the last bit → END the last bit of anything is its end
  • I have → IVE I have contracts to I've in speech
  • first — 'First' says which chunk leads, so the piece named last in the sentence is written at the front of the answer.

Somebody bagging the final piece before anybody else can.

The colon does the joining, and 'the last bit' is not an instruction to take a final letter — it is a synonym, wanted whole.

27 across AIM

I am moving round train (3)

Definition: train · anagram

To train a gun or a camera on something is to aim it there, so 'train' is the verb in its pointing sense.

  • I am → AIM those three letters shifted into another order
  • moving round — Something moving round is shifting position, so the letters it applies to do not stay where the clue put them.

Somebody walking round a train.

'Round' looks like a container instruction, wrapping one piece about another, and there is nothing here to wrap: it belongs to the phrase telling the letters to move.

28 across SUPERIOR

No ordinary lake? (8)

Definition: No ordinary · double definition

Superior means a cut above the ordinary run of things, and Lake Superior is the largest of the Great Lakes, so 'lake' defines it by example.

  • No ordinary → the adjective for whatever is better than the usual sort
  • lake → the biggest of the five Great Lakes carries this word as its name

A lake well out of the common run.

The question mark carries both halves: it flags the definition by example, and it licenses 'no ordinary' as a way of saying the adjective.

29 across PLAINT

Obvious time for old lament (6)

Definition: old lament · charade

A plaint is an archaic word for a lament or complaint, which is why the definition flags it as old.

  • Obvious → PLAIN something obvious is plain to see
  • time → T standard abbreviation

An obvious moment for an old grievance to be aired.

'Old' is not decoration: it is the setter warning you that the word wanted has fallen out of everyday use and survives mainly in law and poetry.

30 across DENTIST

She leads a hand-to-mouth existence (7)

Definition: She leads a hand-to-mouth existence · cryptic definition

Her hands really do go to people's mouths all day, so the idiom for scraping by describes the working day rather than the money.

  • She leads a hand-to-mouth existence → the surface picture is a woman living on whatever comes in each day, with nothing put by
  • hand-to-mouth → taken word by word instead, it describes a job done with the hands inside other people's mouths

A woman who can barely make ends meet.

'Hand-to-mouth' means precarious poverty everywhere else it is used, and there is nothing to separate out into wordplay — the surgery is the second reading.

31 across CREEPER

Ivy for one in a soft shoe (7)

Definition: Ivy for one · double definition

Ivy is one example of a plant that climbs by clinging, and a creeper is also the crepe-soled shoe of 1950s British youth fashion.

  • Ivy for one → 'for one' flags an example: one plant of the climbing kind, not the whole class
  • a soft shoe → the thick crepe-soled shoe of 1950s British youth fashion, quiet to walk in

A sprig of ivy tucked inside a soft shoe.

The link is the quiet walk: a plant that creeps up a wall and a shoe you can creep about in take their name from the same verb.

Jack Reacher is the ex-military drifter at the centre of Lee Child's thrillers, so 'Child hero' names him by his author.

  • treachery → REACHER seven consecutive letters sitting inside this one word, beginning one letter in

A young hero caught up in an act of betrayal.

3 down COMMON ERA

More usual answer for these days (6,3)

Definition: these days · charade

The Common Era is the religion-neutral name for the span of years dated AD, which is the age we are living through now.

  • More usual → COMMONER the comparative form: something more usual than another thing is commoner
  • answer → A standard abbreviation

The reply people tend to give nowadays.

CE dates and AD dates are the same dates, the first being the wording that leaves the church out of it. The surface wants 'commoner' to be a person without a title.

4 down RANGER

Park-keeper’s great annoyance following run (6)

Definition: Park-keeper · charade

A ranger is the warden paid to patrol and look after a park or forest, so 'park-keeper' is the job description.

  • run → R standard abbreviation, as on a cricket scorecard
  • great annoyance → ANGER annoyance turned up to its strongest
  • following — What follows something comes along behind it, so the longer chunk is written after the single letter rather than before it.

A park-keeper's fury at somebody who went running through his flowerbeds.

'Park-keeper's' reads as a possessive belonging to the surface story, and the apostrophe-s is standing in for 'is' — the equals sign between definition and wordplay.

6 down AFTER ALL

Coming in last in spite of everything? (5,3)

Definition: Coming in last · double definition

Finish behind every other runner and you come in after all of them, while the same two words idiomatically mean despite everything that pointed the other way.

  • Coming in last → read literally: the runner who crosses the line behind every one of the others
  • in spite of everything → the idiom you tack on when things turn out contrary to expectation

A straggler trailing home behind the field despite everything.

The question mark flags the literal half, where the phrase is taken apart word by word instead of being heard as the idiom everybody uses without thinking about it.

7 down LORIS

Primate leads off responses in services from the front (5)

Definition: Primate · first letters

A loris is a slow-moving, big-eyed tree-dweller of southern Asia, so 'primate' names it by the order of animals it belongs to.

  • leads off responses in services → LORIS the opening letter of each of these five words, in the order they stand
  • from the front — Taking something from the front of a word means its opening letter, and the instruction is applied to every word in the run.

An archbishop starting the responses off at the beginning of a service.

'Primate' is a senior churchman on the surface, and the zoological sense is the one that pays. 'Leads off' looks like the instruction and is really one of the five words donating a letter.

8 down CAT'S EYE

How Tom sees road safety feature (4,3)

Definition: road safety feature · charade

A cat's eye is one of the reflective studs sunk into a British road that throw the headlights back at you and mark the lanes after dark.

  • Tom → CATS a tom is a male cat, and what belongs to him takes the possessive
  • sees → EYE the organ you see with

A man called Tom giving his opinion of some road safety measure.

'Tom' looks like a man's name, capital and all, and it is the word for a male cat. The road studs were named for what they look like when the headlights catch them.

9 down REVOLUTIONARY

Lenin for one turning in his grave? (13)

Definition: Lenin for one · double definition

Lenin is one instance of the class the word names, and anything revolutionary revolves — which is precisely what a body turning in its grave is doing.

  • Lenin for one → 'for one' flags an example: the man who led the Bolsheviks stands in for the whole class of people who overthrow governments
  • turning in his grave → the second reading takes the word back to its root, a thing that turns round and round

Lenin spinning in his tomb at what has become of his politics.

'Turning in his grave' is the stock phrase for a dead man appalled by the living, and the setter is asking you to picture the turning itself as well.

17 down FIELDFARE

Bird fled a fire, flying off (9)

Definition: Bird · anagram

The fieldfare is a large thrush that crosses to Britain from Scandinavia for the winter, so 'bird' names its class.

  • fled a fire → FIELDFARE those nine letters resettled in a different order
  • flying off — Things that fly off scatter in all directions, and the letters this is attached to scatter into a fresh order.

A bird escaping a blaze and vanishing into the sky.

'Flying off' reads as the bird's escape rather than as an order, which is the whole disguise. Nothing in the sentence is spare — even 'a' donates a letter.

18 down WEEKLIES

Definition: magazines · charade + homophone

Weeklies are the magazines that come out once every seven days, so the plural names them by their publication schedule.

  • Feeble → WEEK feeble means weak, and said aloud that noise is written as the seven-day span instead
  • stories → LIES stories in the sense a parent means when a child is telling them
  • sounding — A thing described by how it sounds is handed to you by ear, so what you hear is spelt another way on the page.

Weak-sounding tales printed in the press.

'Stories' does double duty on the surface, where it means magazine articles, while the wordplay wants the nursery sense children get told off for.

23 down TEMPER

Moderate rage (6)

Definition: Moderate · double definition

To temper a thing is to moderate it, as steel is tempered, and a temper is the rage itself — the verb softens, the noun boils over.

  • Moderate → the verb: to soften something, to take the extremes off it
  • rage → the noun for the state somebody is in when they lose it completely

Anger of the middling sort.

One sense calms a thing down and the other is the thing boiling over, which is why two words sit together and read as a single description of mild fury.

25 down CHEAT

To cheat death, or the gallows, is to escape something that should have caught you, and that is the sense 'luckily avoid' is after.

Somebody lucky enough to escape both the freezing weather and the sweltering kind.

'Both … and' is the setter promising two pieces in plain sight, and the definition is the idiom in 'cheat death' rather than the card-table sense.
