Across

9 across OPERA

Car men possibly needing welding ring AA (5)

Definition: Car men · charade

Car men → OPERA: run together it is Bizet's Carmen, and 'possibly' flags that one named work is standing in for the art form.

  • ring → O a ring is a circle, and a circle is the shape of that letter
  • AA → PERA the first of the two letters is read as the word a, which means per — five pounds a head is five pounds per head — while the second stays as it is
  • possibly — 'Possibly' warns that what precedes is one instance of the answer's category rather than a synonym for the whole of it.
  • welding — Welding fuses separate pieces into a single object, so the chunks are run together in the order given.

Mechanics with a welding job on their hands calling out the motoring breakdown service.

Bizet's Carmen is both the trap and the point: two words on the page, one work in the ear. The AA is a breakdown service only in the surface story.

10 across ULTRASOFT

Hardcore fans frequently very forgiving? (9)

Definition: very forgiving · charade

very forgiving → ULTRASOFT: 'soft' in the sense of lenient, as in soft on offenders, with 'ultra' doing the work of 'very'.

  • Hardcore fans → ULTRAS ultras are football's most fervent supporters, the ones who organise the chanting
  • frequently → OFT oft is the poetic short form of often

Football's most fanatical supporters turning out to be remarkably easy-going.

The definition is the last two words, and they go on sounding like more talk about football supporters, so the sentence never seems to change subject. An S changes sides between the two readings.

11 across PATTY-CAKE

Misuse of tacky tape in Yankees’ game (5-4)

Definition: Yankees’ game · anagram

Yankees’ game → PATTY-CAKE: patty-cake is the American name for the children's clapping game, so 'Yankees'' marks which side of the Atlantic the name comes from.

  • tacky tape → TACKYTAPE anagram fodder, nine letters
  • Misuse of → anagram indicator
  • Misuse of — Misusing something is handling it wrongly, and letters handled wrongly come out in the wrong order.

Sticky tape being put to a use it was never meant for at a baseball game.

The Yankees are Americans in general rather than the New York club, and the game is the hand-clapping nursery rhyme that British children chant as pat-a-cake.

12 across TEDDY

Stuffed thing in underwear (5)

Definition: Stuffed thing · double definition

A teddy is both the plush bear named after Theodore Roosevelt and a woman's one-piece camisole-and-knickers undergarment.

  • Stuffed thing → TEDDY the soft bear a child takes to bed, named after President Roosevelt
  • underwear → TEDDY the one-piece camisole-and-knickers undergarment

Something padded turning up in somebody's underwear drawer.

One word covers a nursery toy and a piece of lingerie, and the clue runs the two senses into a single phrase so that the join between them disappears.

13 across TOBACCO

Explorer brought back oddly cool plant (7)

Definition: plant · charade + reversal + alternate letters

plant → TOBACCO: the broad-leaved crop grown for its dried and cured leaves is a plant in the plain botanical sense.

  • Explorer → TOBAC John Cabot, the navigator who sailed west from Bristol, written backwards
  • oddly cool → CO the first and third letters of CoOl
  • brought back — Bringing a word back turns it round, so in an across entry its letters are written from right to left.
  • oddly — Reading a word oddly keeps the letters in the odd positions and throws the even ones away.

An explorer returning home with an oddly chilly plant.

Cabot claimed Newfoundland for Henry VII in 1497; the leaf that actually crossed the Atlantic came back with later voyagers, which is the quiet joke in the surface.

15 across TANGRAM

Dynasty’s leading male produces puzzle (7)

Definition: puzzle · charade

puzzle → TANGRAM: the Chinese dissection puzzle whose seven flat pieces are rearranged into silhouettes is one particular kind of puzzle.

  • Dynasty’s → TANG the Tang ruled China for three centuries
  • male → RAM a ram is the male sheep
  • leading — Whatever leads goes in front, so the dynasty's letters are placed ahead of the ones that follow.

The leading man of the glossy American soap leaving everybody baffled.

A Chinese dynasty and an eighties soap share a word, and the capital D belongs equally to both, so nothing in the sentence points east.

17 across LUXOR

1-0 by the end for Manchester City (5)

Definition: City · charade + last letter

City → LUXOR: the Egyptian city on the Nile, built over the ruins of ancient Thebes, is a city in the plainest sense.

  • 1 → LUX the answer to 1-down is SOAP, and Lux is the long-running brand of it
  • 0 → O a written nought is the letter it looks like
  • the end for Manchester → R the last letter of ManchesteR
  • the end for — The end of a name is its final letter, so only the last character of the town is used.

A one-nil scoreline going Manchester City's way.

Manchester City is a football club until the two words come apart: 'City' is the definition and Manchester is being asked only for its final letter.

18 across SUD

Comeback of Twilight mostly a bit of froth (3)

Definition: a bit of froth · reversal + deletion

a bit of froth → SUD: the singular of suds, one bubble of the soapy foam that a wash raises.

  • Twilight mostly → DUS twilight is dusk, and 'mostly' leaves the closing K behind
  • Comeback of — A comeback is a return along the way you came, so the letters are read back to front.
  • mostly — Mostly means most of a thing and not all of it, so the word gives up its final letter.

The vampire film franchise making a return and being written off as lightweight.

In italics Twilight is the film series; in plain English it is dusk, and that is the reading the wordplay needs. Setters treat the singular of suds as a fair word.

20 across EVENS

Sinner’s broken by Norrie at the start and quits (5)

Definition: quits · container + first letter

quits → EVENS: to be quits is to owe nothing either way, and evens is that same level state, the bookmaker's word for a 50-50 chance.

  • Sinner’s → EVES Eve was the one who took the fruit in Genesis, and her apostrophe-s comes along with her
  • Norrie → N the first letter of Norrie
  • broken by — Breaking into something opens it in the middle, so the letter is pushed between the ones already there.
  • at the start — The start of a name is the letter it opens with, so only that first character is taken.

A tennis player broken early on by Norrie and then walking off the court.

Jannik Sinner and Cameron Norrie are both on the men's tour, which is what keeps the capital S looking like a surname instead of the woman in Eden.

22 across Y-FRONTS

Father working in old juvenile training pants (1-6)

Definition: pants · container + charade

pants → Y-FRONTS: pants is the British word for underpants, and Y-fronts are the men's briefs with the Y-shaped seam at the front.

  • old juvenile training → YTS the Youth Training Scheme, the British programme that took school-leavers through the 1980s and was wound up in 1990
  • Father → FR Fr is how a priest's title is written in front of his name
  • working → ON a machine that is working is on
  • in — 'In' puts one thing inside another, so the letters before it are dropped into the middle of what follows.

A priest employed on a defunct government scheme for training young people.

A whole institution is compressed into three letters here, and 'old' is the setter's way of saying the scheme no longer exists rather than a word about age.

25 across WASHDAY

Spooner’s cash balance when things may be on the line? (7)

Definition: when things may be on the line · spoonerism

when things may be on the line → WASHDAY: the day the household wash is done, when the laundry is literally pegged out on a line.

  • cash → WASH dosh is British slang for money; it hands over its opening D and takes the W coming back the other way
  • balance → DAY to weigh a thing is to balance it; it gives up its W and collects the D that has just been let go
  • Spooner’s — Spooner was the Oxford don famous for swapping the opening sounds of two words, so those two openings change places.

Spooner totting up how much money he has left.

Say 'dosh' and 'weigh' out loud, trade the noises at their fronts, and the laundry comes out — the second half has to be written as the ordinary spelling of the sound.

26 across JOUST

Nothing stops fair contest (5)

Definition: contest · container

contest → JOUST: the lance-to-lance charge between two mounted knights is a contest, and the word still covers any head-to-head duel.

  • fair → JUST a just decision is a fair one
  • Nothing → O nothing is written as a nought, which is that letter's shape
  • stops — To stop a gap is to plug it, so the letter is pushed into the middle of the word beside it.

Nothing standing in the way of a fair fight.

'Nothing stops fair' reads as a single phrase about there being no obstacle, and all three words are wordplay. The contest is the one knights fought with lances.

27 across DISBARRED

Expelled inspectors embarrassed outside pub (9)

Definition: Expelled · container

Expelled → DISBARRED: a barrister struck off is expelled from the bar, so the general word covers this one particular expulsion.

  • inspectors → DIS DI is the police rank detective inspector, and the clue asks for more than one
  • pub → BAR the bar is the counter a pub serves its drinks over
  • embarrassed → RED an embarrassed face goes red
  • outside — What stands outside goes round the outside, so the pub is enclosed by the two pieces on either side of it.

Detectives going red in the face outside a pub.

The pub in the middle is the same bar a barrister is called to, which is why being stripped of it amounts to expulsion from the profession.

30 across EMBROILED

Dorothy’s aunt and British drunk get involved (9)

Definition: involved · charade

involved → EMBROILED: to be embroiled is to be caught up in a quarrel or a mess, the sense of 'involved' that carries trouble with it.

  • Dorothy’s aunt → EM Auntie Em brought Dorothy up on the Kansas farm in The Wizard of Oz
  • British → BR Br is the standard short form of British
  • drunk → OILED someone well oiled has had far too much to drink

Dorothy's aunt getting mixed up with a drunken Brit.

Well-oiled is British slang for thoroughly drunk, and Auntie Em is Dorothy's guardian on the Kansas farm; the clue introduces two people who have never met.

31 across TIDES

Main movements of Mendelssohn’s 4th introduced by strings (5)

Definition: Main movements · container + fourth letter

Main movements → TIDES: 'main' is the poetic name for the open sea, and the tides are the regular movements of it.

  • strings → TIES ties are strings for fastening, as on an apron
  • Mendelssohn’s 4th → D the fourth letter of menDelssohn
  • 4th — The 4th of a name is the letter standing in fourth place when you count from the front of it.
  • introduced by — To introduce something is to bring it inside, so the strings take the letter into the middle of themselves.

The principal movements of Mendelssohn's Fourth Symphony, opened by the string section.

'Main' is the old word for the open sea, as in the Spanish Main, so the movements are the sea's. Mendelssohn's Fourth is the Italian Symphony, which keeps you in the concert hall.

1 down SOAP

Block essentially tackled by drain cleaner (4)

Definition: cleaner · container + middle letter

cleaner → SOAP: the plainest sense of the word, the household agent that lathers up and takes dirt off.

  • drain → SAP to sap something is to drain it of strength, and the word opens up to take a letter
  • Block → O the letter at the centre of blOck
  • essentially — The essence of a word sits at its very core, so a five-letter word is asked for its single middle letter.
  • tackled by — A tackle closes right round the player being tackled, so whatever does the tackling wraps itself about the letter.

Something blocking a drain, and the stuff poured down the pipe to shift it.

'Block' looks like the thing stuck in the pipe and is really a word being cut down to one letter, while 'drain' is a verb rather than the pipe itself.

2 down BENTO BOX

Packed lunch from dodgy old Spar (5,3)

Definition: Packed lunch · charade

Packed lunch → BENTO BOX: the Japanese compartmented lunch container, rice and fish packed into its trays, is a packed lunch exactly.

  • dodgy → BENT someone bent is crooked or dishonest, as in a bent copper
  • old → O o is the standard short form of old
  • Spar → BOX to spar is to box, trading practice punches

A dubious lunch bought from a corner shop that has seen better days.

Spar is a chain of British convenience stores, which is what the capital letter is there to suggest; the word is really doing service as the boxers' verb.

3 down BABY

Honey bee swallowed by horse (4)

Definition: Honey · container

Honey → BABY: both are what you call someone you dote on, interchangeable as forms of address between fond adults.

  • horse → BAY a bay is a reddish-brown horse
  • bee → B the insect is the name of the letter said aloud, so it stands for that single character
  • swallowed by — Anything swallowed disappears inside the swallower, so the letter ends up in the middle of the word that eats it.

A bee getting itself swallowed by a grazing horse.

'Honey' is a term of endearment rather than the stuff bees make, and putting the insect right next to it keeps the hive in mind.

4 down CURATORS

Aussie groundsmen stuck up schedule to feed dogs (8)

Definition: Aussie groundsmen · container + reversal

Aussie groundsmen → CURATORS: in Australia the staff who prepare a cricket ground carry the job title curator, not groundsman.

  • dogs → CURS a cur is a mongrel, the crossword's usual disparaging dog
  • schedule → ATOR a rota is a duty schedule, and it climbs the entry instead of descending it
  • stuck up — Up a down entry means written from the bottom towards the top, so the schedule is entered the wrong way round.
  • to feed — You feed something into a machine by pushing it inside, so these letters go into the middle of the other word.

Australian ground staff pinning up a feeding rota for the dogs.

In Australia the person who prepares and tends a cricket square is called the curator, and that job title is the whole definition rather than an art-gallery joke.

5 down STREET

Where you might see Neighbours actress not finishing on time (6)

Definition: Where you might see Neighbours · deletion + charade

Where you might see Neighbours → STREET: the soap is set in one, and a street is literally where the people next door live.

  • actress not finishing → STREE Meryl Streep is the actress, halted before her closing letter
  • time → T t is the standard short form of time
  • not finishing — A word that does not finish stops short of its own last character, so the final letter never gets written.

An actress from the Australian soap turning up somewhere, and not quite finishing on time.

The italics make Neighbours a programme, and the show is set in Ramsay Street; with a small n the same word means the people living either side of you.

6 down EASTENDERS

1 tip to stop festival succeeded (10)

Definition: 1 · container + charade

1 → EASTENDERS: the answer at 1-down is the word for a television serial, and this long-running BBC one is an instance of it.

  • festival → EASTER Easter is the festival, and it splits open to take another piece
  • tip → END the tip of a thing is its end
  • succeeded → S s is the genealogist's short form of succeeded
  • to stop — To stop a gap is to plug it, so the piece named goes down into the middle of the word beside it.

A gratuity offered to bring a festival to a halt, and the bribe working.

The bare '1' sends you to 1-down, whose answer names a whole television genre, so the definition here is an example of that genre rather than a synonym for anything.

7 down POWDER

Snow leopard finally cutting teeth (6)

Definition: Snow · container + last letter

Snow → POWDER: on the piste, powder is the fresh dry unpacked snow that skiers go looking for.

  • teeth → POWER a law with teeth is a law with power behind it
  • leopard finally → D the last letter of leoparD
  • finally — The letter a word finally arrives at is its last, so only that closing character is taken.
  • cutting — Cutting into something opens it up, so the letter is driven between the others rather than added on the end.

A snow leopard cutting its teeth.

'Snow leopard' is a single animal until you split it: the first word defines and the second is only there to hand over one letter.

8 down STAY

Supporter beginning to sing Flower of Scotland (4)

Definition: Supporter · charade + first letter

Supporter → STAY: a stay is the rope or brace that holds a mast up, and stays are the stiffening in a corset.

  • sing → S the opening letter of sing
  • Flower of Scotland → TAY the Tay is Scotland's longest river, and a river is a thing that flows
  • beginning to — The beginning of a word is the letter it opens with, so only that first character comes across.

A supporter in the crowd striking up the Scottish anthem.

'Flower' is the crossword's oldest pun — not a bloom but a flow-er, a thing that flows — and the italics on the anthem's title keep you singing rather than looking at rivers.

13 down TELLY

Giveaway on the back of Monopoly set (5)

Definition: set · charade + last letter

set → TELLY: a television receiver is 'the set', and telly is the everyday British word for that same box.

  • Giveaway → TELL a tell is the unconscious sign that gives a poker player away
  • Monopoly → Y the last letter of monopolY
  • on the back of — The back of a word is where its final letter sits, so that closing character is all that comes across.

A free gift advertised on the back of a Monopoly box.

'Set' sits at the end looking like part of the board-game picture, a Monopoly set, and it is the definition: the box in the corner of the living room.

14 down CORONATION

Businessmen making a return on people’s investment (10)

Definition: investment · charade + reversal

investment → CORONATION: the older sense of invest, to clothe someone in the robes of office, makes the crowning ceremony an investment.

  • Businessmen → CO the word has to be split in two: business gives a company, co for short
  • men → RO the second half of that same word is OR, the army's other ranks, and those two letters come back the other way
  • people’s → NATION a nation is a people considered as a single body
  • making a return — Something making a return comes back the way it went, so those letters are written in the opposite order.

City investors collecting a healthy return on money the public put in.

'Investment' is not the financial kind: to invest someone is to install them in office with the robes and regalia. The single word 'Businessmen' has to be read as two.

16 down MESSY

Confused footballer on the radio (5)

Definition: Confused · homophone

Confused → MESSY: a messy situation is a muddled and disordered one, so 'confused' names the state of things rather than a state of mind.

  • footballer → MESSY Lionel Messi, the Argentinian forward, said aloud
  • on the radio — A name heard on the radio reaches you as sound alone, so you write down what it sounds like rather than how it is spelt.

A confused footballer being discussed by commentators on the radio.

Only the spelling moves: the Argentinian's name ends in an I where the adjective ends in a Y, so to the ear the two are identical.

19 down DOWNSIDE

Negative director upset Sid Owen (8)

Definition: Negative · anagram

Negative → DOWNSIDE: the downside of a plan is its negative, the drawback you weigh against the advantages.

  • director → D d is the credits' short form of director
  • Sid Owen → SIDOWEN anagram fodder, seven letters
  • upset → anagram indicator
  • upset — Upsetting something tips its contents out in a heap, and letters tipped out land in a fresh order.

A film director losing his temper with the actor Sid Owen.

Sid Owen played Ricky Butcher in EastEnders for two decades, which is why his name turns up in a soap-themed grid, and the clue reads as a row on set.

21 down ELDORADO

Jackie Corkhill finishes fish in fabulous place (8)

Definition: fabulous place · charade + last letters

fabulous place → ELDORADO: the legendary city of gold exists only in fable, which is what 'fabulous' means before it means wonderful.

  • Jackie Corkhill finishes → EL the closing letters of jackiE and corkhilL; she was a regular in Brookside, the Liverpool soap
  • fish → DORADO the dorado is the dolphinfish of warm seas
  • finishes — Where a name finishes is its last letter, and two names finishing give two closing letters in order.

Jackie Corkhill polishing off the last of the fish in a marvellous spot.

El Dorado was the golden city the conquistadors hunted through South America and never found. It was also a short-lived BBC soap, which is the company this clue's character keeps.

23 down RHUMBA

Hamburg’s endless winding steps (6)

Definition: steps · anagram + deletion

steps → RHUMBA: the Cuban ballroom dance, all hip movement and little travel, is named here by the steps a dancer performs.

  • Hamburg’s endless → HAMBUR the German port with its final letter dropped, six letters of fodder
  • winding → anagram indicator
  • endless — A word made endless loses the letter at its end, so the last one goes before anything else happens.
  • winding — A winding road never runs straight, and letters made to wind arrive out of the order they were given in.

An endless spiral staircase somewhere in Hamburg.

'Winding steps' reads as one phrase, a spiral staircase, and the two words belong to different halves of the clue: one shuffles the letters, the other defines them.

24 down SADDLE

Seat deals flying with Delta (6)

Definition: Seat · anagram

Seat → SADDLE: the seat a rider sits on, whether strapped to a horse's back or bolted to a bicycle frame.

  • deals → DEALS anagram fodder, five letters
  • Delta → D Delta is D in the radio alphabet
  • flying → anagram indicator
  • flying — Things sent flying scatter and land anyhow, which is what becomes of these letters.

Cheap airline seats going on sale with Delta.

Delta is an airline in the surface and a single letter in the wordplay, and that swap is what the clue turns on; the seat is a horse's or a bicycle's.

26 down JOEY

Young Australian’s delight catching English opener (4)

Definition: Young Australian · container + first letter

Young Australian → JOEY: the young of a kangaroo or other marsupial, Australian by species rather than by nationality.

  • delight → JOY joy is delight, and it opens up to take a letter
  • English → E the first letter of English
  • catching — Catching something holds it inside your grasp, so the letter is trapped in the middle of the word.
  • opener — The opener of a word is the letter that opens it, so only that first character is used.

An Australian fielder's glee at catching England's opening batsman.

Every word of the cricket picture is complete — a catch, an opener, an Australian celebrating — and the answer is a kangaroo's young.

28 down ACTS

Defaced information book (4)

Definition: book · deletion

book → ACTS: the Acts of the Apostles is one of the books of the New Testament, named here by its category.

  • information → FACTS facts are information, and this is the word about to lose its face
  • Defaced — Take the face off a word and its front letter goes, which is the reading 'de-faced' is asking for.

A reference book that somebody has scribbled all over.

Acts of the Apostles is a New Testament book, so 'book' defines by example. 'Defaced' works twice over: a vandalised volume in the surface, a beheaded word in the wordplay.

29 down DISH

Prepared food for heart-throb (4)

Definition: Prepared food · double definition

A dish is both a prepared course served at table and, in slang, a person good-looking enough to stop traffic.

  • Prepared food → DISH a course as it reaches the table, cooked and plated
  • heart-throb → DISH slang for a strikingly good-looking person

Somebody cooking a meal for the object of their affections.

Food on the table and food for the eye: the same word covers a plated course and a person worth staring at, and 'for' pretends to be a preposition of purpose.
