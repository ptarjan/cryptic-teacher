Observer Everyman Crossword No 4,132

Across

1 across SCRUMMIEST

Seconds: extremely cheap and most delicious (10)

Definition: most delicious · charade

most delicious: the superlative of 'scrummy', a nursery shortening of scrumptious, so it names food at the very top of tasty.

  • Seconds → S S is the stopwatch abbreviation for seconds
  • extremely cheap → CRUMMIEST crummy means cheap and shoddy, and pushed to its extreme the adjective goes into its superlative form

The colon invites you to read the first word as a second helping, which the definition at the far end is only too happy to encourage. It is a stopwatch abbreviation.

6 across OPUS

Making comeback, builds upon incomplete work (4)

Definition: work · hidden word + reversal

work: a composer's numbered piece is called exactly that, which is why concert programmes print Op. in front of its number.

  • builds upon → OPUS four letters straddle the gap between the two words, and they are read from right to left
  • Making comeback — Something making a comeback returns the way it went, so this run of letters is read against the direction the entry travels.
  • incomplete — The words on either side are not used whole: each gives up only the end or the start of itself to the run.

'work' looks like part of the surface picture — an unfinished job — and a solver who reads it that way has the right words in the wrong roles.

9 across STEM GINGER

Repress redhead that's spicy in the kitchen (4,6)

Definition: spicy in the kitchen · charade

The definition describes rather than substitutes: the preserved root sold in syrup is a baking-cupboard staple with a hot, peppery bite.

  • Repress → STEM to stem a flow or a tide is to hold it back
  • redhead → GINGER British slang for a person with red hair, from the colour of the root

Both halves are ordinary words in kitchen clothes: a verb about holding something back and a nickname for hair colour, edible only once they are pushed together.

10 across URSA

Guard's van regularly out of service: bear up? (4)

Definition: bear · alternate letters

bear: the Latin word for the animal, familiar in English from the two constellations, so the clue defines by translation rather than by synonym.

  • Guard's van → URSA every second letter of the two words, read straight through
  • out of service → surface only: a guard's van is the railway carriage at the tail of a British train, and one out of service completes the picture
  • up → surface only: 'bear up' reads as an encouragement to keep going, but the definition stops at the animal
  • regularly — Letters taken regularly come at a fixed interval, here every second one, counting from the second letter.

The bear is the one in the sky. Ursa Major and Ursa Minor are the Latin names of the two constellations, so the definition asks for a Latin animal, not an English one.

11 across OLD WIVES' TALE

Divorcées getting dog, we're told: it may not be true (3,5,4)

Definition: it may not be true · charade + homophone

it may not be true: the phrase names a piece of traditional lore passed on as fact, and the whole force of the label is that it is unreliable.

  • Divorcées → OLDWIVES a divorcée is a former wife, and former wives are old ones — two words the wordplay uses whole
  • dog → TALE the part of a dog that wags; spoken, it is indistinguishable from the word that gets written down
  • we're told — What we are told arrives as sound, so one piece has to be written down as a different word that happens to sound the same.

The first word does the heavy lifting: former wives are old wives, an idiom rather than an insult. The colon is all that separates wordplay from definition.

15 across READMIT

Let back in to study at Cambridge University (7)

Definition: Let back in · charade

Let back in: to admit someone a second time is to let them in again, the prefix carrying the 'back' of the definition.

  • study → READ at a British university you read a subject rather than major in it
  • at Cambridge University → MIT the Massachusetts Institute of Technology stands in Cambridge, across the river from Boston

The trap is the other Cambridge. The surface points firmly at the English university town, and the institute the setter means is the one in Massachusetts.

16 across SCOURGE

Anathema: jerk takes over company (7)

Definition: Anathema · container

Anathema: something loathed and cursed, and the answer names a plague or affliction that blights whatever it touches, so both nouns name a thing wished away.

  • jerk → SURGE a jerk and a surge are both a sudden movement forward
  • company → CO CO is the abbreviation printed after a firm's name
  • takes over — One firm taking over another swallows it whole, so the shorter piece ends up inside the longer rather than after it.

The colon reads as a definition mark, and it is one. 'takes over' looks like a business manoeuvre, which is exactly the surface the setter is paying for.

17 across DURANGO

Led government that's welcomed by pair in Mexican city (7)

Definition: Mexican city · container + charade

Mexican city: the answer is the capital of the Mexican state of the same name, so the definition is plain geography with no trickery in it.

  • Led → RAN to run a company is to lead it, and the tense matches
  • government → G G is the one-letter abbreviation for government
  • pair → DUO a duo is two of anything, most often two performers
  • welcomed by — A host who welcomes guests takes them indoors, so the pair opens up and receives the other letters between its own.

Durango is a city in northern Mexico and the state around it. 'Led' wants to begin a sentence about a leader; it is a three-letter past tense doing wordplay.

19 across AMNESIA

Forgetting a name is awkward (7)

Definition: Forgetting · anagram

Forgetting: the condition is loss of memory, so the -ing noun naming the act of losing it swaps in without strain.

  • a name is → AMNESIA seven letters spread across three small words, dealt out again
  • awkward — An awkward thing will not sit where you put it, so the letters offered are not in the position they end up in.

The definition sits at the front and the fodder is the three small words behind it, which is what makes this hard to see: small words are easy to skip when you are hunting letters.

20 across NEW HAMPSHIRE

Ashen, whimper pathetically somewhere in the USA (3,9)

Definition: somewhere in the USA · anagram

somewhere in the USA: the answer is one of the six New England states, and such a vague definition is only fair because the wordplay is exact.

  • Ashen, whimper → NEWHAMPSHIRE twelve letters across the two words, dealt again into two new ones
  • pathetically — To do a thing pathetically is to do it badly, and letters handled badly finish out of their proper order.

The instruction looks as though it belongs to the whimpering, which is the point of putting it there, and the comma between the two words is not a real pause.

23 across IPOD

Sample from Antipodean music held here (4)

Definition: music held here · hidden word

music held here: the pocket player stores a library of songs, so the phrase points at the object by the job it does rather than by a synonym.

  • Antipodean → IPOD four consecutive letters sit inside the long word, beginning at its second letter
  • Sample from — A sample is a small piece cut out of something bigger, so a run of letters is lifted straight out of the long word that follows.

The capital letter and the Australian angle are the misdirection, and the word is long enough that the four letters buried inside it go unnoticed.

24 across RETROSPECT

Reports, etc. edited in contemplation of the past (10)

Definition: contemplation of the past · anagram

contemplation of the past: the noun names exactly the act of looking back over events once they are already over.

  • Reports, etc. → RETROSPECT ten letters, the abbreviation counted in, dealt out again
  • edited — An editor cuts and moves whatever they are handed, so the text does not come out in the order it went in.

'etc.' is not a lazy 'and so on' here: its three letters are part of the fodder, which is the small trick the full stop is hiding.

25 across LATE

And others sent back behind (4)

Definition: behind · reversal

behind: in the sense of behind schedule rather than behind in space, which is how a train or a payment earns the word.

  • And others → ETAL et al is the Latin tag a bibliography uses for the authors it cannot be bothered to list
  • sent back — Something sent back travels the way it came, so the four letters are read from the far end towards the near one.

The definition means behind time, not behind in space: a runner behind the field is the wrong picture, a train behind schedule the right one.

26 across OSTENSIBLE

So-called noblesse - it's dubious (10)

Definition: So-called · anagram

So-called: said to be such but not necessarily so, and the adjective marks a reason or a title that is only apparent — the same doubt exactly.

  • noblesse - it → OSTENSIBLE ten letters, the italicised French word plus the little pronoun beside it, dealt again
  • dubious — What is dubious cannot be trusted to be what it looks like, and letters that cannot be trusted are not in the order given.

The italics flag the French word as a quotation, which is the setter dressing the surface; its letters are simply raw material, and the dash is a red herring.

Down

1 down SUSS

Primarily solve / understand (somewhat slangily!) (4)

Definition: Primarily solve / understand (somewhat slangily!) · &lit + first letters

The whole clue reads straight: to work something out or grasp it, in casual British speech, is precisely what this verb means.

  • solve / understand (somewhat slangily!) → SUSS the starting letters of the four words, taken in the order they stand
  • Primarily — To do something primarily is to do it first of all, so each word after it yields only the letter it begins with.

An all-in-one: the very words that hand over their opening letters also describe what the answer means, and the exclamation mark is the setter flagging the informal register.

2 down RUED

Regretted being audibly crass (4)

Definition: Regretted · homophone

Regretted: to rue something is to look back on it with sorrow, a verb that survives mainly in the phrase 'rue the day'.

  • crass → RUED a crass remark is a rude one, and out loud the two spellings are impossible to tell apart
  • audibly — What is audible reaches you through the ear, so the word beside it is to be taken by its sound and then written down another way.

Past tense on both sides: the definition is in the past, so what you hear has to be written down as a past tense too, which is what makes the odd spelling findable.

3 down MEGALOMANIA

Everyman broadcast main goal: modicum of admiration (a delusion) (11)

Definition: a delusion · charade + anagram + first letter

a delusion: the answer names one particular delusion, that of one's own greatness and power, so the clue defines it by the general class.

  • Everyman → ME the setter signs this puzzle Everyman, and a setter pointing at themselves is the commonest convention in the business
  • main goal → GALOMANI eight letters across the two words, resettled into a new order
  • admiration → A the starting letter of the word is all that is taken
  • broadcast — To broadcast seed is to scatter it wide across a field, so the letters land in no order anyone chose.
  • modicum of — A modicum is the smallest amount there is, and the smallest amount of a written word is the single letter at its front.

Everyman naming himself in the clue is standard practice in this series: the byline is the wordplay. The colon is holding the surface together and nothing more.

4 down I KNEW IT

Wine Kit's drunk: that's no shock (1,4,2)

Definition: that's no shock · anagram

that's no shock: the answer is the three-word phrase you blurt when something turns out exactly as expected, so nothing about it surprises you.

  • Wine Kit → IKNEWIT seven letters go in and the same seven come out in a new order
  • drunk — Someone drunk cannot walk a straight line, so the letters handed over will not stay in the line they were given.

The surface is someone tipsy on home-made wine, and the colon hides the join: everything before it is letters to shuffle, everything after is the definition.

5 down SHELVES

Suspends from ledges (7)

Definition: Suspends · double definition

Suspends is the verb, putting a plan on hold; ledges is the plural noun, the boards a wall carries. One word, two parts of speech.

  • Suspends → in the sense of putting a plan on hold, not of hanging something from a hook
  • ledges → the plural noun: flat boards fixed to a wall, named after the rock ledges they imitate

Three words, two definitions and nothing else: 'from' is joinery, and a solver hunting a mechanism inside these seven letters will find none.

7 down PERMAFROST

Performs at resort in which it's always cold (10)

Definition: in which it's always cold · anagram

The definition describes rather than substitutes: the answer is ground that stays frozen right through the summer, so it is never not cold.

  • Performs at → PERMAFROST ten letters across the two words, re-sorted
  • resort — Read as two words, re-sort, it asks for the letters to be sorted a second time into some other arrangement.

The surface offers a chilly holiday town, and buying that picture is the trap: the word in the middle is an instruction, not a place.

8 down SIAMESE CAT

What could be represented as 'eats mice'? (7,3)

Definition: What could be represented as 'eats mice'? · &lit + anagram

The whole clue reads straight: a breed that could indeed be pictured eating mice, the question mark admitting this is one example among many.

  • as 'eats mice' → SIAMESECAT ten letters, the little word before the quotation included, redrawn
  • could be represented — Saying a thing could be shown as something else licenses its letters to be redrawn into a different picture.

An all-in-one: the whole question is the definition, and the same words are the letters. The quotation marks are the flag that a phrase is being handled as raw material.

12 down SPOONERISMS

'Bird watching' is one for these? (11)

Definition: these · spoonerism

'these' points at the category the clue is demonstrating: swapping the front sounds of two words is the verbal slip named after the Oxford don who kept making it.

  • 'Bird watching' → BIRD WATCHING exchange the opening sounds of the two words and a hobby turns into a description of what that exchange does to language

The quotation marks are the signal that a phrase is being handled as a phrase. Say it with its front sounds traded and you get 'word botching', which is the whole joke.

13 down CREDENTIAL

Interlaced characters arranged for an entitlement (10)

Definition: an entitlement · anagram

an entitlement: a document that entitles the bearer to practise or to enter, so the noun names the right the paper confers.

  • Interlaced characters → CREDENTIAL the characters meant are the ten letters of the first word, set out afresh
  • arranged — What has been arranged was put into an order somebody chose, which is not the order it arrived in.

'characters' means letters here, not people, and the word whose letters they are is sitting immediately in front of it. The picture of woven strands is pure decoration.

14 down NARROWBOAT

Note describing quarrel over grain in vessel (10)

Definition: vessel · container + charade

vessel: any craft that carries people on water qualifies, and this one is a canal boat, so the definition is deliberately general.

  • Note → NB nota bene, the Latin tag a writer scribbles in a margin, is the two-letter form
  • quarrel → ARROW a quarrel is the short bolt shot from a crossbow, a piece of medieval vocabulary crosswords keep alive
  • grain → OAT the cereal, singular, three letters
  • describing — To describe a circle is to draw one round something, so these two letters go around the piece that follows rather than beside it.
  • over — In a down entry one thing over another sits above it, so the grain waits underneath until the rest is built.

A narrowboat is the long thin canal boat built to fit Britain's narrow locks. 'Note' is the trap: the musical scale is not wanted here, the Latin margin tag is.

18 down OPPRESS

Act dictatorially towards operation by newspapers (7)

Definition: Act dictatorially towards · charade

Act dictatorially towards: the verb means to rule harshly and keep a people down, which is precisely how a dictator behaves towards those under them.

  • operation → OP OP is the hospital shorthand for an operation
  • newspapers → PRESS the press is the collective name for newspapers and the people who write them

The surface is a medical procedure covered by the papers, and that is where the join hides: the little word between the two halves is joinery and carries no letters.

19 down ASH TREE

A quiet time, breeze regularly blowing in bit of woodland (3,4)

Definition: bit of woodland · charade + alternate letters

bit of woodland: one tree is a piece of what a wood is made of, so the definition names the answer by the larger thing it belongs to.

  • A → A the clue's own letter, taken exactly as it stands
  • quiet → SH the noise you make when asking for silence
  • time → T T is the standard abbreviation for time
  • breeze → REE every second letter of the word, counting from its second
  • regularly — Taken regularly means taken at an even interval, so every second letter of the word is picked up and the rest left behind.

Four pieces for seven letters, and the smallest of them is the bare letter that opens the clue — easy to read as an article and skip straight past.

21 down HERB

Bertie and Rosemary? (4)

Definition: Bertie · double definition

Bertie and the answer are both pet forms of Herbert; rosemary is one example of the kitchen plants the noun covers, which the question mark flags.

  • Bertie → one familiar shortening of a long forename; the four letters wanted are another shortening of that same name
  • Rosemary → a plant grown for flavouring, so this half defines by example — which is what the question mark admits

Two proper names and no wordplay at all: one is a person, one is a plant, and the question mark covers both for being examples rather than equivalents.

22 down STYE

Ocular irritant in some dusty environs (4)

Definition: Ocular irritant · hidden word

Ocular irritant: the small inflamed swelling on an eyelid is exactly that, something at the eye that hurts and will not be ignored.

  • dusty environs → STYE four letters run without a break from inside the first word into the start of the second
  • some — Some of a thing is a portion of it, and a portion of a phrase means a run of its letters rather than any part of its meaning.

The definition works hard to look like the surface's subject — grit blown into the eye — while the letters sit in plain view across the final two words.
