Observer Everyman Crossword No 4,096

Across

1 across ARMED TO THE TEETH

With choppers battle-ready? (5,2,3,5)

Definition: With choppers · double definition

Choppers are teeth in slang and blades in earnest, so the phrase means loaded with weapons — battle-ready — while its own words stop at the molars.

  • With choppers → ARMEDTOTHETEETH choppers are blades — cleavers, machetes — so a man carrying them is armed
  • battle-ready? → ARMEDTOTHETEETH the idiom for carrying every weapon you can hold, kitted out for a fight

Choppers are teeth in slang and heavy blades in the hand, and the phrase quietly names both at once. The question mark is the setter owning the whimsy.

9 across OSSETIA

Excellent: Miss ‘d’Urberville’ and love to return, finding border region (7)

Definition: border region · charade + reversal

Ossetia straddles the Caucasus watershed, divided north and south between Russia and Georgia, so the definition places it by the frontier it sits astride.

  • Excellent → AI A1 is the crossword word for excellent, and its 1 does duty as an I
  • Miss ‘d’Urberville’ → TESS Hardy's heroine, Tess of the d'Urbervilles
  • love → O love is the tennis nought
  • to return — to return is to come back the way you went, so the letters run backwards

Two conventions worth banking for good: love is a nought, and A1 lends its 1 as an I. The colon after 'Excellent' is doing no work but disguise.

10 across PASTEUR

Mouldy Frenchman tears up when cleaned up (7)

Definition: Mouldy Frenchman · anagram

Louis Pasteur is the Frenchman whose germ work gave us pasteurisation, the treatment that keeps milk from going off — so mould is what identifies him.

  • tears up → PASTEUR the seven letters of TEARS UP, rearranged
  • when cleaned up — cleaning something up rearranges it, and here it is the letters that get tidied into a new order

The surface shows a weepy old man being tidied. What the clue wants is the same letters in another order, and the definition leans on the man's own science: pasteurisation is what stops food spoiling.

11 across AMIGO

Is the writer to be sold, my friend? (5)

Definition: my friend · charade

Amigo is Spanish for friend, borrowed into English as a matey form of address, which is exactly how 'my friend' is used.

  • Is → AM put 'is' into the first person and it becomes AM
  • the writer → I the person writing the clue calls himself I
  • to be sold → GO stock that sells goes — going, going, gone

The whole trick is whose voice you are in: 'is' only turns into AM when the speaker is the writer, and the writer is the very next thing the clue names.

12 across AFORESAID

Poorly raised oaf, as previously discussed (9)

Definition: as previously discussed · anagram

Aforesaid is the legal drafter's word for something named earlier in the same document, which is what an office email means by 'as previously discussed'.

  • raised oaf → AFORESAID the nine letters of RAISED OAF, rearranged
  • Poorly — poorly means unwell, and a word in poor condition is one whose letters have fallen out of order

'Poorly' sits where an adverb belongs, so it reads as part of the lout's upbringing rather than as the instruction it is.

13 across I'LL DRINK TO THAT

Evil doctor to tattoo most of infants? Covering bottoms up (3,5,2,4)

Definition: bottoms up · charade + deletion

'Bottoms up' is a drinker's toast, and this is the toast you make when you agree with somebody — both are said with the glass already raised.

  • Evil → ILL ill = evil, as in ill will
  • doctor → DR the usual two-letter abbreviation
  • to tattoo → INK to ink somebody is to tattoo them
  • most of infants → TOT infants are TOTS; most of the word drops its last letter
  • Covering → HAT a hat is a covering — for the head
  • most of — most of a word is all of it but the last letter

Five short pieces run together and none of the joins falls where a word ends: DRINK is built from the doctor and the tattoo, which is where the disguise lives.

17 across UNSATURATED FAT

With endless fury, aunt tasted a dodgy, unhealthy ingredient (11,3)

Definition: unhealthy ingredient · anagram + deletion

Fat is the ingredient every diet counts, so the clue files this one under 'unhealthy' by its family name rather than by nutrition, where it is the better sort.

  • endless fury → FUR FURY without its last letter
  • aunt tasted a → AUNTTASTEDA eleven more letters, taken as they stand
  • endless — an endless word has lost its last letter, so it never reaches its end
  • dodgy — dodgy means not to be trusted, and letters not to be trusted are letters out of their proper order

Fourteen letters of fodder is a lot to hold in the head, so anchor the F and the pair of Us first. 'Dodgy' is the shuffle signal, sitting between the letters and the definition.

20 across RENOVATOR

Fixer-up ran over to stable? No (9)

Definition: Fixer-up · anagram

To renovate is to make an old building good again, so the noun names the person doing it — 'fixer-up' is the informal name for the same trade.

  • ran over to → RENOVATOR the nine letters of RAN OVER TO, rearranged
  • stable? No — stable means fixed in place, so answering 'No' to it makes the letters unstable and free to move

The shuffle signal is a negative: 'stable? No' means unstable, so nothing in front of it will stay put. The horse is there to sell you the stable.

23 across RATIO

Principally relative association that indicates odds? (5)

Definition: Principally relative association that indicates odds? · first letters + &lit

It expresses one quantity relative to another, which is precisely how betting odds are quoted: 3 to 1 is one wearing a bookmaker's coat.

  • relative association that indicates odds → RATIO the first letter of each of the five words, in order
  • Principally — principally means first of all, so it asks for the first letters of the words that follow

'Principally' is the initials instruction, and the sentence it points at is also the definition — which is why the clue is true as a statement as well as useful as a machine.

25 across IBERIAN

Spanish maybe brainier – almost devious (7)

Definition: Spanish maybe · anagram + deletion

The word covers Spain and Portugal together, so a Spaniard is one instance of it rather than a synonym — which is what 'maybe' admits.

  • brainier – almost → BRAINIE BRAINIER stopping just short of its last letter
  • almost — almost a word is the word one letter short of finished
  • devious — devious means not straight, and letters that are not straight are letters out of order

'Almost' is the standing instruction to lop the final letter, and 'maybe' is the setter conceding that Spain is only part of the peninsula.

26 across SWINGER

Acrobat, one who in 60s was iconic (7)

Definition: Acrobat · double definition

Literally it is one who swings, which covers the trapeze act; figuratively it is the permissive party-goer that 'swinging London' made a byword.

  • Acrobat → SWINGER the trapeze artist, who works by swinging
  • one who in 60s was iconic → SWINGER the Swinging Sixties turned the word into a label for a fashionable pleasure-seeker

Both halves hang off the same verb — one swings from a bar, the other swung through a decade — and the comma is the only mark of where the clue splits.

27 across NO MAN IS AN ISLAND

Is Douglas on a peninsula … ? (2,3,2,2,6)

Definition: Is Douglas on a peninsula … ? · cryptic definition

Donne's line means nobody is self-sufficient; the clue makes it literal geography, since Man really is surrounded by water and Douglas is its capital.

  • Is Douglas → not a man being asked a question: Douglas is the capital town of the Isle of Man
  • on a peninsula → the honest reply is no — the place it stands on is ringed by sea, not joined on to anywhere

The clue is a question and the answer is the reply to it: the geography says no, and the rest of the sentence is Donne's most quoted line.

Down

1 down ALOHA

A cow’s audible ‘Goodbye’ (5)

Definition: ‘Goodbye’ · homophone

Aloha is the Hawaiian word used at both ends of a meeting, so it does farewell duty just as readily as it does hello.

  • A cow → ALOHA a beast that lows is a lower
  • audible — something audible is heard rather than read, so the answer is spelt the way the words sound

'Lower' for a cow — a creature that lows — is one of the chestnuts worth banking; it turns up far more often than the moo the surface offers you.

2 down MISDIAL

Offer ring to one who’s not intended? (7)

Definition: Offer ring to one who’s not intended? · cryptic definition

To misdial is to ring a number you did not mean to ring, so the call goes to somebody other than the person you had in mind.

  • Offer ring → not a jeweller's box being opened: a ring is a telephone call, and offering one means punching in a number
  • one who’s not intended → an 'intended' is a fiancée; drop that reading and this is simply the person you did not mean to reach

One joke holds the whole clue up: a proposal made to the wrong woman, read straight, is a call put through to the wrong number.

3 down DOT DOT DOT

Spot Mark and point … (3,3,3)

Definition: … · charade

The clue closes on an ellipsis, and this is what English calls that mark when naming it rather than printing it.

  • Spot → DOT a spot on a die or a dress is a dot
  • Mark → DOT the mark sitting over an i — the name looks like a person's, which is the disguise
  • point → DOT the point in a web address is a dot, as is the decimal point

Three plain synonyms in a row, and the definition is the punctuation the clue ends on: an ellipsis is precisely three of them.

4 down OSAKA

Poo-poos a kabuki’s characters in Japanese port (5)

Definition: Japanese port · hidden word

Osaka is Japan's great commercial harbour city on its own bay, so the definition points at it by nation and by function, one example among several.

  • Poo-poos a kabuki → OSAKA the run starts near the end of 'poo-poos', crosses the stray 'a', and finishes early inside 'kabuki' — unbroken the whole way
  • characters in — characters are letters as well as people, and 'in' says these ones are sitting inside the words rather than being described by them

'Characters in' works twice over — the players in a kabuki drama, and the letters inside the words — and the city straddles two word-gaps, which is where the eye refuses to look.

5 down HIP POCKET

Spooner’s advice to Great Expectations lad considering using pawnbroker where money may be found (3,6)

Definition: where money may be found · spoonerism

A hip pocket is the back trouser pocket a wallet rides in, so it is literally where a man's money may be found.

  • Great Expectations lad → HIP Dickens's boy is Pip; Spooner takes his P and gives him an H
  • considering using pawnbroker → POCKET the advice is 'hock it' — to hock something is to pawn it — and it collects the P that Pip gave up
  • Spooner’s advice to — the Reverend Spooner swapped the opening sounds of two words, so his version of a phrase is that phrase with its first sounds traded

The Reverend Spooner's name is the standing signal to swap two opening sounds, and 'hock' is the pawnshop verb the surface is quietly teaching you.

6 down TASTE

Judgment in hearing’s parallel (5)

Definition: Judgment · double definition

The word covers both the tongue's sense, which sits alongside hearing in the famous five, and the faculty of discrimination we mean by good judgment.

  • Judgment → TASTE discernment — refined judgment of quality, the kind praised in someone with a discerning eye or palate
  • hearing’s parallel → TASTE the five senses run alongside one another, and this is hearing's fellow

The split falls after the first word, and the surface hides it by making 'hearing' sound like a courtroom one rather than a sense.

7 down EYEWASH

Everyman practises hygiene, you say? Baloney! (7)

Definition: Baloney! · charade + homophone

Eyewash is the everyday word for humbug or nonsense — the eye lotion is real, but it is the figurative sense that 'Baloney!' picks up.

  • Everyman → EYE the setter signs himself Everyman, so he is I — the letter said aloud
  • practises hygiene → WASH to wash is to practise hygiene
  • you say — 'you say' asks for the sound of the words rather than their spelling

A setter writing himself into his own clue is a standing convention, and here his pen name is the first person; only the sound of it reaches the answer.

8 down HEREDITY

In this place, heartless simple tune that’s passed down (8)

Definition: that’s passed down · charade + deletion

Heredity is the passing of traits from parent to child, so the definition names the mechanism rather than the trait it delivers.

  • In this place → HERE here = in this place
  • simple tune → DITY a simple tune is a DITTY, and heartless takes its middle letter out
  • heartless — a heartless word is one with its middle taken out

'Heartless' always means the central letter goes, and a five-letter word has exactly one to lose — which is why the setter reached for a ditty rather than a song.

14 down IRRITANTS

Pests playing sitar – with tin whistler at the end (9)

Definition: Pests · anagram + last letter

An irritant is anything that rubs you the wrong way — a rash, a noise, a neighbour — so the definition names them by the nuisance they cause.

  • sitar → SITAR five of the nine letters
  • tin → TIN three more, taken as they stand
  • whistler at the end → R the last letter of whistleR
  • playing — letters at play are letters not staying where they were put
  • at the end — 'at the end' points at the final letter of the word in front of it, not at the whole word

'Playing' shuffles, while 'at the end' quietly asks for one letter. A sitar and a tin whistle sound like a band, which is the last place you look for orders.

15 down ORDERLIES

Calm rests for hospital staff (9)

Definition: hospital staff · charade

An orderly is the hospital worker who moves patients, beds and equipment rather than nursing them, so the definition names them by employer and rank.

  • Calm → ORDER order is calm — a crowd restored to order is a crowd calmed
  • rests → LIES whatever rests, lies: a book lies on a table

The equivalence of calm and order is the step most solvers miss, and the surface's spa-brochure tone is built to keep them missing it.

16 down GUARDIAN

One shooting around outstripped hare, backing over – primarily, this one’s charged to protect (8)

Definition: primarily, this one’s charged to protect · container + charade + middle letters + reversal

A guardian is the person a court appoints to look after someone who cannot look after themselves, so protection is the whole of the office.

  • One shooting → GUN a gun is the thing doing the shooting, and it opens up to let the rest sit inside
  • hare → AR h(AR)e with its outside letters stripped away
  • backing → DIA backing a cause is giving it AID, turned round
  • around — something that goes around another thing is wrapped outside it
  • outstripped — to outstrip is to leave behind, so the letters named are dropped
  • over — in a down clue 'over' means what is written above, which reading upwards reverses

Two traps in one clue: 'primarily' looks like an instruction to take first letters and only means chiefly, and 'backing' is the noun for support, not a direction.

18 down SUNBEAM

Source of warmth, lad’s pronounced grin (7)

Definition: Source of warmth · charade + homophone

A sunbeam is a shaft of sunlight, and sunlight is what actually warms you, so the definition names it by its effect rather than by its look.

  • lad’s pronounced → SUN a lad is a son
  • grin → BEAM to beam is to grin broadly
  • pronounced — pronounced asks for the sound of the word, not its spelling

Son and sun are the classic English pair of soundalikes, and the surface's grinning boy is the same warmth the answer means, one level down.

19 down ANTIGUA

Opposing bulk of US territory, Caribbean island (7)

Definition: Caribbean island · charade + deletion

Antigua, twinned with Barbuda, is an independent state in the Leeward Islands, so the definition places it by sea rather than naming the country.

  • Opposing → ANTI anti- is the prefix for opposing
  • US territory → GUA GUAM is the US territory; the bulk of it is all but the last letter
  • bulk of — the bulk of a word is most of it — everything but the last letter

'Bulk of' is a standing instruction to drop the final letter, and the comma is the only warning that the geography has crossed an ocean.

21 down V-SIGN

What may you get from chavs, ignobly? (1-4)

Definition: What may you get from chavs · hidden word + &lit

Two fingers raised with the back of the hand turned out is Britain's standard gesture of contempt, which is what 'ignobly' promises.

  • chavs, ignobly → VSIGN the gesture's letters run straight across the comma, ending partway into the final word

Every word earns twice: read straight it is an insult offered on a street corner, and read as machinery the answer is sitting in the last two words already.

22 down RISEN

New England gentleman getting up, out of bed (5)

Definition: out of bed · charade + reversal

To have risen is to be up and about, so the definition is the everyday way of saying it — past participle answering past participle.

  • New England → NE New England shortens to its two initials
  • gentleman → SIR a gentleman is addressed as sir
  • getting up — in a down clue the letters run downwards, so anything getting up is read the other way

In a down clue 'getting up' means read the letters from the bottom of the column towards the top, and the surface's early riser is the picture that hides it.

24 down OARED

Cockney mob loudly rowed (5)

Definition: rowed · deletion + homophone

To oar is to propel a boat with oars, so the definition is the past tense of the same action — a verb that survives mainly in crosswords and poetry.

  • mob → HORDE a mob is a horde
  • Cockney → OARED a Cockney drops the aitch off the front, leaving ’ORDE — which is what you say, not what you write
  • Cockney — a Cockney accent drops the aitch off the front of a word
  • loudly — loudly means said out loud, so what matters is the sound and not the spelling

The dropped aitch is the whole device, and it is worth remembering: 'Cockney' in a clue nearly always means an H has gone missing from the front of a word.
