1 across DEMOCRAT

Mog hugging Republican after rally? Not his type! (8)

Definition: Not his type! · charade + container

In American politics a Democrat is the Republican's opposite number, so to a Republican one is precisely not his type — a definition by contrast.

  • rally → DEMO a demo is a rally or protest march
  • Mog → CAT a mog, or moggy, is British for a cat
  • Republican → R R is the party's letter on an American ballot paper
  • hugging — To hug something is to get your arms right round it, so the piece named next is held inside the one doing the hugging.
  • after — 'after' fixes the order: what stands before it in the sentence is written second, behind the piece it follows.

The setter is spotted embracing a Republican after a rally, and somebody sniffs that this is not his sort of person.

Mog signs this puzzle, so the name reads as the setter's own and looks untouchable; it is the pet sense of the word that the wordplay wants.

5 across PHOBIA

Fear of Asian soup mostly prejudice (6)

Definition: Fear · charade + deletion

A phobia is a fear, though a specific and irrational one — the clinical noun where an everyday speaker would just say fear.

  • Asian soup → PHO pho is the Vietnamese beef noodle soup
  • prejudice → BIA prejudice is bias, docked of its final letter
  • mostly — 'mostly' means all but a little of the word, so it arrives without the letter it ends on.

Somebody's dread of Asian soup is dismissed as nothing more than prejudice.

The fear and the prejudice in the surface are the same accusation, which is why the sentence reads as an opinion rather than as a set of instructions.

9 across ALLEGRO

Complete ogre sorted quickly (7)

Definition: quickly · charade + anagram

Allegro is the tempo marking that tells players to take the music at a brisk pace, so it is 'quickly' as musicians write it.

  • Complete → ALL the complete set of something is all of it
  • ogre → EGRO the monster's four letters in a new order
  • sorted — Sorting something puts it into a fresh arrangement, and here it is the letters that come out arranged differently.

An ogre is dealt with completely, and dealt with fast.

'Sorted' is doing two jobs at once — a problem sorted in the surface, letters sorted in the wordplay — and a musical term is the last thing a fairy tale suggests.

10 across SINGLET

Belt out maybe with rented vest (7)

Definition: vest · charade

A singlet is the sleeveless top a boxer or runner wears, which in British English is a vest — what an American would call an undershirt.

  • Belt out → SING to belt a number out is to sing it at full volume
  • rented → LET a flat that has been rented out has been let
  • maybe — 'maybe' flags an example rather than an equivalent: belting something out is one way of doing what the first piece spells.

A performer lets rip on stage wearing a hired vest.

'Belt' wants to be clothing, sitting as it does next to a vest, and the wordplay needs the singing sense instead.

11 across HEREFORDSHIRE

Present president's take on English county (13)

Definition: English county · charade

Herefordshire is the cider-and-cattle county on the Welsh border, so 'English county' names it by class — one member of the set.

  • Present → HERE answering a school register, present means here
  • president's → FORDS Gerald Ford was a US president, and the 's comes along with him
  • take on → HIRE a firm that takes somebody on hires them

A president offers his opinion of an English county.

'Present president' is the kind of repetition that reads as emphasis, and it is two separate pieces — and a particular president, not the current one.

13 across AVAST

A huge cruise stop? (5)

Definition: cruise stop? · charade

Avast is the sailors' cry meaning 'stop!', so on a cruise it is the shout that brings whatever is happening to a halt.

  • A → A the clue's own indefinite article, taken as a single letter
  • huge → VAST vast means huge

A stop-off on the itinerary of an enormous cruise ship.

'Cruise stop' wants to be a port of call, and the answer is not a place at all but the shout that halts things at sea.

15 across ELECTRODE

Conductor to choose right composition (9)

Definition: Conductor · charade

An electrode is the conductor a current passes through going into or out of a cell, so this is the physics sense, not the orchestral one.

  • to choose → ELECT to elect is to choose, by vote or otherwise
  • right → R R is the standard short form of right, as in stage directions
  • composition → ODE an ode is a composition in verse

A conductor picking the right piece to perform.

'Conductor' and 'composition' pull the whole sentence towards the concert hall, and the answer belongs in a laboratory.

17 across PLACEMENT

Endless amusement with setter setting (9)

Definition: setting · charade + deletion

A placement is where something has been set down, and a setting is the position a thing is put in — both nouns name the same idea.

  • amusement → PLA amusement is play, docked of its last letter
  • setter → CEMENT cement is a setter in the literal sense: pour it and it goes hard
  • Endless — Endless means the end has gone, so the word turns up one letter shorter than it should be.

Endless fun in the company of the person who compiles the crossword.

'Setter' in a cryptic almost always means the compiler, so ME or MOG is what a solver reaches for first; here it is something that sets in a mixer.

18 across BLEND

Mix up introductory letter from bank and loan (5)

Definition: Mix up · charade + first letter

To blend is to mix things up until they are one, which is 'mix up' in the same tense and the same everyday sense.

  • letter from bank → B the first letter of bank
  • loan → LEND to loan is to lend
  • introductory — An introduction comes at the very start, so only the character the word opens with is wanted.

The bank writes an opening letter about muddling up a loan.

'Letter from bank' reads as correspondence dropping through the door, and the letter wanted is a single character of the alphabet.

19 across CHAMPS ELYSEES

Cheesy samples arranged in French way (6,7)

Definition: French way · anagram

The Champs-Elysees is Paris's grandest avenue, so 'way' means a road and 'French' places it: a definition by example of a thoroughfare.

  • Cheesy samples → CHEESYSAMPLES anagram fodder, thirteen letters
  • arranged → anagram indicator
  • arranged — Arranging things means setting them out in a chosen order, which is licence to put the letters into a different one.

Samples of cheese laid out on a board in the French manner.

'In French way' reads as 'in the French style', which is where the definition hides: it is one particular street, not a manner of doing things.

24 across INTROIT

Regularly disappearing in nature, or into prayer (7)

Definition: prayer · alternate letters

The introit is the psalm or antiphon sung as the priest comes in at the start of Mass, so 'prayer' names it by what it is.

  • in nature, or into → INTROIT the first, third, fifth and every following odd letter of that run, read in order
  • Regularly disappearing — Letters vanishing at a regular interval means every second one goes, and what survives is read straight off in order.

The countryside is steadily vanishing, and somebody turns to prayer about it.

The letters wanted run across a comma and straight through two little words, which is why the phrase reads as ordinary English and never looks like fodder.

25 across CHARADE

Tea grader outwardly shunned pretence (7)

Definition: pretence · charade + deletion

A charade is a pretence kept up for show — going through the motions of something nobody believes in — so the two nouns match.

  • Tea → CHA cha, like char, is British slang for tea, taken from the Chinese
  • grader → RADE the word with the letters at its two ends taken away
  • outwardly shunned — What is shunned outwardly is dropped from the outside, so the word gives up the letter at each of its ends.

A tea grader makes a visible point of having no time for pretence.

Tea is worth learning twice over, since cha and char both stand for it, and the answer happens to name the very kind of clue it is sitting in.

26 across OXYGEN

Cattle securing tails on violently passing gas (6)

Definition: gas · container + last letters

Oxygen is the gas that makes up a fifth of the air, so 'gas' is the category word: what the element is at ordinary temperatures.

  • Cattle → OXEN oxen are cattle, the old plural of ox
  • violently passing → YG the closing letters of those two words, in that order
  • securing — To secure something is to hold it fast, and letters held fast are kept inside the piece doing the holding.
  • tails on — A tail is the end of a thing, so each word named after this gives up only the letter it ends on.

Cattle thunder past and, less politely, pass gas as they go.

The phrase 'passing gas' is split down the middle: one half hands over a letter to the wordplay and the other half is the definition.

27 across STREAKER

Footballer essentially out for each uncovered runner (8)

Definition: uncovered runner · substitution

A streaker is somebody who runs naked across a pitch, so 'uncovered runner' is a literal description: wearing nothing, and running.

  • Footballer → STRIKER a striker is the forward whose job is scoring
  • essentially out → I the letter at the dead centre of that seven-letter word is the one that leaves
  • each → EA ea. is the price-list short form of each
  • essentially — The essence of a word is its very middle, so this points at the single letter sitting at the centre of it.
  • out for — 'out for' is an exchange: the letter named before it leaves and the piece named after it takes the vacated place.

A footballer is out of the game every time somebody runs across the pitch with nothing on.

'Uncovered' looks like a wordplay instruction, a letter laid bare, and it belongs to the definition instead — which is where the pitch invader turns up.

1 down DRAW

Produce weapon and charge around (4)

Definition: Produce weapon · reversal

To draw is to produce a weapon — pulling a sword or a pistol out ready for use — which is the cowboy sense of the verb.

  • charge → WARD a charge is a ward, the person left in somebody's care
  • around — Turning a thing around leaves it facing the other way, so the letters go into the grid in the opposite order.

Somebody pulls out a weapon and goes charging about with it.

'Charge' looks like the cavalry rush the rest of the sentence promises, and it is the guardianship sense that carries the letters.

2 down MULCH

Compost often coats landscaper's nose (5)

Definition: Compost · container + first letter

Mulch is the rotted covering spread over a garden bed, and compost is what gardeners spread there, so the two nouns swap freely.

  • often → MUCH much and often both mean a great deal of the time — 'she doesn't go there much'
  • landscaper's → L the first letter of landscaper, taken as the word's nose
  • coats — A coat goes on the outside of whatever it covers, so this word wraps one piece right round another.
  • nose — A nose is the part that sticks out in front, so it asks for the letter at the very front of the word.

Compost piled so deep in a garden that it comes up over the landscaper's nose.

Every word of the garden picture earns its keep: an adverb of frequency turns into the outer word, and the landscaper contributes nothing but the letter he leads with.

3 down CIGARETTE

Strange erect gait that could harm your health (9)

Definition: that could harm your health · anagram

Every packet carries the warning, so the words point at a cigarette by its notorious effect rather than by any synonym for it.

  • erect gait → ERECTGAIT anagram fodder, nine letters
  • Strange → anagram indicator
  • Strange — Something strange is not as it should be, and letters that are not as they should be have been put in the wrong order.

Someone walks with an oddly stiff, upright step, in a way that looks bad for their health.

'Strange erect gait' reads as a symptom, so the sentence poses as a description of an illness rather than of something that causes one.

4 down ALOOF

Curry house potatoes, fine cold (5)

Definition: cold · charade

Aloof is cold in the human sense — distant, unwilling to mix — so it stands in for 'cold' said of a person rather than of weather.

  • Curry house potatoes → ALOO aloo is the potato on an Indian menu — saag aloo, aloo gobi
  • fine → F F is the pencil grade meaning fine

Potatoes ordered at the curry house arrive fine in themselves but stone cold.

'Fine cold' reads as one verdict on the food, which hides the join: those two words belong to opposite halves of the clue.

6 down HANDS

People working both sides of Harrods? (5)

Definition: People working · charade + outer letters

Hands are workers — factory hands, all hands on deck — so the plural noun answers 'people working' rather than naming any body part.

  • sides of Harrods? → H the near side of the shop's name is the letter it begins with
  • both → AND taking one and the other means saying the joining word out loud, and here it is spelt out in letters
  • sides of Harrods? → S the far side of the same name is the letter it finishes on

The staff working at either end of Harrods, the Knightsbridge department store.

Read the two end letters of the shop as a pair — H and S — and write the little joining word down as letters rather than saying it. That is the whole device.

7 down BALTIMORE

Brummie dish with added port (9)

Definition: port · charade

Baltimore is a big seaport on Chesapeake Bay, so 'port' defines it by example: the class word standing in for one member of it.

  • Brummie dish → BALTI the balti curry was invented in Birmingham, and a Brummie is someone from there
  • added → MORE more is what you have once something has been added

A Birmingham curry served with a glass of port beside it.

The surface is entirely a meal, with 'port' posing as the drink to finish; the definition is the one word not doing dinner.

8 down AT THE READY

They're confused with data set (2,3,5)

Definition: set · anagram

Set means prepared and waiting — 'all set' — which is what being at the ready describes, and both follow 'is' in the same way.

  • They're → THEYRE six letters of fodder, the apostrophe ignored
  • data → DATA four more letters of fodder
  • confused → anagram indicator
  • confused — Something confused has been muddled out of its proper order, and the letters get exactly that treatment.

People are left baffled by a set of figures somebody has handed them.

'Data set' is a real phrase and the clue counts on you reading it as one, which buries the boundary between the fodder and the definition.

10 down SURFEIT

Use a browser link, looking up excessive amount (7)

Definition: excessive amount · charade + reversal

A surfeit is more of something than anybody needs, usually food or drink, with the sense of over-indulgence built into the noun.

  • Use a browser → SURF surfing is what you do with a browser
  • link → EIT a link between two people is a tie, entered the other way up
  • looking up — In a down clue the letters climb the grid, so 'looking up' asks for that piece to be entered from its far end backwards.

Somebody online follows a link to look up how much is too much.

'Looking up' reads as researching something, which is the whole disguise: it is an instruction about direction, and has nothing to do with the internet.

12 down CAPPUCCINO

Top star of opera cut over drink (10)

Definition: drink · charade + deletion

A cappuccino is the espresso-and-foamed-milk coffee, so 'drink' is the plain category word: the thing that ends up in the cup.

  • Top → CAP the top of a bottle is its cap
  • star of opera → PUCCIN Puccini wrote Tosca and La Boheme, and his last letter is docked here
  • over → O o is the cricket scoreboard's short form for an over
  • cut — Cutting something shortens it, so the name arrives without the letter it ends on.

Opera's biggest name has his part trimmed while everyone is out having a drink.

'Top star of opera' reads as one phrase, and prising the first word off it is the whole solve — after that the composer is unmistakable.

14 down ADAMANTLY

Rock frontman eviscerated legally with conviction (9)

Definition: with conviction · charade + deletion

To say a thing adamantly is to say it with conviction, refusing to be shifted, so the adverb takes the phrase's place exactly.

  • Rock frontman → ADAMANT Adam Ant fronted the British band Adam and the Ants in the 1980s
  • legally → LY the outside letters of the word, with its insides removed
  • eviscerated — To eviscerate is to take the guts out, so the word keeps only its outermost letters.

A rock star is gutted in court and comes away with a conviction.

'With conviction' sits beside 'legally' so that both belong to the courtroom, and the definition is the sense no jury ever uses.

15 down EVEREST

Person booted from Genesis before others peak (7)

Definition: peak · charade

Everest is the highest peak on earth, so 'peak' defines it by example: the class word doing duty for one famous member.

  • Person booted from Genesis → EVE Genesis has her thrown out of Eden alongside Adam
  • others → REST the rest of a group is the others in it
  • before — 'before' settles the order, putting the piece named first ahead of the one that comes after it.

Someone is thrown out of the band Genesis just before the others hit their peak.

'Genesis' looks like the band and 'peak' like the height of a career; the book of the Bible and a mountain are what the clue is after.

16 down TABLEWARE

Flat tuba ultimately ruined live set for diners (9)

Definition: set for diners · charade + last letters

Tableware is the plates, glasses and cutlery laid out for a meal, which is exactly the set put in front of the people eating.

  • Flat tuba ultimately → TA the closing letters of those two words, in the order they stand
  • ruined → BLEW somebody who blew it ruined it
  • live → ARE to live is to be, and 'are' is that verb in the plural present
  • ultimately — 'ultimately' points at the end of things, so the words before it give up only the letter each one finishes on.

A tuba playing flat wrecks a band's live set.

'Live set' belongs to the band, not to the table, and 'ruined' is the dominant false path: it looks like an instruction to shuffle and is a past tense meaning wrecked.

20 down PRONE

Given face down (5)

Definition: Given · double definition

Prone to something means given to it, an inclination; prone also means lying flat on your front, and the two senses have drifted a long way apart.

  • Given → the sense of being inclined towards something — given to sulking, given to accidents
  • face down → the posture sense: stretched out flat with the face towards the floor

A card is dealt face down to the player who was given it.

Two words, two senses and no joinery between them, which is why the clue has nothing in it that looks like an instruction.

21 down YACHT

Current ruptures your once capsized boat (5)

Definition: boat · container + reversal

A yacht is a boat, the pleasure kind under sail or engine, so 'boat' is the plain category word that covers it.

  • your once → YHT thy is the old-fashioned your, and it goes in overturned
  • Current → AC AC is alternating current, the sort that comes out of the wall
  • ruptures — Something that ruptures a thing breaks into it, so this piece is forced inside rather than laid alongside.
  • capsized — A capsized craft has gone right over, so the word it applies to is written the other way round.

A powerful current tears apart the boat you used to own.

'Current' looks like the water doing the damage and is the electrical sort, while 'your once' is a single archaic word — the step that hides longest.

22 down SHARK

Quiet thing taking animals from the water? (5)

Definition: from the water? · charade

A shark is the sea's most notorious hunter, so 'from the water' points at it by habitat while the rest of the clue describes what it does.

  • Quiet → SH sh! is what you say when you want quiet
  • thing taking animals → ARK Noah's ark took two of every animal aboard

Something silent is lifting animals out of the water.

Read straight through, the clue also describes the creature it is building: silent, and helping itself to animals in the sea.

23 down WEAR

Survive with bit of cereal (4)

Definition: Survive · charade

To wear is to last: a coat that wears well survives years of use, so 'survive' catches the sense of standing up to time.

  • with → W w is the standard short form of with, as in a recipe
  • bit of cereal → EAR an ear of wheat or corn is the seed head on the stalk

Getting through the day on nothing but a bit of cereal.

'With' looks like joinery holding the sentence upright, and it is carrying a letter — the commonest one-letter trick in the book.
