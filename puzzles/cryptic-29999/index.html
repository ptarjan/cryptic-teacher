Across

8 across JEWELLER

Rock purveyor we will make rude noises about (8)

Definition: Rock purveyor · container

Rock is slang for a gemstone, so somebody who purveys rocks is the person selling jewellery over a counter.

  • make rude noises → JEER to jeer is to make rude noises at somebody
  • we will → WELL we will contracts to we'll, and the apostrophe counts for nothing
  • about — To go about something is to go all the way round it, so the word for the noises closes round the letters named in front of it.

A dealer in rock that we are all going to heckle.

'Rock' is the trade's slang for a big diamond, and the surface leans on the music sense to keep you away from the shop window.

9 across U BOLT

Sprinter in bar bent back to grasp nuts (1,4)

Definition: Sprinter · double definition

Usain Bolt appears on a start list as U. Bolt, and a U-bolt is a rod bent into that letter, threaded at both ends to take nuts.

  • Sprinter → the fastest man alive, as an entry list would print him: an initial and a surname
  • bar bent back to grasp nuts → the ironmonger's version: a rod curved into a horseshoe with a thread at each end

A sprinter in a pub, leaning back over the counter to grab a handful of nuts.

The pub reading of 'bar' and 'nuts' does all the misleading: the bar wanted is a length of metal and the nuts are the threaded kind you tighten with a spanner.

10 across KENT

Scots knew England’s garden? (4)

Definition: Scots knew · double definition

Kent is the Scots past tense of 'ken', to know, and Kent is also the county long advertised as the Garden of England.

  • Scots knew → north of the border 'ken' is to know, and this is its past tense
  • England’s garden? → the orchard county south-east of London, nicknamed the Garden of England

A question about what the Scots once knew of the garden county down south.

The question mark is the setter flagging a nickname rather than a dictionary definition, and the capital letter that would give the county away is hidden by the start of the clue.

11 across CHESTERTON

Author as boxer, could one say – heavyweight (10)

Definition: Author · charade

G.K. Chesterton wrote the Father Brown mysteries, so 'Author' is the plainest possible pointer to the surname.

  • boxer → CHESTER a box is a chest, so one who boxes could be renamed from the other word
  • heavyweight → TON a ton is the heaviest weight in everyday British use, 2,240 pounds of it
  • could one say — The setter is admitting the word beside it is invented on the spot: somebody who boxes is being renamed after another word for a box.

A writer who might pass for a boxer, and a substantial one at that.

Chesterton was a famously enormous man, so 'heavyweight' describes him as neatly as it clues the letters — which is the joke the dash sets up.

12 across KIDULT

Childish type, endlessly dim, breaking equipment (6)

Definition: Childish type · container + deletion

A kidult is a grown-up who keeps a child's tastes in toys and cartoons, which is a childish type in person.

  • equipment → KIT your kit is the equipment you take to a match
  • dim → DULL a dim person is a dull one, and this word arrives one letter shorter than it is printed
  • endlessly — A word read endlessly never reaches its end, so the final letter of the one beside it is left off.
  • breaking — Something breaking into a place has got inside it, so the letters just named split open the word that follows.

A childish sort smashing the gear up and too dim to know better.

The word is a portmanteau of kid and adult, barely thirty years old, and the surface's picture of a dim vandal is what stops 'equipment' looking like three letters.

14 across TAKE A PEW

Invite to relax and steal from church? (4,1,3)

Definition: Invite to relax · double definition

The phrase is the everyday British invitation to sit down; word for word, it also describes carrying a bench out of a church.

  • Invite to relax → the standard British way of telling a visitor to sit down and make themselves comfortable
  • steal from church? → the same three words read one at a time, as a plan involving a bench and a getaway

An offer to make yourself comfortable that sounds like permission to walk off with the furniture.

'Take a pew' is ordinary spoken English for sit down, and reading it literally — a pew being a church bench — turns a welcome into a theft.

15 across SHADOWY

Indistinct notice with garish border (7)

Definition: Indistinct · container

Something shadowy is hard to make out, half-lost in shade — the visual sense of indistinct rather than the vague-idea one.

  • garish → SHOWY a showy waistcoat is a garish one
  • notice → AD a notice in the paper is an ad, short for advertisement
  • border — A border runs right round the outside of a thing, so the word beside it encircles the letters already named.

A blurred sign with a loud, gaudy frame round the edge of it.

'Indistinct' asks for the plain sense of a word that usually carries a hint of menace, and there is nothing sinister at all about the surface's gaudy frame.

17 across ASPERSE

Slander, heartlessly audacious in itself (7)

Definition: Slander · charade + outer letters

To asperse somebody is to attack their reputation with false charges, which is what slandering them is; both are verbs of damage by speech.

  • audacious → AS the outside letters of the word, with the seven in the middle thrown away
  • in itself → PERSE per se is the Latin for 'in itself', its two words run together
  • heartlessly — A word taken heartlessly loses its middle, and here the whole middle goes rather than the single central letter the device usually removes, leaving only the two ends.

A piece of slander described as cruel and brazen in its own right.

The verb hides inside the familiar phrase 'cast aspersions', which is where most solvers have met it without ever meeting it on its own.

20 across CHARTRES

Cathedral search resolved: it’s right in the centre (8)

Definition: Cathedral · anagram + container

The town is known almost entirely for its cathedral, the most celebrated in France, so the definition names the place by its one famous building.

  • search → CHARES the six letters of the word rearranged, ready to be split open
  • right → RT Rt is how Right is written in Rt Hon, the style given to a cabinet minister
  • resolved — To resolve a thing is to break it into its parts, and letters broken apart are free to come back together in any order.
  • in the centre — The centre is the middle of what has just been assembled, so the letters named next drop into a gap halfway along it.

A hunt for the cathedral ends happily: it is right there in the middle of town.

Chartres is the cathedral town south-west of Paris, famous for its blue glass, and the clue's closing phrase works both as the answer to a lost tourist and as the instruction.

22 across SATURN

God posed with big pot of tea (6)

Definition: God · charade

Saturn is the Roman god of sowing and harvest, whose festival gave us Saturnalia, so 'God' names him by example.

  • posed → SAT anyone who posed for a portrait sat for it
  • big pot of tea → URN the urn is the catering-sized boiler tea comes out of at a village hall

A god sitting for his portrait beside an enormous pot of tea.

'Big pot of tea' points hard at a teapot and wants the tea urn instead, the metal drum with a tap on the front at every British fete.

23 across CINEMAGOER

Screen addict perhaps, one contorted with grimace (10)

Definition: Screen addict perhaps · anagram

A cinemagoer is somebody who goes regularly to films, one instance of a screen addict, which is why the definition carries 'perhaps'.

  • one → ONE three letters of fodder, the word itself rather than the usual single letter
  • grimace → GRIMACE seven more letters, which with the three before them make the full ten
  • contorted — A face contorted has its features hauled out of place, and letters hauled out of place land in a new order.

Somebody hooked on screens, their face twisted into a grimace.

'One' is fodder here rather than the I or A it usually stands for, which is what keeps the ten letters from falling into place, and 'perhaps' warns that the answer is only one sort of screen addict.

24 across BOON

Youngster spends year on benefit (4)

Definition: benefit · deletion + charade

A boon is a blessing or a help — 'a real boon' — which is 'benefit' in the sense of something that does you good, not a payment.

  • Youngster → BOY a boy is a youngster, and he arrives here a letter lighter
  • year → Y Y is the standard short form of year, and it is the letter parted with
  • on → ON the little word taken exactly as it stands and added at the end
  • spends — To spend a thing is to part with it, so what the clue names next is gone from the letters standing before it.

A young person spending a year living on state benefit.

'On benefit' is the British phrase for drawing state welfare, and the clue quietly splits it in two: one word hands over its own letters and the other is the definition.

25 across HIT ON

Suddenly discover hotel is losing pounds (3,2)

Definition: Suddenly discover · deletion

To hit on an idea is to come across it suddenly, the way a solution arrives unlooked for — discovery with the accident built in.

  • hotel → HILTON the Hilton is a hotel, one of those chains whose name stands in for the thing itself
  • pounds → L L is money here: the £ sign is a written L, from the Latin libra
  • losing — Something losing a thing no longer has it, so the letter named next simply drops out of the word before.

A hotel chain shedding money hand over fist.

'Hotel' in a cryptic is nearly always the single letter H from the radio alphabet, and this clue wants a whole chain instead.

26 across NOCTURNE

Short walk in once moving moonlit scene (8)

Definition: moonlit scene · anagram + container

A nocturne is a painting or a piece of music evoking night, so 'moonlit scene' names the subject the word is built around.

  • once moving → NOCE the four letters of 'once' rearranged, ready to be split open
  • Short walk → TURN a turn round the garden is a short walk
  • in — 'in' drops the piece just named inside the letters that follow it.
  • moving — Anything moving will not stay put, and letters that will not stay put come out in a different order.

A stroll taken through a moonlit view that was once rather affecting.

'Once' is fodder rather than a word about time, and 'moving' is an instruction rather than a comment on the view — the wistfulness of the surface is entirely decorative.

Down

1 down NEHEMIAH

One hard man, he was involved with Ezra initially (8)

Definition: One hard man, he was involved with Ezra initially · &lit + anagram + first letter

The whole clue reads straight: Nehemiah was the stern governor who rebuilt Jerusalem's walls beside the scribe Ezra.

  • One → I the figure 1 doing duty as a letter
  • hard → H H is the hard of a pencil grade, HB and 2H
  • man, he → MANHE five letters handed over as printed, the comma counting for nothing
  • Ezra initially → E the letter the prophet's name begins with
  • was involved — Somebody involved in a mess is tangled up in it, and letters tangled together do not come out in the order they went in.
  • initially — At the initial stage of a name there is only one letter to be had, and 'initially' asks for exactly that one.

A tough character who first got himself mixed up with somebody called Ezra.

Nehemiah and Ezra share the pair of Old Testament books that tell how Jerusalem's walls went back up, and every word here describes the man while also handing over a letter.

2 down FELT

Considered a soft fabric (4)

Definition: Considered · double definition

Two everyday senses of one short word: the past tense of 'feel' meaning judged, and the pressed woollen cloth.

  • Considered → the past tense of 'feel' in its opinion sense rather than its touching sense
  • a soft fabric → the matted woollen cloth used for hats, snooker tables and pen tips

Somebody weighing up a piece of soft cloth.

'Considered' is feeling with the mind rather than the fingers, which is the sense that keeps the fabric out of sight until the second half of the clue.

3 down PLACET

Dwelling on tense vote in favour (6)

Definition: vote in favour · charade

A placet is the formal yes recorded in a university or church assembly, so the definition names it by the act it performs.

  • Dwelling → PLACE your place is where you dwell — come round to my place
  • tense → T T is the grammarian's tense
  • on — In a down entry whatever sits on something else is above it, so the word named first goes on top of the letter named next.

Somebody brooding over a tense ballot that went the right way.

'Dwelling' looks like the brooding verb the surface wants and is really the noun for a house, and a placet is Latin — 'it pleases' — the word Cambridge and church assemblies use for an aye.

4 down TRIESTE

Sad that Spain has invaded a foreign port (7)

Definition: a foreign port · container

Trieste is a working Adriatic seaport in north-east Italy, so the definition names it by example rather than by any synonym.

  • Sad → TRISTE triste is the French and Spanish word for sad, borrowed into English for a melancholy mood
  • Spain → E E is Spain's international vehicle letter, from España — the oval sticker on the back of the car
  • has invaded — An army that has invaded is inside the country it attacked, so the letter standing before it ends up within the word named earlier.

A gloomy bulletin that Spanish forces have taken a harbour somewhere abroad.

Trieste is the Italian seaport at the head of the Adriatic, tucked against the Slovenian border, so 'foreign' is written from a British desk.

5 down OUT-TAKES

They don’t make the film, dates having been switched (3-5)

Definition: They don’t make the film · cycling

Out-takes are the takes dropped in the edit, so the definition puns on 'make the film' as making the finished version.

  • dates → OUT to date somebody is to take them out, and this is the back half of that phrase, sent round to the front
  • dates → TAKES the front half of the same phrase, following on with its letters untouched
  • having been switched — Switching two things puts each where the other stood, so the back half of a phrase comes round to the front with its own letters undisturbed.

A film that never got shot because the shooting dates were swapped about.

'They don't make the film' has nothing to do with production: making the film is making the final cut, and these are the shots that failed to.

6 down TOURNAMENT

What we are called in explosive competition (10)

Definition: competition · container

A tournament is a competition run as a series of matches down to one winner, so the definition is the plain general noun.

  • explosive → TNT TNT, trinitrotoluene, is the crossword's standard explosive
  • What we are called → OURNAME what we are called is our name, the two words run together
  • in — 'in' puts the words just named inside the ones that follow, the plainest insertion instruction there is.

The name we go by, caught up in a fiercely explosive contest.

'Explosive' is an adjective in the story and the noun for the stuff itself in the wordplay, and the last word of the clue is the definition standing alone.

7 down STOOGE

Time goose should be cooked in foil (6)

Definition: foil · anagram

A foil is the plain partner set against a comedian to make him look funny, and a stooge is exactly that hired straight man.

  • Time → T T is the standard short form of time, as in half-time
  • goose → GOOSE five more letters of fodder, which with the one before them makes six
  • should be cooked — Cooking changes whatever goes into the pot, so the letters beside it come out in an order they did not go in.

A recipe note about roasting a goose wrapped up in foil.

Every word of the surface is kitchen talk, so the definition sits there looking like cooking wrap until the letters come out and it turns into a person.

13 down UNDERNEATH

Hunt neared ground, positioned for concealment? (10)

Definition: positioned for concealment? · anagram

Something underneath is tucked below another thing and out of sight, which is what 'positioned for concealment' describes rather than names.

  • Hunt neared → HUNTNEARED ten letters of fodder, exactly the length the grid wants
  • ground — 'Ground' here is the past tense of grind, and anything ground down has lost the shape and the order it arrived in.

A hunt closing in on the patch of ground where something is hiding.

'Hunt neared ground' reads as a chase running its quarry to earth, so the last word of that phrase looks like scenery right up until it turns out to be the instruction.

16 down WAR PAINT

Distortion isn’t commonly cosmetic (3,5)

Definition: cosmetic · charade

War paint is the joking name for heavy make-up, so 'cosmetic' names it by what is actually in the jar.

  • Distortion → WARP the warp in a board is the distortion the rain left in it
  • isn’t → AINT 'ain't' is the everyday non-standard version, and the apostrophe goes
  • commonly — 'Commonly' asks for the common way of saying the word beside it — the form a grammar teacher would strike out of an essay.

A claim that distortion is not, as a rule, a matter of mere make-up.

War paint is what British English calls make-up laid on thick, borrowed from the paint a warrior puts on before a battle.

18 down SORBONNE

Aching to suppress German city university (8)

Definition: university · container

The Sorbonne is the historic heart of the University of Paris, so 'university' points at it by example.

  • Aching → SORE an aching knee is a sore one
  • German city → BONN Bonn, on the Rhine, was West Germany's capital until 1990
  • to suppress — To suppress a thing is to hold it down out of sight, so the word before the instruction closes over the letters that follow it.

Somebody aching to hold a German city down.

'Aching to' reads as longing to do something, which is the phrase's ordinary use in English and has nothing to do with the pain the letters actually need.

19 down ASTOUND

Stagger about, ‘run out’ replaced by ‘stumped’ (7)

Definition: Stagger · substitution

To stagger somebody is to knock them sideways with surprise — the figurative sense, not the unsteady walk.

  • about → AROUND about ten o'clock is around ten o'clock
  • ‘run out’ → R R is the run on a cricket scorecard, and the clue is sending it out
  • ‘stumped’ → ST st is how a batter stumped by the wicketkeeper is entered on that same scorecard
  • replaced by — 'Replaced by' names a swap: what has just been quoted leaves the word, and the thing quoted next stands in the gap it made.

A scorer amending a cricket dismissal from run out to stumped.

Run out and stumped are two ways a cricketer loses their wicket, and the quotation marks are the setter's way of saying the words are being handled as marks on a card rather than as events.

21 down HEIGHT

The peak hour, dinner time? (6)

Definition: The peak · charade

The height of a thing is its highest point: the height of summer, the height of fashion, which is what a peak is.

  • hour → H h is the standard short form of hour, as on a 24h clock
  • dinner time? → EIGHT the hour people sit down to eat, as in the old film title Dinner at Eight

The busiest hour of the evening, somewhere around the time dinner is served.

'The peak hour' looks like a single phrase — rush hour — so the definition seems to run on when in fact it stops after two words.

22 down STRICT

Uncompromising boss ultimately deceived on Radio 4 (6)

Definition: Uncompromising · charade + last letter + homophone

A strict rule or a strict parent allows no bending, which is what uncompromising means; both are adjectives for refusing to give ground.

  • boss → S the very last letter of the word
  • deceived → TRICT to deceive somebody is to trick them, and that past tense said aloud ends on a hard t
  • ultimately — What comes ultimately comes at the very end, so only the final letter of the word before it is wanted.
  • on Radio 4 — Something on the radio is heard and not read, so the word beside it is wanted for its sound; the 4 only finishes the name of the BBC's speech station and carries no instruction.

A hard-line boss caught telling lies during a Radio 4 interview.

'Tricked' spoken has no separate k in it at all: the ck and the -ed run together into one hard ct, and the spelling here records the noise rather than the word.

24 down BOUT

Start to bash away in contest (4)

Definition: contest · charade + first letter

A bout is one fight or round of one — a boxing bout, a wrestling bout — so 'contest' is the plain noun sense.

  • Start to bash → B the letter bash begins with
  • away → OUT somebody away from home is out
  • Start to — The start of a word is its opening letter, and that is all the clue takes from the word standing next to it.

A fighter starting to bash away in the middle of a contest.

'Bash away' reads as one phrasal verb, hammering on and on, and the clue takes a single letter from the first word and the whole of the second.
