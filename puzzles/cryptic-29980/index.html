1 across CLOSING DOWN

First of the chickens moulting as programmes finishing once (7,4)

Definition: programmes finishing once · charade + first letter

Closing down is what a channel does when its programmes stop for the night, from the era when broadcasting ended before midnight.

  • the chickens → C the letter the bird's name opens with
  • moulting → LOSINGDOWN a moulting bird is shedding the soft feathers underneath, so it is losing its down
  • First of — Asking for the first of something takes only the letter standing at the front of the word that follows.

Hens shedding their feathers while the evening's television programmes come to an end.

'Moulting' looks like an instruction to shuffle letters and is a plain synonym instead. 'Once' points at the years when British channels shut for the night rather than running on until dawn.

9 across HAM ROLL

Poor actor’s part as expressed on picnic menu (3,4)

Definition: on picnic menu · charade + homophone

A ham roll is the filled bread roll of every buffet, which is why the setter points at the menu it appears on rather than at a synonym.

  • Poor actor → HAM a ham is an actor who overplays every line
  • part → ROLL an actor's part is a role, and that sound is spelt another way in a baker's window
  • as expressed — Expressed means said out loud, so what counts about the word before it is its sound and not its spelling.

An overacted part being declaimed while somebody reads what is on offer for the picnic.

Both food words in the answer are theatre words first: the ham is the actor and the part is his role, and only the sound of the second one ever reaches the picnic basket.

10 across NO CAN DO

Bottle party? (2,3,2)

Definition: Bottle party? · &lit + charade

A bottle party is a do where guests bring their own bottles, so the clue names the answer by reading its words literally instead of by its sense of refusal.

  • Bottle → NOCAN at this sort of party the drink turns up in bottles, so the tin is the thing the room has none of
  • party → DO a do is a party, from a works do to a big do

An invitation to the kind of party where every guest brings a bottle.

Both words work twice over, and the joke is about the answer's own syllables rather than its meaning: a party supplied with bottles is a do at which the other container is missing. Nothing here defines the flat refusal the phrase normally is.

11 across LUTHERANS

Martin’s followers reform most of unearthly beliefs ultimately (9)

Definition: Martin’s followers · anagram + deletion + last letter

Martin Luther's followers are the members of the Protestant church founded on his teaching, so the plural definition names the people rather than the creed.

  • most of unearthly → UNEARTHL eight of that word's nine letters, the tail one dropped
  • beliefs ultimately → S the letter that word finishes on
  • reform — To re-form a thing is to form it again in a different shape, which is what happens to the letters it governs.
  • most of — Most of a word is all of it bar the end, so its final letter is left behind before anything else happens.
  • ultimately — 'Ultimately' points at the very end of the word before it, meaning its last letter and nothing more.

A congregation finally overhauling most of its unearthly doctrine.

'Reform' is the joke as well as the instruction: the Martin whose followers these are set the Reformation going, and only a first name is needed to name him.

12 across DOWNS

They’re not here, but are in Sussex and Hampshire (5)

Definition: They’re not here · double definition

The Downs are the chalk hills of southern England, and in solvers' shorthand the downs are the clues running vertically — the ones this row does not hold.

  • They’re not here → the puzzle talking about itself: in an across entry, the clues running the other way are the ones not in this row
  • in Sussex and Hampshire → the chalk hill ranges that roll through both of those southern counties carry the word as their name

A claim that certain things are absent from here but present in two southern counties.

One half of this is a crossword being cheeky about its own grid, which is why 'not here' has to be read as a position on the page; the other half is ordinary English geography.

13 across DEAN

College official – idle, vain, absent now and again (4)

Definition: College official · alternate letters

A dean heads a college faculty or a cathedral chapter, so the definition names an office rather than describing the person holding it.

  • idle, vain → DEAN the second and fourth letters of each of the two words, taken in turn
  • absent now and again — Letters absent now and again means every other one has gone missing, so you read only the ones still standing.

A college officer accused of being lazy, conceited and never at his desk.

'Absent' reads as a third failing in the list and is half the instruction instead: it says letters go missing, and 'now and again' says how often they do it.

14 across PERSIAN CAT

Welsh girl about to be cuddled by cute pedigree pet (7,3)

Definition: pedigree pet · container + charade

A Persian cat is one long-haired pedigree breed, so 'pedigree pet' defines by example: one of the animals a cat show is full of.

  • cute → PERT pert is cute in the cheeky, saucy sense, and it opens up to take the other pieces
  • Welsh girl → SIAN Siân is the Welsh form of Jane and a common girl's name in Wales
  • about → CA ca. is short for circa, the 'about' written in front of a date nobody can pin down
  • to be cuddled by — Being cuddled by something means being wrapped up in its arms, so those letters close around whatever is named before them.

A Welsh girl being cuddled by an adorable pedigree animal.

'Cute' reads as part of the description of the animal and belongs to the wordplay instead, which leaves only two words to define with — and both of them are true of a whole family of breeds.

16 across LOUD-HAILER

Rude hallo I might produce? (4-6)

Definition: Rude hallo I might produce? · anagram + &lit

A loud-hailer is the hand-held megaphone that turns a shout into a bellow, so a rude hallo is precisely what one might produce.

  • Rude hallo I → RUDEHALLOI ten letters, taken exactly as printed before anything is done to them
  • might produce — What is produced comes out unlike the stuff it was made from, so the letters named are free to arrive in a new order.

An impolite greeting somebody is threatening to deliver.

Every word does both jobs at once: read straight, the line is the gadget boasting about the noise it can make; read as instructions, those same ten letters spell it. The question mark is the setter admitting it.

19 across FETA

Regularly – often – tea is cheese (4)

Definition: cheese · alternate letters

Feta is the white, salty Greek cheese kept in brine, so the single word names it by the family it belongs to.

  • often – tea → FETA the second, fourth, sixth and eighth letters of the two words read as one run
  • Regularly — Taken regularly means taken at a fixed interval — every second letter of what follows, and none of the ones between.

Somebody remarking that their tea is regularly nothing but cheese.

Tea across much of Britain is the evening meal rather than the drink, which is what makes a sentence about cheese sound like an ordinary complaint. The dashes are decoration.

20 across HELEN

Beauty captured by the lens (5)

Definition: Beauty · hidden word

Helen of Troy is the standard example of a beauty, the face that launched a thousand ships, so the noun names her by what she is famous for.

  • the lens → HELEN five consecutive letters running out of one word into the next
  • captured by — What is captured is held inside its captor, so the letters are prisoners of the words that follow.

A beautiful subject caught by a photographer's camera.

'Captured by the lens' is photography in the surface and imprisonment underneath, and the answer is a name doing duty as a common noun.

21 across RETROUSSE

Trousers, lost at the start, eventually turned up (9)

Definition: turned up · anagram + first letter

Retroussé describes a nose tipped up at its end, and 'turned up' is the plain English adjective for exactly that shape.

  • Trousers → TROUSERS eight letters of raw material, the garment itself playing no part
  • eventually → E the letter that word begins with
  • lost — Something lost has wandered off from where it belongs, and letters that have wandered do not come back in order.
  • at the start — The start of a word is the single letter it opens with, and that letter is all this asks for.

Trousers mislaid early on and found at last with their hems folded over.

'Turned up' is a hem in the surface and a nose in the answer, which is the French word English keeps for a nose tilted at the tip.

23 across ARTISTE

Tastier dancing from this performer? (7)

Definition: performer · anagram

An artiste is a professional entertainer — singer, dancer, circus turn — the French spelling English keeps when 'performer' wants a little glamour.

  • Tastier → TASTIER the seven letters of the fodder, exactly as printed
  • dancing — A dancer never stays in the same place for long, and neither do the letters standing beside the word.

Dancing in better taste than usual, from the entertainer responsible for it.

'This performer' points back at the fodder rather than forward at anything new: the more tasteful dancing comes out of those very letters, which is what the question mark acknowledges.

24 across NO SCORE

Duck. On reflection, 20! (2,5)

Definition: Duck · charade + reversal

Duck is cricket's word for an innings that ends without a single run, so it is precisely a nought on the scoreboard.

  • On → NO two letters taken as printed, then turned round
  • 20 → SCORE a score is twenty, as in three score years and ten
  • reflection — A reflection shows a thing the other way round, so the two letters in front of it are written back to front.

Somebody thinking again and settling on the number twenty.

'20' looks like a cross-reference to the clue numbered 20 and is the plain number instead. A duck in cricket is a batter dismissed without troubling the scorers, named after the duck's egg a nought resembles.

25 across PRODIGAL SON

NT character making drop-goals in error (8,3)

Definition: NT character · anagram

The prodigal son is the wastrel of Jesus's parable in Luke, so 'NT character' names him as one figure out of the New Testament.

  • drop-goals in → DROPGOALSIN eleven letters of raw material, the hyphen and the little word included
  • error — Something written in error has come out wrong, and these letters have come out in the wrong order.

A rugby player whose attempts at drop-goals keep going wrong.

NT is the New Testament, and the parable behind the phrase is the one about the son who spends his inheritance and comes home to a feast. 'In error' reads as the player's mistake and is the instruction.

1 down COME TO A FULL STOP

Finish one’s sentence and heed driving instructor (4,2,1,4,4)

Definition: Finish one’s sentence · double definition

A sentence ends at a full stop, and a driving instructor asks for one at a junction — the punctuation mark and the dead halt share the phrase.

  • Finish one’s sentence → the punctuation reading: the dot that every written sentence ends on
  • heed driving instructor → the traffic reading: what a learner is told to do at a junction, wheels not moving at all

Somebody being told both to finish what they are saying and to obey the man in the passenger seat.

'Full stop' is a mark of punctuation in one reading and a complete halt in the other, and the 'and' invites you to read the whole line as one sentence rather than as two separate demands.

2 down OZONE

Invigorating air in Gozo? Never! (5)

Definition: Invigorating air · hidden word

Ozone is the gas, but in everyday British use it is the sharp seaside tang people say is doing them good.

  • Gozo? Never → OZONE five letters in an unbroken run out of the island's name into the word after it, the question mark counting for nothing
  • in — The little word says the letters are sitting inside what comes next rather than being described by it.

Somebody scoffing at the suggestion that the air on a Maltese island is bracing.

Gozo is the smaller of Malta's two inhabited islands, and the indignant 'Never!' keeps you reading the line as an argument instead of as a place to look.

3 down ILL FAME

Sick notes lead to notoriety (3,4)

Definition: notoriety · charade

A house of ill fame is a brothel, and ill fame on its own is the bad reputation itself, a noun that swaps straight in for notoriety.

  • Sick → ILL sick and ill are the same complaint
  • notes → FAME fa and me are two of the sol-fa notes, sung in that order

A pile of doctor's certificates that ends up making somebody notorious.

Sol-fa hands crosswords a whole scale of tiny words — do, re, me, fa, so, la, te — and two of them run together here, which is why the plural 'notes' matters.

4 down GENESIS

Band’s origin (7)

Definition: Band · double definition

The Bible's first book gives the word its sense of a beginning, and the rock group took it as a name, so 'Band' defines by example.

  • Band → the rock group Peter Gabriel and Phil Collins fronted took this word as its name
  • origin → the plain sense: the beginning of something, the point it started from

The story of where a rock group got started.

The apostrophe-s reads as a possessive and is doing nothing of the kind — it is the equals sign between two meanings, one of them the Bible's opening book, which is why beginnings carry its name.

5 down ORCADIAN

Islander’s love of pastoral life lacks initial attractiveness (8)

Definition: Islander · charade + deletion + first letter

An Orcadian is a native of Orkney, the islands off the north coast of Scotland, so 'islander' names the person by where they come from.

  • love → O love is nothing on a tennis scoreboard, and nothing is written as a nought
  • of pastoral life → RCADIAN Arcadian means belonging to an idealised country life, and it arrives here short of its opening letter
  • attractiveness → the A that this word begins with is the letter taken away
  • lacks — What a word lacks is missing from it, so the letter named next never gets written down.
  • initial — 'Initial' picks out the letter a word starts with, here to say which single letter is the one to go.

An islander whose fondness for the simple country life does not look appealing to begin with.

Arcadia was a mountainous district of Greece that poets turned into shorthand for shepherds and simplicity, so 'pastoral life' is doing the work of a place name here.

6 down NINE WICKETS DOWN

Windies won – Kent gets caught out, when the last man is batting (4,7,4)

Definition: when the last man is batting · anagram

Nine wickets down means nine of the eleven batters are out, and the eleventh — the last man — is the one still batting.

  • Windies won – Kent → WINDIESWONKENT fourteen letters of raw material: the Windies are the West Indies cricket team and Kent an English county side
  • caught → C c is what a scorer writes for caught, beside the fielder's name
  • out — 'Out' here is not the cricketer's dismissal but the sense of out of order, so the letters beside it do not stay as printed.

A match report in which the West Indies beat Kent and a Kent player is caught.

Every word of the surface is cricket, which is where the definition hides too: a side has eleven batters, so nine of them already dismissed leaves the last man at the crease.

7 down PHILADELPHIAN

Letter to boy questionable help getting Botham as US citizen (13)

Definition: US citizen · charade + anagram

Someone from Philadelphia is a US citizen, so the definition works by example: one city's residents standing in for Americans at large.

  • Letter → PHI phi is a letter of the Greek alphabet, the one that gives us physics and philosophy
  • boy → LAD a lad is a boy
  • help → ELPH the same four letters in another order
  • Botham → IAN Ian Botham, England's great cricket all-rounder, hands over his first name
  • questionable — Something questionable is not to be taken as it stands, and neither is the printed order of the four letters beside it.

Dubious assistance with a letter to a boy, somehow involving a famous cricketer.

Greek letters are the crossword's spare alphabet — phi, chi, psi, eta — so 'Letter' asks for one of those rather than for something posted, and a surname alone is enough to summon a forename.

8 down WORSE THAN EVER

It’s deteriorated a lot, sadly wasn’t over here (5,4,4)

Definition: It’s deteriorated a lot · anagram

The phrase is the superlative complaint — the state of a thing that has now declined further than at any point before.

  • wasn’t over here → WASNTOVERHERE thirteen letters of raw material, the apostrophe ignored
  • sadly — Read as 'in a sorry state' rather than as the speaker's regret, and letters in a sorry state have fallen out of the order they were given in.

A regretful report that something has gone downhill and was not on this side of the water.

The comma does the hiding: read straight on past it and 'sadly' looks like the speaker's regret rather than the instruction governing the three words after it.

15 down THANKS TO

F. Gump actor cuts toe, because of … ? (6,2)

Definition: because of · charade + deletion

Thanks to something is because of it — the phrase works for a cause of good fortune and, just as often, ironically for the cause of a mishap.

  • F. Gump actor → THANKS Tom Hanks played Forrest Gump, and the forename is cut back to its initial in front of the surname
  • toe → TO the same knife takes the last letter off the toe
  • cuts — To cut a word is to shorten it, and this one does the job twice over, on the name in front of it and on the word after.

Somebody explaining how the star of Forrest Gump came to cut his toe, then trailing off.

'Cuts toe' reads as an accident in the surface and is a pair of instructions underneath. The trailing dots leave the sentence hanging, which is how a definition that normally opens a phrase can sit at the end of one.

17 down ISRAELI

Sharon, say, is PM, losing head (7)

Definition: Sharon, say · deletion

Ariel Sharon led Israel as its prime minister, so he is one instance of the nationality the answer names, which is what 'say' marks.

  • PM → DISRAELI Benjamin Disraeli was Queen Victoria's prime minister, so two letters call up his whole name
  • losing head → the D that name begins with is the letter dropped
  • losing head — The head of a word is the letter at its front, and losing it means starting one letter further in.

A prime minister called Sharon losing her head over something.

Sharon reads as an English woman's first name and is Ariel Sharon, who led Israel — 'say' flagging him as an example. A second prime minister is hiding behind the two letters PM.

18 down ESTONIA

Land collapsing into sea (7)

Definition: Land · anagram

Estonia is a country on the Baltic, and 'land' is the plainest word there is for a country, defining it by its category.

  • into sea → INTOSEA seven letters, the two little words taken together as raw material
  • collapsing — Anything that collapses falls in on itself and keeps none of its shape, and neither do the letters after it.

A stretch of coastline crumbling into the water.

'Into sea' reads as where the falling land is going and is the raw material instead, which leaves exactly one word to do the defining.

22 down OASIS

Orchids, originally unaltered, an ikebana requirement? (5)

Definition: an ikebana requirement · charade + first letter

Oasis is the trade name of the water-holding foam florists push stems into, so an arrangement needs one — the question mark flags the brand.

  • Orchids → O the letter the flower's name starts with
  • unaltered → ASIS something left unaltered is left as is, and those two words run together
  • originally — The origin of a word is the letter it begins with, and that opening letter is all it hands over.

Orchids kept exactly as they came, and what a Japanese flower arrangement needs.

Ikebana is the Japanese art of flower arranging, and what it needs is the florist's block of green foam that holds stems where they are put — not the desert waterhole the orchids are steering you towards.
