9 across LAUGH

Something funny from US city that’s disgusting (5)

Definition: Something funny · charade

A laugh is a thing that amuses — 'he's a good laugh' — so 'Something funny' names it as a noun, not as the act of laughing.

  • US city → LA the two letters everybody writes for Los Angeles
  • that’s disgusting → UGH the noise of revulsion, spelled the way the comics spell it

A joke out of an American city that turns your stomach.

'That's disgusting' reads as somebody's verdict on the city, and it is a noise rather than a judgement — written down exactly as it sounds.

10 across AMPLIFIER

Music enhancer, provided one’s in more spacious surroundings (9)

Definition: Music enhancer · container + charade

An amplifier boosts a guitar or a voice until it fills the room, so 'Music enhancer' describes it by the job it does.

  • more spacious → AMPLER a room with more space in it is ampler, and this word is split to take the rest
  • provided → IF 'if' does the work of 'provided' in a sentence about conditions
  • one’s → I one is written I, the Roman numeral
  • in — The word says where the pieces named before it go: inside, not alongside.
  • surroundings — Your surroundings are everything round about you, so the last word closes over what has been named before it.

A gadget that makes the music better, so long as you have the room for it.

The comma turns 'Music enhancer' into the opening of a small ad, so everything after it reads as the seller's fine print rather than as instructions.

11 across BRIEFCASE

Holder, accountant, wears pants with elasticated top (9)

Definition: Holder · container + charade + first letter

A briefcase is the flat case that holds papers, so 'Holder' names it by what it does, one step more general than the word itself.

  • pants → BRIEFS briefs are the underwear that British English calls pants
  • accountant → CA ca is a chartered accountant, the letters that follow the name on the door
  • elasticated top → E the first letter of 'elasticated'
  • wears — Somebody wearing a garment is inside it, so the letters named before go within the word that follows.
  • top — The top of a word is the letter it begins with, and only that one letter is taken.

An accountant getting dressed in underwear with a stretchy waistband.

'Holder' stands in front of a comma looking like one more job title to go beside 'accountant', which is how the definition hides inside a list of people.

12 across SET UP

Was an establishment type wrongly implicated? (3,2)

Definition: Was an establishment type · double definition

If you set a firm up you established it, so you were an establishment type; and to be set up is to be framed for something.

  • Was an establishment type → to establish a business is to get it going, and whoever did that was the establishing sort
  • wrongly implicated → framed — made to look guilty of something arranged by somebody else

A pillar of the establishment asking whether he has been framed.

The question mark is doing real work here, flagging the first half as playful: the same two words cover establishing a business and being fitted up for a crime.

13 across CENSURE

Start to neuter rescue strays and put down (7)

Definition: put down · anagram + first letter

To censure somebody is to condemn them formally, and 'put down' is the informal verb for the same public telling-off.

  • Start to neuter → N the first letter of 'neuter'
  • rescue → RESCUE anagram fodder, six letters
  • Start to — The start of a word is its opening letter, and that single letter is all this takes from it.
  • strays — A stray has wandered off from where it belongs, and the letters beside the word wander out of order in the same way.

Neutering the rescue centre's strays, and putting some of the animals down.

'Put down' is placed where it reads as the vet's needle at the end of a sad afternoon, and the sense wanted is the dressing-down a boss hands out.

15 across STAND UP

Do a ‘homo erectus’ and wash? (5,2)

Definition: Do a ‘homo erectus’ · double definition

Homo erectus is upright man, so doing one is getting to your feet; and an excuse that washes is one that stands up to scrutiny.

  • Do a ‘homo erectus’ → homo erectus is Latin for upright man, the ancestor who got up on two legs
  • wash → an excuse that washes is one that survives being looked at closely

Doing an impression of an early human, then having a wash.

'Wash' looks like the second half of a morning routine and means to be believed: a story that won't wash is one that won't hold up in court.

17 across MY EYE

Not some other viewer’s rubbish (2,3)

Definition: rubbish · charade

'My eye' is an old-fashioned exclamation meaning nonsense, so 'rubbish' is the modern noun for exactly that dismissal.

  • Not some other → MY if a thing is not somebody else's, it is mine
  • viewer → EYE the viewer meant is the organ that does the viewing

Somebody dismissing another television watcher's opinion as rubbish.

'My eye!' is the Victorian cry for nonsense — all my eye and Betty Martin — and the clue buries it in a squabble about what is on television.

18 across GAG

Sally’s restraint (3)

Definition: Sally · double definition

A gag is a joke, which is what a sally is when somebody fires off a witticism; and a gag is also the cloth that stops a mouth.

  • Sally → a sally is a witty remark launched into a conversation
  • restraint → the strip of cloth tied over a captive's mouth

A woman called Sally showing admirable restraint.

The capital letter turns 'Sally' into a woman's name and her apostrophe-s into a possessive, when the s is only the verb 'is' joining two definitions.

20 across INTRO

Played by band just starting to almost ring out (5)

Definition: Played by band just starting · anagram + deletion

The intro is the opening passage a band plays before the song proper gets going, so the definition names it by when you hear it.

  • to → TO the word itself, handing over two letters of the fodder
  • almost ring → RIN 'ring' with its last letter left off
  • almost — Almost a whole word means all of it bar the end, so the last letter never arrives.
  • out — A shoulder that is out is out of its proper place, and the letters go the same way.

A band striking up, the sound not quite filling the hall.

'Out' sits at the end of the clue where it reads as part of 'ring out', and it is the shuffling instruction; the definition runs all the way to 'starting'.

22 across CABARET

Nude in rude act? (7)

Definition: Nude in rude act? · &lit + container + anagram

A cabaret is a floor show of songs and turns, often risqué, so the clue read straight through is a fair description of one.

  • act → CAT the three letters of 'act' in another order, then opened up to take more
  • Nude → BARE nude and bare are the same idea: nothing on
  • in — The word puts one piece inside another rather than beside it.
  • rude — Rude also means rough and unfinished — a rude hut, a rude sketch — and letters left rough do not keep their order.

A nude performer in a risqué stage act.

Read straight, the four words describe the sort of show the answer names; read as instructions, the same four words build it — which is what the question mark is admitting.

25 across OWN GOAL

In town? Go alone – the other side will appreciate it (3,4)

Definition: the other side will appreciate it · hidden word

An own goal is one you put into your own net, so the benefit goes to the team you are playing against — hence their appreciation.

  • town? Go alone → OWNGOAL the letters run in order across the three words, starting one letter into the first
  • In — The first word says the letters are already in what follows, running on through the words without a break.

Somebody asked whether they are in town, and told to go on their own.

The question mark after 'town' breaks the phrase up on the page at exactly the point where the letters run straight on, and the other side is the opposition.

26 across PUNCH

Hit the drink (5)

Definition: Hit · double definition

A punch is a blow with the fist, and punch is the spiced fruit drink served from a bowl — two unrelated senses of the one word.

  • Hit → the boxer's blow, thrown with a closed fist
  • the drink → the bowl of fruit and spirits ladled out at a party

Ditching in the sea, or starting on the booze.

'Hit the drink' reads as one idiom, and there is nothing joining the two halves at all: the clue is two plain definitions laid end to end.

27 across MUSIC HALL

Entertainment from such activity around one in shopping centre (5,4)

Definition: Entertainment · container + anagram

A music hall was the Victorian variety theatre of songs, comic turns and singalongs, so 'Entertainment' names it by what was sold there.

  • shopping centre → MALL the shopping centre is the mall, and it splits open to take the rest
  • such → USCH the four letters of 'such' in another order
  • one → I one is written I, the Roman numeral
  • activity — Letters in a state of activity are letters on the move, not sitting in the order they were handed over.
  • around — What goes around a thing encloses it, so the shuffled letters close over the piece named next.
  • in — The word drops everything assembled so far inside the word that follows.

The sort of goings-on that happen all around you in a shopping centre.

'Such' points back at nothing in particular, which is what makes it so hard to see as four loose letters rather than as a word describing the activity.

30 across APENNINES

Mountains figure in Aspen resort (9)

Definition: Mountains · container + anagram

The Apennines are the mountain chain running the whole length of Italy, so 'Mountains' names them by their kind, plural as the range is.

  • Aspen → APENS the five letters of the ski town in another order, then split open
  • figure → NINE a figure is a single digit, and this one is spelled out in letters
  • in — The figure named before it ends up inside the letters that follow, not beside them.
  • resort — Read as re-sort, the word is an instruction to sort the letters again into a different order.

A well-known figure holidaying at the Aspen ski resort.

'Resort' completes the picture of Aspen as a ski destination while quietly telling you to sort the letters again, and 'figure' is a digit rather than a celebrity.

31 across CLOWN

Sovereign changes sides – fool! (5)

Definition: fool · substitution

A clown is the circus fool in the white face and big shoes, and 'fool' is what anyone behaving like one gets called.

  • Sovereign → CROWN the sovereign is the crown, the monarchy spoken of as an institution
  • changes sides — In crosswords right is R and left is L, so changing sides swaps the one letter for the other.

A monarch switching political allegiance and being called a fool for it.

The dash sets 'fool!' up as the insult somebody shouts at a turncoat, which is what stops it reading as the definition it actually is.

1 down CLUB

Iron a suit – not hem (4)

Definition: Iron · deletion

An iron is one of the clubs in a golf bag, so the definition names the whole category by pointing at a single example of it.

  • a suit → CLUBS one of the four suits in a pack of cards, written out in full before the trim
  • not hem — A hem is the bottom edge of a garment, and in a down entry the bottom is the last square, so the final letter is the one left off.

Ironing a suit and leaving the hem alone.

'Iron' opens the clue looking like the verb an ironing board wants, and it is a noun: golfers carry irons and woods, and both of them are clubs.

2 down AUDIENCE

Poet outside independent church house (8)

Definition: house · container + charade

The house in a theatre is the people out front — playing to a full house means a full audience, not a full building.

  • Poet → AUDEN W H Auden, the twentieth-century poet, five letters ready to be opened up
  • independent → I i is the standard short form of independent, as on a ballot paper
  • church → CE ce is the Church of England, the crossword's usual church
  • outside — A thing standing outside another has that other within it, so the name in front closes round the letter named next.

A poet loitering outside a house belonging to an independent church.

'Independent church house' reads as one address, three words describing a single building, and each of the three is a separate instruction.

3 down CHEF

Cook big cheese after centre removed (4)

Definition: Cook · deletion

A chef is the trained cook who runs a kitchen, so 'Cook' is the same job named one rank more broadly.

  • big cheese → CHIEF the big cheese is the boss, the one in charge of the outfit
  • centre removed — The centre of a five-letter word is its third letter, here an I, and 'removed' takes it away rather than adding anything.

A cook taking over once the person in charge has been got rid of.

'Big cheese' is American slang from the 1920s for the person in charge, and it is parked next to 'Cook' so that it reads as something on the menu.

4 down LAY AN EGG

Utterly fail to produce a breakfast item? (3,2,3)

Definition: Utterly fail · double definition

To lay an egg is American stage slang for flopping badly, and a hen laying one produces what ends up in the frying pan.

  • Utterly fail → stage slang from America: a routine that dies on its feet and raises no laugh at all
  • produce a breakfast item? → what a hen does, and the question mark admits that breakfast is only one thing to do with it

A cook failing completely at the simple job of getting breakfast on the table.

Comedians borrowed the henhouse for their worst nights, and the small word 'to' welds the two senses into one sentence about a kitchen disaster.

5 down SPIELS

Piles rocks on top of soil to form pitches (6)

Definition: pitches · anagram + first letter

A spiel is the fast talk of somebody selling you something, which is what a pitch is in the sales sense — both plural here.

  • Piles → SPIEL the five letters of 'piles' in a fresh order
  • soil → S the first letter of 'soil'
  • rocks — A boat that rocks throws its cargo about, and letters beside it will not stay in the order they were handed over.
  • on top of — In a down entry whatever sits on top of a thing is written above it, and the top of a word is the letter it begins with.

Stones heaped over the earth to build up a couple of playing fields.

'Pitches' sits where it reads as the cricket square being built out of rocks and soil, and the sense wanted is a salesman's practised patter.

6 down DISSUASION

Onus is said to melt caution (10)

Definition: caution · anagram

To caution somebody is to warn them off a course of action, and dissuasion is the noun for that talking-out-of.

  • Onus is said → ONUSISSAID anagram fodder, ten letters
  • to melt — Melting a thing destroys whatever shape it was holding, and the letters run together into a different one.

Being handed the responsibility is supposed to melt away somebody's carefulness.

'Is said' sits there wearing the standard costume of an instruction to listen for a sound, and both words are simply fodder — the shuffling is done by 'melt'.

7 down JILTED

Jack’s partner getting end away with Bill’s partner, so dropped (6)

Definition: dropped · charade + deletion

To be jilted is to be dropped by somebody who breaks off the romance, so 'dropped' is the everyday word for that same desertion.

  • Jack’s partner → JIL Jack and Jill went up the hill, and her name turns up here minus its last letter
  • Bill’s partner → TED Bill and Ted, the pair of the Excellent Adventure films
  • getting end away — The end of a word is its last letter, and 'away' sends that letter off, so the name arrives one short.

Jack's girlfriend slipping off with Bill's mate.

'Getting your end away' is British slang for a night that ends in bed, which is why the phrase reads as part of the affair rather than as an instruction to lose a letter.

8 down PROP

Support for piano (4)

Definition: Support · charade

A prop is the post or wedge that holds a thing up, so 'Support' is the same noun in less physical dress.

  • for → PRO to be pro something is to be for it, as in the pros and cons
  • piano → P p is the musician's mark for piano, meaning play it softly

Something holding a piano up.

'For' looks like plain joinery between a support and the thing it supports, and it is the first piece of the build.

13 down COMIC

Taking the ---s out of universal wit (5)

Definition: wit · deletion

A comic is a professional wit, somebody who makes a living being funny, so 'wit' names the person rather than the quality.

  • universal → COSMIC cosmic means universal, belonging to the whole cosmos rather than one corner of it
  • Taking the ---s out of — The setter has blanked out a rude phrase and left only its final s showing, and that printed letter is the one to remove.

Somebody mocking a wit whom the whole world admires.

The dashes hide the rude idiom for mocking somebody, and the setter prints the s because the s is also the letter you take out of COSMIC.

14 down UNEARTHING

‘Turning up a hunter’s pants’ in German (10)

Definition: Turning up · anagram + charade

To unearth a thing is to dig it up, and 'turning up' is what you say when the digging finally produces it.

  • a hunter’s → UNEARTH the seven letters of 'a hunter' in a fresh order
  • in → IN the word is taken at face value, two letters straight off the page
  • German → G g is the dictionary's short form for German
  • pants — British slang for rubbish, and letters called rubbish are letters not standing in the order they ought to be.

A phrasebook rendering a complaint about a hunter's trousers into German.

The quotation marks make the first half look like a phrase waiting to be translated, so 'in German' reads as the instruction when it is two more pieces of the build.

16 down PROWL

Discontented, poor, solemn type – creep! (5)

Definition: creep · charade + outer letters

To prowl is to move about softly on the lookout for something, which is 'creep' used as a verb — the burglar's way of crossing a floor.

  • poor → PR the outer letters of 'poor', with everything between them emptied out
  • solemn type → OWL the owl is the bird proverbially grave and wise
  • Discontented — Something discontented has had its contents taken out, so only the letters at the two ends of the word survive.

A glum, po-faced sort being called a creep.

'Creep!' arrives after a dash, dressed as the insult thrown at the solemn type, and it is the verb for moving quietly across a room.

19 down GROSSEST

Go mad with stress – most off-putting (8)

Definition: most off-putting · anagram

Something gross is disgusting enough to put you off, so the superlative 'most off-putting' is the same idea at full strength.

  • Go → GO anagram fodder, two letters
  • stress → STRESS anagram fodder, six letters
  • mad — Somebody driven mad has lost all order in their head, and so have the letters standing either side of the word.

Being told to go mad with the stress of it all.

'Go mad' reads as a single phrase about losing your temper, which is what keeps 'go' from looking like two loose letters waiting to be shuffled.

21 down TOOK A BOW

Stole from a milliner and acknowledged public approval? (4,1,3)

Definition: acknowledged public approval? · charade

To take a bow is to step forward and bend at the waist when the applause comes, which is acknowledging the crowd's approval.

  • Stole → TOOK the past tense of steal: what you stole, you took
  • from a milliner → ABOW a hat-maker's stock in trade includes ribbon bows, and one of those is what went missing

A thief helping himself to something from a hat shop.

'Stole' opens the clue as a noun, the fur wrap a milliner might well sell, and it is the past tense of the verb instead.

23 down BANTER

Ten cooks in pub making jokey remarks (6)

Definition: jokey remarks · container + anagram

Banter is the light back-and-forth teasing between friends, so it is a run of jokey remarks rather than any single one.

  • pub → BAR the pub is the bar, and it is about to be split open
  • Ten → NTE the three letters of 'ten' in a fresh order
  • cooks — To cook the books is to rearrange the figures dishonestly, and the number beside the word gets the same treatment.
  • in — The word places the shuffled letters inside the word that follows it.

Ten chefs working a pub kitchen and trading jokes over the pans.

'Ten cooks' reads as a kitchen full of staff, which hides both the number being three letters and 'cooks' being the instruction to shuffle them.

24 down TIMING

Making of a good joke? Turning it up on Dynasty (6)

Definition: Making of a good joke? · reversal + charade

Timing is what turns a line into a laugh — comedians say it is everything — so 'the making of a good joke' names it by its effect.

  • it → TI the two letters of 'it' climbing the grid, so they arrive the other way round
  • Dynasty → MING the Ming were the Chinese dynasty of the blue-and-white porcelain
  • Turning — Turning a thing round leaves it facing the other way, so the letters are written in the opposite order.
  • up — This is a down entry, so letters travelling up are read from the bottom of the grid towards the top.
  • on — In a down entry whatever sits on something else is written above it.

Turning up the volume on an episode of Dynasty.

The italics make 'Dynasty' the 1980s soap on the television, and the word wanted is the Chinese dynasty of the vases.

26 down PEAK

Top dictator’s little dog (4)

Definition: Top · homophone

The peak of a mountain is its highest point, so 'Top' is the plain noun for the same place.

  • little dog → PEAK a peke is a Pekingese, the small flat-faced lapdog
  • dictator’s — A dictator speaks words out for somebody else to write down, so what you get is the sound of the word rather than its spelling.

The little dog belonging to a leading dictator.

'Top' looks as though it belongs to 'dictator', making him the chief tyrant, and it is the definition standing on its own at the front of the clue.

28 down COCK

Bird’s tail pulled out of drink (4)

Definition: Bird · deletion

A cock is a male bird, the farmyard rooster being the familiar one, so 'Bird' defines it by the wider family it belongs to.

  • drink → COCKTAIL the mixed drink of a bar, spirits and a slice of fruit
  • tail → TAIL the four letters of the word 'tail' are the piece that gets pulled out
  • pulled out of — Pulling a piece out of a word takes those letters away for good and leaves the rest standing as they were.

A bird's tail feather fished out of somebody's drink.

The apostrophe glues 'Bird's tail' into one thing, a feather, when the definition stops after the first word and the tail belongs to the drink instead.

29 down LINE

Dash to cover the inside (4)

Definition: Dash · double definition

A dash is the printer's short stroke, which is a line on the page; and to line a coat or a pipe is to cover it on the inside.

  • Dash → the punctuation mark, a short stroke drawn on the page
  • cover the inside → what a tailor does to a jacket and a builder does to a chimney

Somebody making a dash for cover and hiding inside.

'Dash to cover' reads as a single movement, a sprint for shelter, and the two definitions actually part company in the middle of that phrase.
