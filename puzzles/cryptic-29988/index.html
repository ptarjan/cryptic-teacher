Across

1 across PESTLE

Footballer crossing road to get pound (6)

Definition: pound · container

To pestle something is to pound it in a mortar, so the tool's name doubles as the verb for the job it does.

  • Footballer → PELE Pele is the Brazilian forward a crossword reaches for whenever it wants a footballer
  • road → ST ST is the map shorthand for street, which is a road you can cross
  • crossing — One thing crossing another lies right over it, so the name in front opens up and the road's letters sit inside.

A footballer running across a road, apparently to collect some money.

'Pound' looks like the money the surface is chasing, and the sense wanted is the kitchen verb for crushing spices in a mortar.

4 across SHANKS

Poor shots from Spain’s No.1 player in Philadelphia (6)

Definition: Poor shots · charade + first letter

A shank is a golf shot struck off the hosel that squirts sideways, the classic bad one, so the plural answers to 'poor shots'.

  • Spain’s → S the letter standing at the front of 'Spain', taken by position rather than looked up
  • player in Philadelphia → HANKS Tom Hanks played the lead in the film Philadelphia
  • No.1 — 'No.1' picks out whatever stands first in the line, and for a word that means the letter it begins with.

Spain's top-ranked player mishitting the ball at a tournament in Philadelphia.

'Player in Philadelphia' wants to be a footballer or a tennis seed, and the player meant is the actor who starred in the film of that name.

9 across DOGE

Italian dignitary delivers Elon Musk’s baby? (4)

Definition: Italian dignitary · double definition

A doge headed the republics of Venice and Genoa, and the same four letters are the acronym of Musk's Department of Government Efficiency.

  • Italian dignitary → the title of the elected chief magistrate of the old Venetian republic
  • Elon Musk’s baby → the cost-cutting department he set up in Washington, and the meme coin he kept posting about

An Italian bigwig handing over Elon Musk's newborn child.

The question mark flags how loose the second half is: 'baby' means a pet project rather than a child, and the two senses are centuries apart.

10 across BELLWETHER

Ring with number for trendsetter (10)

Definition: trendsetter · charade

A bellwether is the lead sheep wearing the bell that the flock follows, so it names anything the rest take their cue from.

  • Ring → BELL a bell is the thing that rings, the noun sitting behind the verb
  • with → W W is the standard shorthand for 'with', as written on a menu or a recipe card
  • number → ETHER read as one that numbs: ether was the anaesthetic surgeons dosed patients with

An instruction to telephone somebody on a particular number.

'Number' is the trap. It is not a figure at all but a thing that makes you numb, which is how what looks like a comparative adjective turns into an anaesthetic.

11 across DIPPER

Duck followed by a bird (6)

Definition: bird · charade

The dipper is the river bird that walks under water to feed, so 'bird' is the plain category the name belongs to.

  • Duck → DIP to duck out of the way is to dip
  • a → PER 'per' means 'a' in phrases like sixty miles an hour
  • followed by — What follows comes along behind, so the letters named second are written after the letters named first.

A duck paddling along with another bird behind it.

'Duck' looks like the bird and turns out to be the verb, and the little word 'a' is material rather than grammar: twice a day is twice per day.

12 across CHEVIOTS

Pervert chose ITV2 (8)

Definition: 2 · anagram

Cheviots are the hardy hill sheep bred along the Scottish border, so the plural answers to what 2 down names.

  • chose ITV → CHOSEITV anagram fodder, eight letters
  • Pervert → anagram indicator
  • Pervert — To pervert a thing is to twist it out of its proper form, and letters twisted like that come out in a new order.

Somebody settling down in front of ITV2 for the evening.

The channel name is split down the middle: the three letters belong to the wordplay and the digit beside them is a cross-reference to 2 down.

13 across TEESWATER

Comic Stewart Lee forgetting line 2 (9)

Definition: 2 · anagram + deletion

The Teeswater is a longwool breed from the Tees valley in northern England, so it is one of the animals 2 down names.

  • Stewart Lee → STEWARTLEE the stand-up comedian named in the surface supplies the letters
  • line → L line is L in the setter's shorthand, and that L is the letter dropped
  • Comic → anagram indicator
  • Comic — Something comic is out of true and played for laughs, and letters put out of true no longer sit in the order handed over.
  • forgetting — What you forget gets left out, so the letter named next never joins the rest.

The comedian Stewart Lee cutting a line out of his stage act.

'Comic' does two jobs at once: on the surface it introduces a real stand-up, and in the wordplay it is the instruction to rearrange what follows.

15 across STEP

Backing favourites to show pace (4)

Definition: pace · reversal

A step and a pace are the same unit of walking, one movement of one foot, so either word measures out a distance.

  • favourites → PETS a favourite is a pet: a teacher's pet, a pet project
  • Backing — Backing a thing turns it round to face the other way, and in an across answer that means the letters read right to left.

Punters putting their money on the favourites to run quickly.

'Backing favourites' is one phrase at the bookmaker's, and the split falls inside it: the first word is the instruction and the second is the material.

16 across BAAL

‘Dog in the mirror’ is entertaining answer for god (4)

Definition: god · reversal + container

Baal was the Canaanite storm god denounced all through the Old Testament, so 'god' names him about as plainly as it can.

  • Dog → LAB a lab is a labrador, the retriever kept as a family dog
  • answer → A A is the standard abbreviation for answer, as in a Q and A session
  • in the mirror — A mirror shows everything the other way round, so the dog's letters go in back to front.
  • entertaining — To entertain somebody is to have them inside your house, so these letters hold another one within them.

A dog amusing itself with its own reflection.

The quotation marks make 'Dog in the mirror' look like the title of a photograph, and they are the setter's way of parcelling up an instruction.

17 across HEBRIDEAN

Ambassador’s wife getting tips from Ascension Islander (9)

Definition: Islander · charade + outer letters

A Hebridean is somebody from the Hebrides, so the answer names an islander by the particular islands they come from.

  • Ambassador’s → HE HE is His Excellency, the formal style of address for an ambassador
  • wife → BRIDE a bride is a wife on her wedding day
  • Ascension → AN the letters standing at the two tips of 'Ascension'
  • getting tips from — The tips of a word are the points at either end of it, so its first and last letters are what come away.

An ambassador's wife picking up tips from somebody who lives on Ascension Island.

'Ascension Islander' reads as one person from that South Atlantic rock, and the definition is only its second word — the island group meant is off the coast of Scotland.

21 across LAMB CHOP

Dear Charlie Dance showing a bit of flesh (4,4)

Definition: a bit of flesh · charade

A lamb chop is a cut of meat off the ribs, so 'a bit of flesh' describes it as one piece of an animal.

  • Dear → LAMB a lamb is a term of endearment: 'the poor lamb' means the poor dear
  • Charlie → C Charlie is C in the radio alphabet
  • Dance → HOP a hop is a dance, as in a village hop

The actor Charles Dance turning up in a revealing scene.

The capitals make 'Charlie Dance' look like Charles Dance, the British actor, and both words are ordinary vocabulary doing quite separate jobs.

22 across MOB LAW

Untidy Womble briefly embracing a form of anarchy (3,3)

Definition: a form of anarchy · anagram + deletion

Mob law is what obtains when a crowd enforces its own will instead of the courts, so it is one particular form anarchy takes.

  • Womble → WOMBL the Wombles are the litter-collecting creatures of a British children's series, and this one arrives a letter short
  • a → A the bare article, taken in as one more letter of the fodder
  • Untidy → anagram indicator
  • Untidy — An untidy room has everything in the wrong place, and untidy letters are out of their proper order too.
  • briefly — Said briefly, a word is cut short, so the name gives up its final letter.
  • embracing — An embrace gathers something in, so the letter named next joins the pile being shuffled.

A scruffy Womble clinging on while everything descends into anarchy.

The joke is that Wombles are famously tidy, forever clearing up other people's litter, so 'untidy' looks like a description of the creature rather than an instruction.

24 across CORRIEDALE

2 drunk cider or beer (10)

Definition: 2 · anagram

The Corriedale is a New Zealand crossbred kept for wool and meat, so it is one of the animals 2 down names.

  • cider or → CIDEROR anagram fodder, seven letters
  • beer → ALE beer is ale, and the synonym joins the fodder rather than the word itself
  • drunk → anagram indicator
  • drunk — A drunk cannot walk a straight line, and letters in that state will not stay in the order they were given.

Somebody who has had far too much cider or beer.

'Beer' is not fodder as it stands: it has to be turned into its three-letter synonym before the letters will come out right.

25 across EWES

Females take drugs orally (4)

Definition: Females · homophone

Ewes are the female sheep of a flock, so 'Females' names them by sex, the plural matching the plural.

  • take drugs → EWES to use is to take drugs, and said aloud that word is written down quite differently
  • orally — What is done orally is done by mouth, so the wordplay hands you a sound and leaves the spelling to you.

Women being told to take their medicine by mouth.

'Take drugs orally' reads as an instruction off a pill packet, and 'orally' carries none of that sense: it is the flag that a sound is wanted.

26 across SUSSEX

South American relations in part of England (6)

Definition: part of England · charade

Sussex is the county along the south coast below London, so the definition names it by example, one county out of forty-odd.

  • South → S S is the compass abbreviation for south
  • American → US US does duty for American, the adjective taken from the country's initials
  • relations → SEX relations in this sense is the polite word for it

A South American's family connections somewhere in England.

'South American' looks like one nationality and is two separate pieces, which is what keeps the next word looking like cousins and aunts.

27 across STATED

Declared dead by the entrance of Serpentine Gallery (6)

Definition: Declared · charade + first letter

To state something is to declare it, and the past tense of the one stands in for the past tense of the other.

  • Serpentine → S the letter at the front of 'Serpentine'
  • Gallery → TATE Tate is the gallery a crossword means when it says gallery
  • dead → D D is the standard abbreviation for dead
  • the entrance of — An entrance is the way in at the front, so what is taken is the letter standing at the front of the word.

A death being announced at the door of the Serpentine Gallery.

The Serpentine Gallery, in London's Kensington Gardens, is a real place, so the two words hold together on the surface while the wordplay cuts between them.

1 down PROVINE

Coat of plaster like a 2-layer (7)

Definition: layer · charade + outer letters

To provine is the gardener's verb for layering a vine, bending a shoot down into the soil until it takes root.

  • of plaster → PR the two letters on the outside of 'plaster', its first and its last
  • like a 2 → OVINE the cross-reference points at 2 down, and ovine is the adjective meaning like that animal
  • Coat — A coat is what goes round the outside of a thing, so only the letters on the outside of the word beside it are taken.

A plasterer describing the second coat going on to a wall.

The hyphen in '2-layer' does the hiding: it glues the cross-reference to the definition so that the two read as a single compound word.

2 down SHEEP

What’s up with record stock? (5)

Definition: stock · reversal + charade

Stock is livestock, the farmer's collective word for the animals kept on the land, and a flock of these is exactly that.

  • What’s → SHE 'eh?' is the noise you make for 'what?', so 'what's' is EHS, and this down clue stands it on end
  • record → EP EP is the extended-play record, a seven-inch disc with a couple of tracks a side
  • up — In a down clue 'up' means reading back up the column, so the letters named beside it are entered the other way about.

Somebody asking what has become of the shop's supply of records.

'Stock' reads as the records on the shelves, and the farming sense is the one the setter means.

3 down LIBERIA

Pianist leaving church to tour one part of Africa (7)

Definition: part of Africa · deletion + container

Liberia is a country on the west African coast, so the definition names it by example: one part of a much bigger continent.

  • Pianist → LIBERACE Liberace was the candelabra-and-sequins pianist of American television
  • church → CE CE is the Church of England, the usual two letters for church
  • one → I the Roman numeral for one, a single letter for the tour to close around
  • leaving — What leaves has gone, so the two letters named next walk out of the name they were sitting in.
  • to tour — A tour travels right round its subject, so the letters left behind go all the way around the one that follows.

A concert pianist skipping church so as to travel round part of Africa.

'Pianist leaving church' sounds like a man walking out of a service, and it is an instruction to take two letters out of his name.

5 down HAWKER

Salesman’s wife getting into fish recipe (6)

Definition: Salesman · container + charade

A hawker peddles goods from a barrow or door to door, which is a salesman working without a shop behind him.

  • fish → HAKE hake is the long white sea fish sold at the fishmonger's counter
  • wife → W W is the standing abbreviation for wife
  • recipe → R R is recipe, the Latin instruction to take, still written at the head of a prescription
  • getting into — Getting into something puts you inside it, so the single letter named first ends up within the word named next.

A salesman's wife joining in with a fish recipe in the kitchen.

The apostrophe-s is doing two jobs: on the surface it gives the wife an owner, and in the wordplay it is only the join between the definition and everything after it.

6 down NITWITTED

Foolish jester made woollen items for Spooner (9)

Definition: Foolish · spoonerism

Nitwitted is the adjective built from nitwit, so it describes exactly the person the word 'foolish' describes.

  • jester → NIT a jester is a wit, and it takes the opening sound of the other word in exchange
  • made woollen items → WITTED knitting makes woollen items, and its opening noise is an N because the K is silent
  • for Spooner — Spooner was the Oxford don remembered for swapping the opening sounds of two words, so his name asks you to do the same.

A silly court jester turning out jumpers and scarves.

'Foolish jester' reads as one description of one man, and the split falls after the first word: only that is the definition, and the jester is material.

7 down SWEPT UP

Performed chore and cried into drink (5,2)

Definition: Performed chore · container

Sweeping up is the household chore itself, so the past tense of the phrase matches 'performed chore' as a past-tense act.

  • drink → SUP to sup is to drink, and this is the word that opens up to take something in
  • cried → WEPT wept is the past tense of weeping, which is what crying is
  • into — Tears going into a glass end up inside it, so the crying is placed within the letters of the drink.

Somebody who did the housework and then wept into their drink.

'Performed chore' is deliberately vague — it could be a dozen different verbs — and the crossing letters are what tell you which household job is meant.

8 down BLACK EYED PEAS

Becks played incredibly to inspire team’s midfield for Fergie’s old outfit (5,4,4)

Definition: Fergie’s old outfit · anagram + container + middle letters

Fergie sang with the Black Eyed Peas before going solo, so 'old outfit' is the band she used to belong to.

  • Becks played → BECKSPLAYED anagram fodder, eleven letters
  • team’s midfield → EA the two letters in the middle of 'team', with the T and the M left out at the ends
  • incredibly → anagram indicator
  • incredibly — What is incredible cannot be taken as it stands, and letters you cannot take as they stand are letters to be rearranged.
  • to inspire — To inspire is literally to breathe in, and whatever is breathed in finishes up inside the thing doing the breathing.
  • midfield — A midfield is the middle of the park rather than either end, so what is taken is the middle of the word beside it.

David Beckham playing brilliantly, feeding the midfield of Alex Ferguson's old side.

Fergie on the surface is Alex Ferguson, Beckham's manager at Manchester United; the Fergie of the definition is the American singer who took the same nickname.

14 down SCABBARDS

Fencing covers problem for 2 poets (9)

Definition: Fencing covers · charade

Scabbards are the sheaths a sword slides into, so they are the covers that belong to fencing blades.

  • problem for 2 → SCAB sheep scab is the mite disease that plagues a flock, the problem the cross-reference points at
  • poets → BARDS a bard is a poet, Shakespeare being the Bard

A fence hiding some trouble that a pair of poets have got into.

'Fencing' reads as the wire round a field, which is where the surface wants you; the sport is meant instead, and 'covers' is a noun rather than a verb.

16 down BEACONS

Unfinished Monster Munches regularly account for fires (7)

Definition: fires · deletion + container + alternate letters

Beacons are the signal fires lit on hilltops to pass a warning down the country, so 'fires' names them by what they physically are.

  • Monster → BEAS a monster is a beast, arriving here with its tail lopped off
  • account → CON the second, fourth and sixth letters of 'account', which are C, O and N
  • Unfinished — Something unfinished stops short of the end, so the word for the monster arrives without its final letter.
  • Munches — Munching is eating, and what is eaten goes inside, so the letters in front close around the letters picked out next.
  • regularly — Taking letters regularly means stepping through the word at an even beat, keeping every second one and dropping the rest.

Somebody regularly polishing off packets of Monster Munch and charging them to the account.

Monster Munch is a British brand of crisps, which is what the surface is buying; taken as two ordinary words it stops being a snack and becomes a creature and a verb.

18 down RAMBERT

Male chimney sweep in dance company (7)

Definition: dance company · charade

Rambert, founded by Marie Rambert, is Britain's oldest dance company, so the definition names the institution by what it is.

  • Male → RAM a ram is a male sheep
  • chimney sweep → BERT Bert is the sweep Dick Van Dyke plays in Mary Poppins

A man who sweeps chimneys taking his place in a dance troupe.

'Male' is the whole of the wordplay's first half rather than an adjective attached to the sweep, which is what keeps the two of them looking like one man.

19 down ARANEID

Spider has distinctive pattern on behind occasionally (7)

Definition: Spider · charade + alternate letters

An araneid is any member of Araneae, the spider order, so the definition is the everyday word for the zoologist's one.

  • distinctive pattern → ARAN Aran is the cabled knitting pattern named after the islands off Galway
  • behind → EID the second, fourth and sixth letters of 'behind'
  • on — In a down clue whatever is 'on' something sits above it, so the pattern's letters are entered first, at the top of the column.
  • occasionally — Occasional letters are the ones taken now and then rather than all of them, here every second letter of the word.

A spider carrying unusual markings on its rear end.

The knitting is the piece most solvers miss: Aran jumpers are the heavy cream ones covered in raised cables, and the word is a pattern before it is anything else.

20 down CHEESE

Stuck-up date tucking into revolutionary food (6)

Definition: food · reversal + container

Cheese is food in the barest sense: the definition names the whole class the answer sits in rather than anything peculiar to it.

  • revolutionary → CHE Che Guevara is the crossword's revolutionary, and his name splits to let something in
  • date → EES to see somebody is to date them, and this down clue stands that word on end
  • Stuck-up — Stuck up means pinned upwards, and up the page is the direction a down answer's letters are read when they are turned.
  • tucking into — To tuck into a meal is to get right inside it, so these letters are placed within the ones that follow.

A snooty date tucking into the food at a restaurant.

'Stuck-up' reads as the adjective for snobbish, describing the date, and the setter is using it as a direction of travel instead.

23 down BLEAT

Club accepting the French complaint (5)

Definition: complaint · container

A bleat is a whining complaint, the noun borrowed from the sheep's cry and used for any feeble grumble.

  • Club → BAT a bat is a club you swing
  • the French → LE 'le' is French for 'the', a standard two-letter import
  • accepting — Accepting something is taking it in, so the club's letters open up to hold the two that follow.

A club taking on a grievance raised in French.

'The French' is crossword shorthand for the French word for 'the', which is LE or LA depending on which the letters want.
