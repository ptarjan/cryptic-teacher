Across

1 across COCOA

Company business comes before a drink (5)

Definition: drink · charade

Cocoa is the hot chocolate made from the powder, so 'drink' is the plainest sort of synonym: the noun for what ends up in the mug.

  • Company → CO co. is the everyday short form of company
  • business → CO a business is a company too, so co. does duty a second time
  • a → A the clue's own indefinite article, taken as a single letter
  • comes before — 'comes before' is an instruction about order: the pieces named so far are written first and what follows them goes on the end.

Work gets dealt with first and the drink is the reward waiting at the end of it.

Two different words hand over the same two letters: 'company' and 'business' are both co., and doubling a piece like that is what makes the wordplay look too thin to be right.

4 across FRIED EGGS

Reggie's finally tired of cooked breakfast? (5,4)

Definition: breakfast · anagram + last letters

Fried eggs are the defining item of a British cooked breakfast, so the definition works by example rather than by synonym.

  • Reggie's → REGGIES the name's seven letters, apostrophe and all, are the bulk of the fodder
  • tired of → DF the closing letters of these two words, in the order they stand
  • finally — 'finally' asks for last letters only, so the words after it give up nothing except the letter each one ends on.
  • cooked — 'cooked' is a change-of-state word: what comes out of the pan no longer looks like what went in, letters included.

Reggie has at last gone off the greasy breakfast he used to look forward to.

'Finally' reaches across both 'tired' and 'of', which is the step that hides: one small indicator harvesting the last letter of two separate words.

9 across COUCH POTATO

Lazybones put across rubbish business cases (5,6)

Definition: Lazybones · charade + container

A couch potato is someone who lies about watching television, which is what 'lazybones' names — an idler, said with the same affection.

  • put across → COUCH to couch something is to put it across in words of a particular kind
  • business → POO business is the nursery euphemism, as in doing one's business, and it opens up to take something in
  • rubbish → TAT tat is cheap rubbishy stuff
  • cases — 'cases' is a verb here: to case something is to enclose it, so the piece it names shuts around another.

The idler of the office is presenting weak business cases and convincing nobody.

'Business cases' looks like the phrase the surface needs, and it is two separate instructions: a euphemism for what the dog left, and a verb meaning to enclose.

10 across EON

Heroin taken regularly for a long time (3)

Definition: a long time · alternate letters

An eon is an immense stretch of time, the geologist's unit, so 'a long time' is the everyday phrase for that noun.

  • Heroin → EON the second, fourth and sixth letters of the word, read in order
  • taken regularly — 'taken regularly' means at a fixed interval, and the interval here is every second letter rather than every letter.

Someone has been taking the drug steadily for years on end.

Alternate letters usually start at the first one; the count here begins at the second, which is why the source word looks two letters too long to fit.

11 across ULCERATE

Rotten clues mostly deserve to fester? (8)

Definition: fester · anagram + deletion + charade

To ulcerate is to break down into an open sore that will not heal, and 'fester' is the everyday verb for the same suppuration.

  • clues → CLUE the plural loses its final letter before anything is shuffled
  • deserve → RATE to rate a mention is to deserve one
  • Rotten — 'Rotten' describes something decayed and out of shape, and letters in a rotten word no longer sit where they started.
  • mostly — 'mostly' means take most of a word — all of it except the letter it ends on.

Bad clues deserve to rot away, which is a setter making a joke at his own expense.

'Rotten' looks like a verdict on the clues and is the instruction instead, and Coot is happy to let the insult land on his own puzzle.

12 across BLOTCH

Irregular discoloration left within clumsy work (6)

Definition: Irregular discoloration · container

A blotch is a patch of colour that has spread unevenly across skin or fabric, which is irregular discoloration in dictionary terms.

  • clumsy work → BOTCH a botch is a clumsily made job
  • left → L L is the standard short form of left
  • within — 'within' puts the letter it follows inside the word that comes next, dropping it into the middle rather than onto an end.

A patch of uneven colour is what the careless workmanship has left behind.

'Left' is a letter, not the past tense the sentence wants — the surface reads it as what the work left behind, and that is where the parse stalls.

14 across MINUTES

Stokes close to speechless defending home record (7)

Definition: record · container + charade

The minutes are the written record of what was said and decided at a meeting, kept by the secretary.

  • speechless → MUTE someone mute has no speech
  • home → IN to be in is to be at home
  • Stokes → S the stokes, the unit of kinematic viscosity, is written S
  • close to — 'close to' is a placement instruction: the letter it names is written up against the rest of the assembly, not inside it.
  • defending — 'defending' is a surrounding word — whatever defends something stands all round the outside of it.

A cricketer, nearly lost for words, is defending his side's unbeaten home record.

'Stokes' is the surface's England all-rounder and the wordplay's unit of viscosity, named after the physicist George Stokes — one letter hiding behind a famous name.

16 across JOAN

Sir Elton abandons husband in favour of American woman (4)

Definition: woman · substitution

Joan is a woman's name, so the definition works by example: a given name is defined by the category it belongs to.

  • Sir Elton → JOHN Sir Elton John, the knighted British singer, supplies his surname
  • husband → H H is the standard short form of husband, and it is the letter being dropped
  • American → A A is the standard short form of American, and it is the letter moving in
  • abandons — 'abandons' is the losing half of the swap: the letter named next is dropped and does not appear.
  • in favour of — 'in favour of' names the replacement — the thing preferred takes the seat that has just been emptied.

Elton drops his husband and takes up with an American woman instead.

Sir Elton has a husband in real life, which is what gives the surface its sting; the wordplay only ever wanted his surname.

20 across AUNT

Out of hospital, frequently visit relative (4)

Definition: relative · deletion

An aunt is a relative by blood or by marriage, so the definition is the plain category word — one relative, singular as the answer is.

  • frequently visit → HAUNT to haunt a place is to visit it again and again
  • hospital → H H is the standard short form of hospital, as on a British road sign, and it is the letter taken out
  • Out of — 'Out of' is the removal instruction: the letter it names comes out of the longer word rather than being added to it.

Now that you are out of hospital you can call on your relative as often as you like.

'Out of hospital' reads as being discharged, and that is the whole disguise: the same three words are an instruction to remove a single letter.

21 across SQUEEZE

Extort from girlfriend? (7)

Definition: Extort from · double definition

To squeeze someone is to extort money from them; and your squeeze is the person you are going out with, which the question mark marks as informal.

  • Extort from → the sense of putting the pressure on somebody until the money comes out
  • girlfriend? → the informal noun for the person somebody is going out with, which the question mark marks as slang

Somebody is putting the screws on the person they are seeing.

The slang sense is 1920s American and survives mainly in 'main squeeze', while the extortion sense is the same physical image put to work on a wallet.

24 across ELEVEN

Team taking part in Whitechapel event (6)

Definition: Team · hidden word

A British cricket or football side is called an eleven, after the number of players, so 'team' is that everyday sporting sense.

  • Whitechapel event → ELEVEN the letters run consecutively over the gap between the two words
  • taking part in — 'taking part in' asks for a part of the words that follow: the letters are inside them, not clued separately.

A team is entered for a sports day being held in Whitechapel.

Whitechapel is the East End district, and it is here purely to supply letters — no part of the answer has anything to do with the place.

26 across CROSS SEA

Vexed witness reported dangerous swimming conditions (5,3)

Definition: dangerous swimming conditions · charade + homophone

A cross sea is water where two wave systems meet at an angle, throwing up unpredictable surf that drowns strong swimmers.

  • Vexed → CROSS cross is the everyday word for annoyed
  • witness → SEA to witness something is to see it, and that word has a homophone spelt quite differently
  • reported — 'reported' means passed on by mouth, so the second piece is spelt the way you hear it rather than the way its own word is written.

An irritated onlooker tells the lifeguards that the water is not safe to swim in.

'Vexed witness' reads as one angry person and it is two separate pieces, the second of them heard rather than read.

29 across IRE

Broadcast exposed anger (3)

Definition: anger · deletion

Ire is the formal, faintly literary word for anger, kept alive mostly by headline writers and crosswords.

  • Broadcast → AIRED a programme that was broadcast was aired
  • exposed → the A and the D at the two ends are stripped off, one from each
  • exposed — Something exposed has lost its covering, so the letters at each end come away and the middle is left showing.

A television programme has laid somebody's fury bare.

'Exposed' looks like part of the news story and is the instruction instead: a word stripped of its covering keeps only what was inside it.

30 across TRAILER TENT

River borders dilapidated retail and holiday accommodation (7,4)

Definition: holiday accommodation · anagram + container

A trailer tent is the folding canvas unit towed behind a car and pitched at a campsite, which is holiday accommodation of the cheapest sort.

  • River → TRENT the Trent is one of England's big rivers, and it does not stay in one piece
  • retail → RETAIL six letters of fodder, shuffled and dropped between the river's two halves
  • borders — 'borders' is a verb here: to border something is to lie along its edges, so the river's letters sit at both ends of the rest.
  • dilapidated — 'dilapidated' describes something that has fallen apart, and a fallen-apart word is put back together in a new order.

The river runs past a row of shabby shops and somewhere for visitors to stay.

The Trent is a setter's first choice when five river letters are wanted, and 'borders' is the word that breaks it in two instead of leaving it whole.

31 across GO TO PRESS

Republican with ego stops mingling to progress publication (2,2,5)

Definition: to progress publication · anagram

To go to press is to send an issue off for printing, the step that moves a publication from copy to printed pages.

  • Republican → R R is the standard short form of Republican in American politics
  • ego stops → EGOSTOPS eight letters of fodder, joined by the R to make nine
  • mingling — 'mingling' is guests mixing at a party, and letters told to mingle end up standing in new company.

A politician too full of himself has stopped mixing with the guests.

'Stops' looks like the verb the sentence needs and is fodder instead, which is what keeps the anagram out of sight until you count the letters.

32 across SUPER

Marvellous old queen in pursuit of drink (5)

Definition: Marvellous · charade

Super is the everyday British intensifier for marvellous, the adjective you use about something that has thoroughly pleased you.

  • drink → SUP to sup is to drink, in small mouthfuls
  • old queen → ER ER is Elizabeth Regina, the queen on the pillar boxes
  • in pursuit of — 'in pursuit of' means chasing along behind, so the piece doing the chasing is written after the one it chases.

An elderly queen is on the hunt for something to drink.

ER is the crossword's standard queen, and 'old' now does real work: there has been a king since 2022, so the letters belong to the late Elizabeth.

1 down COCK UP

Make a mess of power grab acquiring fashion brand (4,2)

Definition: Make a mess of · container

To cock something up is British slang for botching it, so the phrasal verb answers 'make a mess of' exactly, tense for tense.

  • power grab → COUP a coup is a seizure of power
  • fashion brand → CK CK is Calvin Klein, the label's two initials as stamped on the clothing
  • acquiring — 'acquiring' is a taking-in word: whatever acquires something wraps round it and holds it inside rather than beside it.

A grab for power goes wrong, and the plotters end up buying a designer label.

A designer's initials are standard crossword currency, and the surface never admits that clothing is involved — 'fashion brand' looks like the thing being seized.

2 down CHURCHMAN

Devoted Christian's two chapters about ancient city soldier ... (9)

Definition: Devoted Christian · container + charade

A churchman is a clergyman, or any committed member of the church, which is what 'devoted Christian' names — singular noun for singular noun.

  • two chapters → CHCH chapter is abbreviated ch., and the clue asks for a pair of them
  • ancient city → UR Ur was the Sumerian city of the Old Testament, and the crossword's usual ancient city
  • soldier → MAN a man is a soldier of the ranks, as in officers and men
  • about — 'about' puts one piece around another: to be about something is to be all round the outside of it.

A devout author's two chapters describe a soldier from an ancient city.

The trailing dots hand the sentence on to 3 down and carry no letters with them: the two clues share a picture, not a mechanism.

3 down ADHERENT

... partially mislead her enthusiastic follower (8)

Definition: follower · hidden word

An adherent is someone who sticks to a leader or a cause, so 'follower' is a direct synonym — same singular noun, same sense of allegiance.

  • mislead her enthusiastic → ADHERENT the letters run consecutively across the three words, beginning inside the first of them
  • partially — 'partially' says only part of the words is wanted, which is the standard flag for a run of letters lying inside them.

An enthusiastic follower is being partly misled by the person she looks up to.

The dots carry the sentence over from 2 down, which is why the eye reads 'mislead her' as ordinary phrasing and never stops there to count letters.

4 down FRONT

Basic for baptism that rector's in prominent position (5)

Definition: prominent position · container

The front is the prominent position — the head of a queue, a procession or a stage, the place where you are most visible.

  • Basic for baptism → FONT the font is the stone basin a church keeps its baptismal water in
  • rector → R R is the standard short form of rector on a parish noticeboard
  • in — 'in' is the plain container word: the rector is put inside the other piece rather than added onto the end of it.

The essentials of a christening, with the rector standing where the whole congregation can see him.

'Basic for baptism' is a church fitting rather than anything doctrinal, and a solver hunting for water or faith loses time before the stone basin arrives.

5 down IPAD

Tablet dad found amongst papers (4)

Definition: Tablet · container

An iPad is the best-known tablet computer, so the definition names the category and the answer supplies the brand that stands for it.

  • papers → ID your papers are your identity documents — ID
  • dad → PA pa is the familiar word for a father
  • found amongst — 'found amongst' is a container instruction: one piece turns up in the middle of another instead of next to it.

Dad has been found somewhere in a heap of paperwork.

'Tablet' is the gadget, not the pill or the stone tablet of the commandments — Coot leans on the newest of the word's three senses while the surface stays in the filing cabinet.

6 down DOODLE

Over for a day, walk in the park and draw (nothing in particular) (6)

Definition: draw (nothing in particular) · substitution

To doodle is to draw aimlessly, and 'nothing in particular' is the setter spelling out that the drawing has no subject.

  • walk in the park → DODDLE a doddle is something that takes no effort at all
  • Over → O O is the over on a cricket scorecard, and it is the letter moving in
  • a day → D d is the standard short form of day, and it is the letter giving up its place
  • for — 'for' is the swap word here: one letter is written where the other used to be, rather than joined onto it.

A visitor over for the day takes a stroll through the park and sketches whatever is in front of them.

'Walk in the park' is the idiom for something easy rather than an actual stroll, which is exactly what the surface's park is there to hide.

7 down GREAT

Remarkable rasping sound heard (5)

Definition: Remarkable · homophone

Great means remarkable in the everyday sense of standing out — a great performance is one worth remarking on.

  • rasping → GREAT to rasp is to grate, and said aloud that word has a second spelling
  • sound heard — A 'sound heard' points at pronunciation rather than spelling, so you write the version that fits the grid, not the one the clue's word uses.

Somebody has picked up a harsh scraping noise and thinks it is impressive.

Two words spelled quite differently share one pronunciation, and the clue gives you the harsh one to lead you away from the flattering one.

8 down SUNSHINE

Eric and Ernie wanted this star to excel (8)

Definition: Eric and Ernie wanted this · charade

'Bring Me Sunshine' was Morecambe and Wise's signing-off song, so what Eric and Ernie wanted is the weather named in its title.

  • star → SUN the sun is the star our own sky has
  • to excel → SHINE to shine at something is to excel at it

Eric and Ernie are hoping the celebrity booked for the show will be brilliant on the night.

Eric Morecambe and Ernie Wise were Britain's biggest television double act, and they closed every programme singing the request the definition quotes.

13 down ESAU

Some recalled you as extremely hairy man (4)

Definition: hairy man · hidden word + reversal

Esau, Isaac's elder son, is described in Genesis as red and hairy all over, so the definition identifies him by his one famous feature.

  • you as extremely → ESAU the letters run backwards across the word breaks, starting inside the last of the three
  • Some — 'Some' asks for a part of the words that follow rather than all of them, the usual flag for letters already lying in the clue.
  • recalled — 'recalled' means called back, so the run of letters is read in the opposite direction to the way it is printed.

Old acquaintances remember you as having been extraordinarily hairy.

Esau in Genesis was born covered in hair, which is how his brother Jacob was able to fool their blind father with a goatskin on his arms.

15 down TOSH

Getting sides confused, presenter is rubbish (4)

Definition: rubbish · anagram

Tosh is British slang for nonsense, the sense of 'rubbish' that means talk not worth listening to rather than refuse.

  • presenter → HOST the host is the one presenting the show
  • Getting sides confused — The 'sides' are the outer letters and 'confused' muddles them, so the two ends of the word change places.

The commentator keeps mixing up the two teams and is useless at his job.

'Sides' means teams in the surface and outer letters in the wordplay, and only those two letters move — everything between them stays put.

17 down OVERSLEEP

See lover agitated - terminal to relationship to miss alarm? (9)

Definition: to miss alarm? · anagram + last letter

To oversleep is to sleep past the hour you meant to wake, which is what missing your alarm comes to — the question mark flags the loose phrasing.

  • See lover → SEELOVER eight letters of fodder, both words taken whole
  • relationship → P the letter this word ends on
  • agitated — Something agitated is shaken about, and letters shaken about do not come to rest where they began.
  • terminal to — 'terminal' names the end of a thing, so only the last letter of the word after it is taken.

An agitated lover, at the end of the affair, is in no state to hear the morning alarm.

The dash makes 'terminal to relationship' read as the end of the affair, which is the surface talking while the words do plain letter-picking underneath.

18 down CATERING

Cringe at awful food at function (8)

Definition: food at function · anagram

Catering is the trade of supplying food for an event, so the definition describes the job rather than offering a one-word synonym.

  • Cringe at → CRINGEAT eight letters of fodder, exactly the length of the entry
  • awful — 'awful' is a spoiling word: food called awful has been turned into something other than what it should have been, and so have the letters.

The buffet at the reception is bad enough to make the guests wince.

'At' appears twice and only the first one belongs to the fodder; the eye slides between them, which is what hides where the definition starts.

19 down DESSERTS

Turned up anxious for culinary courses (8)

Definition: culinary courses · reversal

Desserts are the sweet courses at the end of a meal, matching the plural definition — the answer names the category those courses fall into.

  • anxious → DESSERTS anxious gives STRESSED, and this entry takes those letters climbing back up the grid
  • Turned up — 'Turned up' is a reversal on a down entry: the letters are read from the bottom of the column upwards.

A nervous guest arrives at dinner with only the pudding on their mind.

STRESSED reversed is the neatest reversal in English, and 'turned up' pretends to mean arrived rather than telling you which way to read.

22 down PENT-UP

Hospital department entertained by youngster kept unwillingly (4-2)

Definition: kept unwillingly · container

Pent-up describes feelings or people shut in and not let out, which is what being kept somewhere unwillingly amounts to.

  • youngster → PUP a pup is a young dog, and the word is used of other young creatures too
  • Hospital department → ENT ENT is the ear, nose and throat department of a British hospital
  • entertained by — To entertain a guest is to have them inside your house, so the piece named first goes within the piece named after it.

A small child is keeping the whole hospital department amused.

The definition sits at the end and reads as more of the hospital scene, so the join falls between 'youngster' and 'kept' rather than anywhere the sentence suggests.

23 down WAITER

He'll bring drink one assumes (6)

Definition: He'll bring · container

A waiter is the one who brings your order to the table, so 'he'll bring' defines him by the job rather than by a synonym.

  • drink → WATER water is the drink that arrives without being ordered
  • one → I the Roman numeral for one
  • assumes — 'assumes' is used in its take-on sense: the word takes the letter into itself instead of standing beside it.

Presumably somebody will be along with the drinks before long.

'One assumes' reads as 'presumably', which is the whole disguise: both of its words are working, one as a letter and one as an instruction.

25 down EJECT

French writer appearing in European court to exit rapidly (5)

Definition: to exit rapidly · container

To eject is to get out in a hurry — the word a pilot uses for leaving an aircraft — which is exiting rapidly by any measure.

  • European → E E is the standard short form of European
  • French writer → JE the writer of a clue is 'I', and the French for I is je
  • court → CT ct is the abbreviation for court, the one used in street addresses
  • appearing in — 'appearing in' places the piece before it inside the letters named after it, showing up in the middle of them.

A French novelist is making an appearance before a European court.

'Writer' means the person who wrote the clue, so it stands for the first person — a convention worth owning, because any writer in a clue may turn out to be I or me.

27 down RULES

Sport Dawson dominates (5)

Definition: dominates · charade

A side that rules its league dominates it, so the verb matches the definition sense for sense and in the third person.

  • Sport → RU RU is rugby union, the game's usual short form on a fixture list
  • Dawson → LES Les Dawson supplies his first name

A player called Dawson is head and shoulders above everyone else in his sport.

Les Dawson was a British comedian famous for deadpan mother-in-law jokes and deliberately terrible piano playing; the sportsman the surface suggests never existed.

28 down LATE

Departed towards the end (4)

Definition: Departed · double definition

Late is the obituary word for someone who has died, and also the plain sense of being well on in the day.

  • Departed → the funeral sense, used of a person who has died
  • towards the end → the clock sense, well on in the evening or the season

Somebody slipped away shortly before the finish.

Both readings are ordinary English, and the trap is that they join into a single sentence about leaving early, which is neither of them.
