Observer Everyman Crossword No 4,165

Across

1 across AFRICA

A day to get around the Continent (6)

Definition: the Continent · charade

Africa is one of the seven continents; the capital C teases the British habit of meaning mainland Europe by 'the Continent'.

  • A → A the article is taken at face value, one letter off the page
  • day → FRI FRI is the diary abbreviation for Friday, so any 'day' may be three letters
  • around → CA CA is short for circa, the Latin for about or around, used before a rough date

The capital C on Continent points squarely at Europe, which is the one continent this cannot be. Circa is worth banking: 'around' or 'about' is very often just those two letters.

5 across SCRUFF

Slovenly type's got some neck! (6)

Definition: Slovenly type · double definition

A scruff is an unkempt, badly turned-out person, and the scruff is also the loose skin at the nape that a cat is carried by.

  • Slovenly type → the unshaven, unpressed sort who looks like he dressed in the dark
  • some neck → not the whole neck but the loose skin at the back of it

The exclamation mark sells 'some neck!' as cheek — the nerve of the man. The setter means the anatomy, the bit you would pick up a kitten by.

8 across I PROMISE YOU

Everyman has potential to improve, solver: honestly (1,7,3)

Definition: honestly · charade

'Honestly' is the interjection thrown in to insist you mean it, which is the whole job of this three-word phrase.

  • Everyman → I the setter writing about himself is I or ME — here he signs his own name
  • potential to improve → PROMISE a player who shows promise has it in him to get better
  • solver → YOU the person on the other side of the clue is you

Setter and solver written into the same clue is Everyman's signature move, and both conventions are worth banking: the byline is I, and whoever is reading is YOU.

11 across REHEARSAL

Eyre hears a line that needs cutting in preparation for performance (9)

Definition: preparation for performance · hidden word

A rehearsal is the run-through a company works at before an audience is let in: practice held for the sake of the show.

  • Eyre hears a line → REHEARSAL the answer runs on from the tail of the name, through the next two words, and stops at the first letter of the last
  • needs cutting — A cutting is a strip snipped out of a longer text, so the answer is trimmed free of the words on either side of it.

Eyre, a line, a cut: those three words make a theatre out of the clue, and the theatre is there to stop you reading the letters as one continuous string.

12 across LILAC

Small athletic club in purple (5)

Definition: purple · charade

Lilac names both the shrub and the pale purple of its blossom, so it is a particular shade rather than the full colour.

  • Small → LIL lil is little with the middle worn away, the spelling of a nickname
  • athletic club → AC AC is the athletic club on a hundred sports badges, from AC Milan down

AC is the half most solvers see first; the informal 'lil' is the harder half, and it is the sort of spelling a setter can only use in short answers.

13 across COLOSSI

Company constructed silos: they're enormous (7)

Definition: they're enormous · charade + anagram

Colossi are giant statues — the Colossus of Rhodes and its kin — so sheer size is the whole of what the word carries.

  • Company → CO CO is the abbreviation on any company's letterhead
  • silos → LOSSI the five letters of the grain towers, rebuilt in a new order
  • constructed — Anything constructed has been put together out of parts, and parts put together afresh rarely go back in the order they arrived.

The Colossus of Rhodes was a bronze giant standing over a harbour, and the Latin plural is what 'they're' is quietly telling you to expect.

14 across SHYSTER

Conman's retiring, wanting rest badly (7)

Definition: Conman · charade + anagram

A shyster is a disreputable operator, usually a crooked lawyer, who works the small print rather than picking pockets.

  • retiring → SHY a retiring person is shy, hanging back rather than leaving a job
  • rest → STER the four letters of rest, shaken into a new order
  • badly — Rest taken badly is broken rest, and letters broken up come back in a different arrangement.

The surface has a crook giving up the trade and craving a holiday, so 'retiring' looks like leaving work. The word the setter wants is the timid sort.

15 across DISDAIN

A little garland is dainty, getting scorn (7)

Definition: scorn · hidden word

Disdain is scorn with a cold shoulder in it: contempt for something you consider beneath your notice.

  • garland is dainty → DISDAIN the answer starts inside the first word and finishes inside the last, running straight across the middle one
  • A little — A little of something is a part of it, not the whole, so only a stretch of these words is wanted.

Garlands, daintiness and scorn make a sentence that means something, and a sentence that means something is the best cover a setter ever has.

17 across AMBIENT

Bin meat that's rotten all around (7)

Definition: all around · anagram

Ambient describes whatever surrounds you — ambient noise, ambient temperature — the conditions on every side rather than any one source.

  • Bin meat → BIN MEAT seven letters from two short words, mixed together
  • rotten — Meat gone rotten has broken down, and material that has broken down is free to re-form as something else.

Bin and meat sit together so naturally that neither looks like fodder, and 'rotten' looks like a comment on the meat rather than an instruction about its letters.

20 across TUBER

Send back - drive back, perhaps, potato? (5)

Definition: potato? · reversal

A potato is the best-known tuber: a swollen underground stem that stores the plant's food, so the example points at the class.

  • drive back, perhaps → REBUT to rebut a charge is to drive it back, and 'perhaps' admits it is one of several words for that
  • Send back — Sending a thing back turns it to face the way it came, so its letters are read from the far end.

Two backs in a row, and only the first is an instruction. The question mark does the work of 'for example', since a potato is one of many.

21 across BELL TOWER

Lofty structure from which it's announced: 'It's time for girdles' (4,5)

Definition: Lofty structure · homophone

A bell tower is built high so its peal carries — campanile, steeple, belfry — which is why height is the point of the thing.

  • girdles → BELT a girdle is a belt, the band that goes round the waist
  • It's time → HOUR the hour is the time being called, the way a bar calls happy hour
  • It's time for girdles → BELL TOWER say those two words in one breath and they run together into the answer
  • announced — What is announced is taken in by ear, so the words are written down as they sound rather than as they are spelt.

The joke is the announcement itself: the tall thing that tells a town what time it is, telling it that it is time for belts.

22 across RED-HOT POKER

Re-do the pork: awkward bloomer (3-3,5)

Definition: bloomer · anagram

A bloomer is a plant that flowers, and this garden perennial sends up flame-coloured spikes every summer.

  • Re-do the pork → REDO THE PORK eleven letters from three words, the hyphen counting for nothing
  • awkward — An awkward thing sits wrong however you put it down, so these letters will not stay in the order given.

A bloomer is a blunder as well as a plant, and the surface is so busy being a kitchen disaster that the garden never occurs to you.

23 across ASTERN

One authoritarian at back of boat (6)

Definition: at back of boat · charade

Astern is the sailor's word for behind a vessel or towards its stern, which is the rear end of the hull.

  • One → A one of a thing is a thing, so 'one' is the article A
  • authoritarian → STERN an authoritarian parent is humorless and severe, quick to lay down the law

The strict sort and the back of a vessel are the same five letters, and the clue quietly uses one to build the other without ever pointing at the coincidence.

24 across DYNAST

One primarily dominant, yet nepotistically appointed, securing throne? (6)

Definition: One primarily dominant, yet nepotistically appointed, securing throne? · &lit + first letters

A dynast rules by family line, so a portrait of someone dominant and installed by relatives reads straight as well as cryptically.

  • dominant, yet nepotistically appointed, securing throne → DYNAST the opening letter of each of those six words, taken in the order they come
  • primarily — Primarily means first of all, so nothing counts in each word but the letter it opens with.

The question mark marks the whole sentence as the definition as well: the description of the man and the instruction for spelling him are the same words.

Down

2 down FLIGHTLESS BIRDS

Multiple sentences with no escaping e.g. penguins? (10,5)

Definition: e.g. penguins? · charade

Penguins are birds whose wings drive them through water instead of air, so they stand as one example of the whole class.

  • escaping → FLIGHT flight is what you take when you flee — a prisoner in flight is escaping
  • with no → LESS the suffix that means 'with no' is a word in its own right here
  • Multiple sentences → BIRDS bird is old prison slang for a stretch inside, so sentences are birds

The slang comes from birdlime rhyming with time, which is why a stretch inside is bird. Everything in the surface — sentences, escaping — belongs to a jail, and none of it belongs to the answer.

3 down INROADS

Some progress when visiting Greek island, reportedly (7)

Definition: Some progress · charade + homophone

You make inroads into a task — advances driven into ground that was resisting you, which is what progress amounts to.

  • visiting → IN a guest who is visiting is in, the way a host at home is in
  • Greek island → ROADS the Greek island is Rhodes, and said aloud it is spelt the other way
  • reportedly — Something reported is passed on by ear, so what matters is the sound of the word and not its spelling.

The setter never says which island, because only the sound of it counts — a homophone of a place name is Everyman's stock in trade, and it works on the ear alone.

4 down ADMISSION

Concession for entrance (9)

Definition: Concession · double definition

A concession is something you admit; entrance is being admitted. Both senses come straight off 'admit', one mental and one physical.

  • Concession → conceding a point in an argument is admitting it
  • entrance → the way in, and the price on the sign beside it

Both halves turn on the verb admit — admitting a fact, admitting a visitor. The surface reads as a ticket office offering cheap entry, which is a third sense the clue never uses.

5 down SISAL

Rope material is brought into stable regularly (5)

Definition: Rope material · container + alternate letters

Sisal is the stiff fibre stripped from agave leaves and twisted into rope, twine and matting.

  • stable → SAL the first, third and fifth letters of STABLE, keeping the steady beat
  • is → IS the little verb is taken as two letters and dropped inside
  • brought into — Bringing something into a word puts it inside, so the letters part company and let it sit between them.
  • regularly — Doing a thing regularly means at a steady interval, so take every other letter and keep the rhythm.

The surface is a tidy picture of a stable yard, which is exactly what makes 'regularly' read as a habit rather than as an instruction about letters.

6 down ROYALTY

Songwriter's payment: Taylor collected Japanese currency (7)

Definition: Songwriter's payment · anagram

A royalty is the cut a writer earns every time the song is played or sold: payment by usage rather than a flat fee.

  • Taylor → TAYLOR the surname goes into the mixture whole, all six letters of it
  • Japanese currency → Y Y is the symbol for the yen, and single-letter currencies are a setter's staple
  • collected — Things collected are gathered up in whatever order they come to hand, so the letters need not stay as they were.

The surname is left deliberately bare, so you cannot tell which Taylor is meant and start hunting for a first name. Seven letters is a short mix — write them out before guessing.

7 down FOUR-LETTER WORDS

'Term'; 'oath'; 'news'; 'chat'? (4-6,5)

Definition: 'Term'; 'oath'; 'news'; 'chat'? · cryptic definition

Each quoted item is spelt with exactly that many characters, and 'oath' carries the swearing sense the phrase is normally reaching for.

  • 'Term'; 'oath'; 'news'; 'chat'? → the surface offers a list of unrelated nouns and dares you to say what they share; count the items, then count the letters in each
  • 'oath' → the item that gives the game away, because a sworn oath and a shouted curse are the same word, and the phrase being defined usually means the second

There is nothing to take apart here: the clue is four demonstrations of itself, and the semicolons are only there to make it look like a quiz question.

9 down CROC

Caught by fabled avian, one with dozens of teeth (4)

Definition: one with dozens of teeth · charade

A croc is a crocodile, and a crocodile carries some sixty teeth at a time, replacing them throughout its life.

  • Caught → C C for caught comes off a cricket scorecard, where it stands before the fielder's name
  • fabled avian → ROC the roc is the giant bird of the Sinbad voyages, large enough to carry off an elephant

Two crossword regulars in four letters. The surface has the bird doing the catching, which is why nothing warns you that 'Caught' is a single letter.

10 down ICER

Cake decorator's instructions returned with two cuts (4)

Definition: Cake decorator · deletion + reversal

The icer is the one who spreads the icing, so the job title comes straight off the sugar coating they apply.

  • instructions → RECIPE the instructions for making a cake are its recipe
  • two cuts → RECI two letters are lopped off the end, the P and the E, leaving four
  • returned — To return is to come back the way you went, so what is left is read from its far end.

Cakes, decorating and instructions all belong to one kitchen, so nothing in the surface hints that one of those words is there only for its letters.

14 down SCALLOPED

Wavy at the edges, building collapsed (9)

Definition: Wavy at the edges · anagram

Scalloped means edged with a row of shallow curves, like the rim of a scallop shell — the pattern, not the seafood.

  • collapsed → COLLAPSED nine letters taken whole, straight off the page
  • building — Read it as the verb: to build is to assemble something from pieces, and these pieces go up in a different order.

A building that collapsed is a picture you accept without a second look, and that is what stops you noticing that one of those two words is material and the other is the instruction.

15 down DATE

Ascertain age of potential partner (4)

Definition: Ascertain age of · double definition

To date a fossil is to work out how old it is; a date is also the person the evening has been arranged with.

  • Ascertain age of → what a laboratory does to a bone or a pot, carbon and all
  • potential partner → the person you have arranged to meet, and hope to meet again

Carbon dating and a Friday night are the same four letters in different worlds, one a verb and one a noun, which is the whole of the joke.

16 down ABRIDGE

A card game? Cut (7)

Definition: Cut · charade

To abridge a book is to cut it down to a shorter version while keeping the sense: the editor's kind of cutting.

  • A → A the article stands as its own letter at the front
  • card game → BRIDGE bridge is the card game crosswords lean on hardest, usually for its bidding

The question mark is the setter admitting that plenty of other games would have fitted the words — it is the length and the letters that pick this one.

18 down BETOKEN

Give evidence of risk: Barbie's boyfriend accepting love (7)

Definition: Give evidence of · charade + container

To betoken is to be a token or sign of something — dark clouds betoken rain — which is evidence short of proof.

  • risk → BET a bet is money put at risk on an outcome
  • love → O love is nothing on a tennis scoreboard, and nothing is written as O
  • Barbie's boyfriend → KEN Ken has been the doll's boyfriend since 1961
  • accepting — To accept something is to take it in, so the letters on either side open up and let it sit between them.

Two conventions in one short clue: love for nothing comes off a tennis scoreboard, and the nothing is written as a letter O rather than a zero.

19 down TORY

Right-winger offering two extremes in tyranny? (4)

Definition: Right-winger · container + outer letters

A Tory is a British Conservative, so the label names a place on the political spectrum rather than a temperament.

  • tyranny → TY the two ends of the word, its opening letter and its closing one
  • offering two → OR 'or' is the little word that offers a choice between two things
  • extremes in — A word's extremes are its first and last letters, and here they close around what has gone before.

The question mark is the setter admitting the surface is unkind: a right-winger dealing in tyranny is a joke, not a definition, and only the letters are meant.

21 down BROWN

Author Dan is toast (5)

Definition: Author Dan · double definition

Dan Brown wrote The Da Vinci Code; and to brown bread under a grill is to toast it — a surname and a cooking verb.

  • Author Dan → the novelist who wrote The Da Vinci Code, named by his first name only
  • toast → what a grill does to bread, taken as a verb

'Is toast' is slang for doomed, which is what the surface wants you to hear about the novelist, and it hides a plain surname beside a plain cooking verb.
