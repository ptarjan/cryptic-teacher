Across

1 across ACCOUNT

Bill’s relative keeps Charlie company (7)

Definition: Bill · container + charade

A bill and an account are the same demand for money — the restaurant's bill is the account you settle.

  • relative → AUNT an aunt is a relative, and she is the word doing the keeping
  • Charlie → C Charlie is C in the NATO alphabet
  • company → CO co = company, as in the name of a firm
  • keeps — To keep someone company is to stay wrapped around them, so 'keeps' asks one word to hold the others inside it.

Someone's aunt is sitting with a man called Charlie so that he is not left on his own.

'Company' does two jobs at once: it is the CO the wordplay wants, and it is what makes 'keeps Charlie company' read as a kindness rather than an instruction.

5 across WALLABY

Aussie rugby international’s fool to tackle All Blacks’ biggest units (7)

Definition: Aussie rugby international · container + first letters

The Wallabies are Australia's national rugby union team, so the answer names one of their players — the team nickname used of a single man.

  • fool → WALLY a wally is a fool in British slang
  • All Blacks’ → AB the two capitals in the name of New Zealand's rugby side, taken as they stand
  • to tackle — A rugby tackle wraps your arms right round the man, so 'to tackle' asks one word to go round another.
  • biggest units — The biggest version of a letter is its capital, so a name's biggest units are the initials printed large.

An Australian player throws himself at the heaviest men in the New Zealand pack.

The clue reads as one pack of forwards taking on another, and the men it names are New Zealanders while the answer is an Australian — the definition sits right at the front.

9 across REACH

Stretch and run, feel pain after cycling (5)

Definition: Stretch · charade + cycling

Stretch and the answer trade places as noun or verb: a stretch of river is one, and to stretch out for something is to do the other.

  • run → R R is the cricket scorer's abbreviation for a run
  • feel pain → EACH to ache is to feel pain, and its final letter comes round to the front
  • after cycling — Cycling sends letters round like a wheel, so the word is wanted as it stands once its last letter has come round to the front.

A training routine — stretch, run, and the pains that arrive once the ride is over.

'Cycling' is not a sport here but an instruction, and the surface leans on that hard: every other word in the clue is exercise.

10 across LIFE-FORCE

Person getting break on QI? (4-5)

Definition: QI · charade

Qi is the Chinese name for the vital energy said to flow through a living body, and the answer is the plain English phrase for it.

  • Person → LIFE in insurance and in headlines a life is a person: the crash claimed five lives
  • break → FORCE to force a door is to break it open

A contestant gets a lucky break on a television quiz.

QI is a British comedy quiz programme, which is what the italics want you to see; the same two letters are the Chinese word the definition needs.

11 across PAPER OVER

This could make Liam hide? (5,4)

Definition: hide · reversal

Covering a crack with wallpaper instead of mending it is the image behind the phrase, and it means to conceal a fault — to hide it.

  • Liam → PAPER Liam read backwards is MAIL, and the Mail is a British national newspaper
  • This could make → OVER the clue asks which operation produces that name, and turning a thing over is what reverses it

Something has frightened a boy called Liam into hiding.

'Liam' looks like anagram fodder and is the finished product: the answer names an operation on a newspaper's title, and the clue shows you only what that operation produced.

12 across RULED

Determined game was up (5)

Definition: Determined · charade

A judge who ruled on a point determined it — the past tense of handing down a decision, not the sense of being strong-willed.

  • game → RU RU is rugby union, the game as it is abbreviated on fixture lists
  • was up → LED a side that was up in a match led it

Someone works out that the contest is lost and the game is over.

'The game was up' is the idiom the surface leans on, meaning the end has come, and the wordplay needs 'game' and 'was up' read as two separate things.

13 across SIGNS

Suit (gent’s) regularly discounted in Marks (5)

Definition: Marks · alternate letters

Signs are marks: the marks on a page or in the sky are the things you read a meaning from, and both words are plural.

  • Suit (gent’s) → SIGNS the odd-numbered letters of SUIT GENTS, counting the brackets out
  • regularly discounted — Discounting at a regular interval throws away every second letter, leaving the first, third, fifth and so on.

A gentleman's suit is on sale at a cut price in a high-street shop.

'Marks' in the surface is Marks & Spencer, the British chain where a suit might be discounted, and the definition needs it as plain marks instead.

15 across STAGGERED

Stunned as Bambi became this grand deer with animation (9)

Definition: Stunned · charade + anagram

To be staggered is to be stunned — the shock sense of the word, not the one about walking unsteadily.

  • Bambi became this → STAG the fawn of the film grows into a stag by the end of it
  • grand → G G is the standard abbreviation for grand
  • deer → ERED the four letters of DEER rearranged
  • with animation — Something done with animation is done with lively movement, and letters moved about that lively will not stay in the order given.

Bambi's growing up into a full-grown animal is one of the great moments in the cartoon.

'With animation' means liveliness and points at the film in the same breath, which is how the anagram signal disappears into the surface.

18 across BE EXCUSED

90 got through on buzzer to go to the loo (2,7)

Definition: go to the loo · charade

In a British school you ask to be excused when you need the lavatory, a polite formula that has come to mean that errand and no other.

  • buzzer → BEE a bee is the thing that buzzes
  • 90 → XC ninety in Roman numerals: ten short of a hundred
  • got through → USED to get through a supply is to use it up
  • on — In an across clue one thing 'on' another leans against it from the right, so the buzzer's letters are written first.

A quiz contestant reaches 90 by buzzing in, then asks for a comfort break.

The surface is a game show, so '90 got through on buzzer' reads as a score; the answer is the British schoolroom request for permission to leave the room.

19 across TUDOR

Edward VI possibly died thrashing about on reflection (5)

Definition: Edward VI possibly · container + reversal

Edward VI belonged to the royal house that ruled England from 1485 to 1603, so the king is one example of the dynasty and 'possibly' flags it.

  • thrashing → ROUT a rout is a thrashing, one side beaten out of sight
  • died → D d = died, as on a gravestone or in a family tree
  • about — Thrashing about goes round and round, so 'about' asks the thrashing word to wrap itself around the letter beside it.
  • on reflection — A reflection shows a thing the other way round, so the assembled letters are read backwards.

A guess at how the boy king met his end, thrashing about in his last hours.

'On reflection' means thinking again in the surface and a mirror in the wordplay, which is the only reason the two readings sit together so quietly.

21 across HENNA

News article on ambassador to add colour? (5)

Definition: add colour · charade

The reddish plant dye is used as a verb for what it does: put it on hair and you add colour to it, which the question mark flags as loose.

  • ambassador → HE HE is His or Her Excellency, the style an ambassador is addressed by
  • News → NN N is the abbreviation for new, and doubling a letter makes it plural, the way pp means pages
  • article → A A is an article, taken as the word itself
  • on — In an across clue one thing 'on' another sits to its right, so the ambassador's letters are written first.

A newspaper piece about an ambassador, written to brighten up the day's coverage.

'Add colour to a story' is the surface's idiom for livening it up, and the answer needs the words literally — dye on hair, not detail in a report.

23 across PREDATORY

Penny read about politician being prone to stalking (9)

Definition: prone to stalking · charade + anagram

A predatory animal stalks its prey, so 'prone to stalking' names the very habit the adjective describes.

  • Penny → P p = penny, the coin's abbreviation on a price tag
  • read → REDA the four letters of READ rearranged
  • politician → TORY a Tory is a British Conservative
  • about — Letters sent about are sent every which way, so the four beside it come back in a different order.

Penny reads an article about a politician with a habit of following people.

'Penny read about' looks like a woman with a newspaper and is three separate instructions; 'about' is the shuffling signal, not the subject of her reading.

25 across ASSAILANT

Alan Turing’s beginning to comprehend Nazis’ smart tech he attacks (9)

Definition: he attacks · container + charade + first letter

The word names the person doing the attacking, the one who sets upon somebody, which is what the clause describes.

  • Alan → ALAN the mathematician's first name, taken as it stands
  • Turing’s beginning → T the opening letter of the surname
  • Nazis’ → SS SS is the Nazi party's paramilitary corps
  • smart tech → AI AI is artificial intelligence, the field Turing's famous test belongs to
  • to comprehend — To comprehend something is to take it in and hold it, so the name outside closes around what follows.

Alan Turing starts to make sense of German codes with some clever machinery.

Turing broke the Nazis' ciphers and wrote the test for machine intelligence, so every word of the surface is true of him — and the definition is the one thing that is not.

26 across HORSE

Hotel of inferior quality – not the first in Whitley Bay? (5)

Definition: Bay · charade + deletion

A bay is a reddish-brown one, so the definition names the animal by its coat — an example standing for the whole, which the question mark admits.

  • Hotel → H Hotel is H in the NATO alphabet
  • of inferior quality → ORSE worse means of inferior quality, and it arrives here without its W
  • not the first in Whitley — The first letter in Whitley is its W, and 'not' throws that letter away, so the quality word starts one letter later.

A poor hotel at the seaside — not the first place you would try in Whitley Bay.

Whitley Bay is a seaside town on England's north-east coast, so the surface passes as a bad hotel review, and the definition is only the second word of its name.

27 across TRY IT ON

How’s your father’s leg strain initially? See how far you can push it? (3,2,2)

Definition: See how far you can push it? · charade

The idiom means chancing your arm — pushing to see what you can get away with before somebody stops you.

  • strain → TRY to strain at something is to try it, and both words mean testing a thing to its limit
  • your father → IT 'how's your father' is old British slang for sex, and 'it' is the coy word for the same thing
  • leg → ON in cricket the leg side of the field is the on side
  • initially — 'Initially' says where a piece goes rather than which letters it is: the strain word is placed at the front of the assembly.

Someone asks after dad's pulled leg muscle and how much more it can take.

'How's your father' is old British slang for hanky-panky, which is how the surface can enquire innocently after a torn muscle while the wordplay collects a euphemism.

28 across ENCAMPS

Man cleared out space in resort and pitches tents (7)

Definition: pitches tents · anagram + outer letters

An army doing this pitches its tents and settles for the night, one verb covering the whole business of making camp.

  • Man cleared out → MN MAN emptied of its middle leaves the two ends
  • space → SPACE five more letters of fodder, taken as they come
  • cleared out — A room cleared out has nothing left in the middle, so the word keeps only its two end letters.
  • resort — To re-sort is to sort again into a new order, which is what the letters beside it are asked to do.

A man clears out a space at a holiday resort and puts up tents.

'Resort' looks like the seaside town the surface needs and means re-sort, a second shuffle of the letters — the hyphen it hides is the whole trick.

1 down APROPOS

A very large rugby player breaks fitting (7)

Definition: fitting · container + charade

Fitting is the adjective sense — a remark that is fitting suits the moment exactly, nothing to do with plumbing.

  • A → A the clue's own A starts the assembly
  • rugby player → PROP a prop is the front-row forward who props up the scrum
  • very large → OS OS = outsize, the label on very large clothing
  • breaks — A prop breaking through a line ends up in the middle of it, so 'breaks' puts one word inside another.

An enormous forward smashes a fixture, the sort of damage a heavy pack leaves behind.

'Fitting' is the trap's other half: in the surface it is a plumber's fixture, a thing a big man can break, and the answer needs it as an adjective.

2 down CHAMPAGNE SOCIALIST

Rich red and variety of canapes might do inside (9,9)

Definition: Rich red · anagram + container

The sneering name for a wealthy person with left-wing politics — 'red' for the politics, 'rich' for the lifestyle.

  • canapes might → CANAPESMIGHT anagram fodder, 12 letters
  • do → SOCIAL a do is a social — the British word for a party you get invited to
  • variety of — A variety of a thing is that thing in a different arrangement, which is what these letters are asked for.
  • inside — 'Inside' says the party word is not tacked on the end but buried in the middle of the shuffled letters.

A deep red wine and a few little canapés would do nicely for people entertaining at home.

The definition is a jibe rather than a description: someone who votes left and lives well. 'Do' is the British word for a party, which lets 'might do inside' read as catering.

3 down USHER

You, me and that Girl Guide (5)

Definition: Guide · charade

To guide someone to their seat is exactly what the answer's verb means — the wedding sense of showing people where to go.

  • You, me → US you and me together are us
  • that Girl → HER the girl being pointed at is her

A Girl Guide is being pointed out to two people standing together.

'Girl Guide' is the trap: the capital G makes the two words look like one thing, and the definition is only the second of them.

4 down TELEVISES

Shows guys making American jeans (9)

Definition: Shows · substitution

A broadcaster shows a match by televising it: the two verbs stand in for each other directly, both in the third person.

  • guys → TEASES to guy someone is to tease them, mocking them for sport
  • American → A A is the standard abbreviation for American, and there is one A sitting in the middle of the previous word
  • jeans → LEVI Levi's are the American jeans; the wordplay takes the name without its S
  • making — 'Making' is one thing turning into another: the letter standing for American is made into the jeans brand.

Programmes about men on a production line turning out denim in America.

'Guys' looks like a plain noun and is a verb — to guy is to mock — which is why the swap is so hard to spot.

5 down WAFER

Returning uncooked iron-enriched biscuit (5)

Definition: biscuit · reversal + container

A wafer is a very thin crisp biscuit, the sort served beside ice cream — a plain synonym in the baking sense.

  • uncooked → RAW raw food is uncooked, and these three letters are then read backwards
  • iron → FE Fe is iron's chemical symbol
  • Returning — Something returning comes back the way it went, so the letters beside it are read the other way about.
  • enriched — Food enriched with iron has the iron put into it, so 'enriched' asks for the metal's symbol to go inside.

A diner sends back something from the tea tray for being underdone, iron supplement and all.

Breakfast cereals really are iron-enriched, which is what lets the surface pose as a nutrition label instead of an instruction.

6 down LEFT RIGHT AND CENTRE

These parts of 26 will get her everywhere (4,5,3,6)

Definition: everywhere · first letter + last letter + middle letter

The idiom means all over the place — things coming at you from every side at once — so the whole phrase stands in for 'everywhere'.

  • These parts of 26 → LEFT read the answer as an instruction: the left-hand end of the five letters at 26 is its H
  • These parts of 26 → RIGHT the right-hand end of those same five letters is the E they finish on
  • will get her → AND the phrase needs its joining word, and the picks are collected in the order it names them
  • will get her → CENTRE the middle of the five is the R, so the three picks come out as H, E and R

The bits of a horse, it is promised, will carry a rider anywhere she wants to go.

The answer is the instruction rather than its result: it says which letters of 26's entry to pick, and the three of them spell HER. A horse really would get her everywhere.

7 down APRIL

Case of protestor getting into trouble in days immediately following March (5)

Definition: days immediately following March · container + outer letters

The month that follows March in the calendar, defined by its days rather than named outright.

  • trouble → AIL to ail is to be in trouble, and this word is split to take the insert
  • Case of protestor → PR the first and last letters of protestor, the case around the rest
  • Case of — A case is what encloses something, so 'case of' takes the outside letters of the word and nothing within.
  • getting into — Getting into trouble puts you right in the middle of it, which is where these two letters go.

A demonstrator lands in hot water in the weeks just after a big protest march.

'March' is capitalised as a protest march and works as the month, which is the hinge the whole surface hangs on.

8 down YIELDED

Bore seeing almost all eyelids sadly, drooping at the start (7)

Definition: Bore · anagram + deletion + first letter

A tree that bore fruit yielded it: both are past tenses of producing a crop, which is the verb sense hiding behind 'bore'.

  • almost all eyelids → EYELID eyelids without its final letter, six letters of fodder
  • drooping at the start → D the opening letter of drooping joins the fodder
  • almost all — 'Almost all' of a word is every letter but the last, so one letter goes before the shuffling starts.
  • sadly — Eyelids drooping sadly are not holding their shape, and letters that are sad in this sense will not hold their order.
  • at the start — 'At the start' points at the opening letter of the word before it, taking one letter rather than the whole.

Someone is worn down watching a room full of tired eyes slowly close.

'Bore' wants to be the noun, a tedious person, and it is the past tense of a verb instead — the reading the answer's -ED ending settles.

17 down RADIOGRAM

Player of music ultimately wanting in on Mardi Gras parties (9)

Definition: Player of music · anagram + deletion

The old cabinet that housed a wireless and a record player in one piece of furniture is what the definition points at.

  • in on Mardi Gras → IOMARDGRA IN, ON, MARDI and GRAS each lose their final letter, leaving nine letters to shuffle
  • ultimately wanting — A word that is ultimately wanting is missing its final letter, and here every word in the list loses its last one.
  • parties — To part is to split up, so 'parties' says the letters break apart and re-form rather than naming a night out.

Someone is angling for an invitation to the carnival balls in New Orleans.

'Wanting in on' reads as one phrase — angling for an invitation — and it is really two instructions with a word boundary through the middle, which is where the four short words come from.

18 down BY HEART

From memory, Spooner’s greeting a Simpson (2,5)

Definition: From memory · spoonerism

The idiom puts memory in the heart: knowing a poem this way is knowing it from memory, word for word without the page.

  • greeting → BY the greeting is HI, and it gives up its aitch to the word after it
  • a Simpson → HEART the Simpson is Bart, and taking that aitch in exchange makes him a hart, a male deer, which is written out differently again
  • Spooner’s — The Reverend Spooner was famous for swapping the opening sounds of two words, so his name says trade the noises at the fronts.

Somebody says hello to one of the Simpsons.

Say 'hi Bart' aloud, swap the noises at the front and you hear 'by hart' — the vowels drift a little on the way, and the deer is respelled as the body part.

20 down RHYMERS

Short song by Queen covered by extremely raucous rappers? (7)

Definition: rappers · deletion + charade + container + outer letters

Rappers rhyme for a living, so the answer defines them by the thing they do — a definition by example, which the question mark admits.

  • Short song → HYM a hymn is a song, cut short by one letter
  • Queen → ER ER, Elizabeth Regina, is the crossword's standard queen
  • extremely raucous → RS the outer letters of raucous, which close around the rest
  • Short — A word made short loses its last letter, so the song arrives one letter down.
  • covered by — Being covered by something means it goes over you on both sides, so those end letters close around everything assembled so far.
  • extremely — The extremes of a word are its two ends, so 'extremely' takes the first and last letters and nothing between.

A brief Queen number buried under some very loud rapping.

Queen is a rock band in the surface and the monarch's cipher in the wordplay, which is the switch the whole clue turns on.

22 down NOSEY

Snooping on revolutionary agreed in retrospect (5)

Definition: Snooping · charade + reversal

The answer is the adjective for someone forever poking into other people's business, which is exactly what snooping is.

  • on → NO the clue's own word ON, turned round
  • agreed → SEY agreed is YES, and it too is read backwards
  • revolutionary — A revolution turns a thing full circle, so the little word beside it is read the other way round.
  • in retrospect — Looking in retrospect is looking back over what has been said, so those letters are taken in reverse.

A revolutionary is being watched, and what he agreed to looks different in hindsight.

'Snooping on' reads as one phrase and breaks after the first word: 'on' belongs to the wordplay, where it is two letters, not to the definition.

23 down PLAIN

Discussed aircraft’s manifest (5)

Definition: manifest · homophone

Something plain is manifest — out in the open, obvious at a glance — rather than the flat-ground or unadorned senses.

  • aircraft → PLAIN an aircraft is a plane, and said aloud it can be written a second way
  • Discussed — Something discussed is spoken rather than written, so the answer is what the other word sounds like, not how it is spelt.

The cargo list of an aircraft is being talked over.

'Manifest' is the trap: on an aircraft it is the cargo list, a noun, while the definition wants the adjective meaning obvious.

24 down AD HOC

For this special purpose, stir round hot and cold (2,3)

Definition: For this special purpose · container + charade

The Latin tag translates as 'for this': a committee formed this way exists for one particular purpose and nothing else.

  • stir → ADO an ado is a stir, a fuss — much ado about nothing
  • hot → H H is the hot tap
  • cold → C C is the cold tap, the two of them labelled that way on every sink
  • round — Stirring goes round in circles, so 'round' asks the stir word to close around the letter that follows it.

A recipe step: stir the mixture with hot and cold water to hand.

The letters come straight off a pair of taps, which is how 'hot and cold' can sit in a cooking instruction without looking like wordplay at all.
