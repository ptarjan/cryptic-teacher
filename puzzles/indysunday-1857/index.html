Across

7 across TEMPE

Beautiful valley not enough for tyrant emperor (5)

Definition: Beautiful valley · hidden word

Tempe is the Thessalian gorge the ancients held up as the model of scenic beauty, so the definition names it by reputation.

  • tyrant emperor → TEMPE five letters in a row, starting inside the first word and finishing inside the second
  • not enough — Not enough of a phrase is only part of it, so what is wanted is a run of letters out of the middle rather than the words themselves.

A cruel emperor for whom a lovely valley was never going to be enough.

The Vale of Tempe lies below Mount Olympus and the Greek poets treated it as the loveliest place on earth, which is why 'Beautiful valley' can define it on its own.

8 across PANCETTA

What deli may offer visiting prodnose down west (8)

Definition: What deli may offer · charade + reversal

PANCETTA is Italian cured pork belly, sold sliced at a delicatessen, so the definition names it by where you buy it.

  • visiting → AT someone visiting the dentist is at the dentist
  • prodnose → TEC a prodnose is British slang for a nosy investigator, and a tec is old slang for a detective
  • down → NAP the nap is the soft down raised on the surface of a cloth
  • west — West is the left-hand end of an across row, so the letters are written out leftwards, arriving back to front.

A busybody dropping in at the delicatessen counter.

'Down' is the pile on a fabric, not a direction, and every one of these little words is a noun pretending to be part of a sentence about a nosy visitor.

9 across ADO

Promotion introducing love song and dance (3)

Definition: song and dance · charade

To make a song and dance is to make a fuss, and ADO is that fuss, as in much ado about nothing.

  • Promotion → AD an ad is a promotion, short for advertisement
  • love → O love is a score of nothing at tennis, written as a nought
  • introducing — Introducing something means bringing it in behind you, so the promotion leads and what it brings in follows.

An advertisement opening with a love song and a dance number.

'Song and dance' is not music at all: making a song and dance about something is making a fuss about it, which is what the answer means.

10 across COLON

Pass over Panamanian port (5)

Definition: Panamanian port · charade

Colón stands at the Caribbean mouth of the Panama Canal and is the country's chief Atlantic port, named after Columbus.

  • Pass → COL a col is the dip between two peaks, the pass a walker crosses by
  • over → ON a coat over a chair is on it, so the two prepositions swap

Somebody being told to skip past a port in Panama.

'Pass over' reads as one verb meaning to ignore, and it is two separate words doing two separate jobs — the trap is treating them as a phrase.

11 across LIPSTICK

Spooner’s suggestions are much better than cosmetic (8)

Definition: cosmetic · spoonerism

LIPSTICK is make-up, so 'cosmetic' is the noun for it, sitting where the surface reads it as an adjective.

  • suggestions → LIPS suggestions are tips; hand its opening T to the word behind and take that word's L in exchange
  • are much better than → TICK to lick a rival is to beat them soundly; its L goes forward and the T arrives from the front word
  • Spooner’s — The Reverend Spooner's slips traded the opening sounds of neighbouring words, so you say the phrase aloud and swap the noises at the front.

Spooner insisting his advice beats any amount of make-up.

The hard half is 'are much better than', which is the verb lick — Victorian slang for a thrashing, still alive in 'a licking'.

12 across OWL

How hard, hiding large hooter (3)

Definition: hooter · deletion + charade

An OWL is the bird that hoots, so 'hooter' names it by the noise it makes rather than by anything you can see.

  • How → OW the question word with its opening letter gone
  • hard → hard is H, the grade stamped on a pencil, and that is the letter that hides
  • large → L L is the size on a clothing label
  • hiding — What goes into hiding is out of sight, so the letter named just before it never appears in the answer.

Someone wondering how difficult it is to conceal a big nose.

'Hooter' is British slang for a nose, which is the picture the comma sets up; the bird is the thing that actually hoots.

14 across SOLAR

The Sun’s as large when Earth’s eclipsed (5)

Definition: The Sun’s · charade + deletion

SOLAR means of the sun, and the possessive 'The Sun's' does the same job — a solar year is the sun's year.

  • as → SO as big as, so big — the two words swap freely
  • large → LAR LARGE with its final two letters dropped
  • Earth’s → Ge is the Greek earth goddess, the ge- of geology and geography, and hers are the letters that go
  • eclipsed — An eclipse blots a thing out, so the letters it names disappear from the word they were sitting in.

An eclipse in which the Sun looks as big as ever while the Earth is blacked out.

Earth is GE here, which is worth keeping: setters reach for the Greek goddess whenever those two letters suit them, and nothing in the clue announces her.

15 across CHESTS

Trunks he’s thrown in for nothing in the price of suit (6)

Definition: Trunks · substitution

A CHEST is a stout box for storage, which is exactly what a trunk is, so the two plurals match sense for sense.

  • in the price of suit → COSTS the price of a thing is its cost, and the suit is spades, written S on a bridge scorecard
  • he’s → HE the word taken at face value, its 's dropped as the ordinary verb 'is'
  • nothing → nothing is the letter O, and it is the one that makes way
  • thrown in for — Throwing one thing in for another is an exchange, so what follows is taken out and what precedes it takes the vacant place.

Swimming trunks thrown in free when you buy the suit.

'Trunks' wants to be swimwear or elephants and is the plural of a packing box, while 'suit' is not tailoring but cards — one of the four.

16 across TAU

Don’t read all of tense letter (3)

Definition: letter · deletion

TAU is the nineteenth letter of the Greek alphabet, so 'letter' defines it as one instance of that set.

  • tense → TAUT a rope pulled tense is taut
  • Don’t read all of — Not reading all of a word means stopping before the end, so its final letter never arrives.

Being told not to bother finishing a tense letter.

'Letter' is the alphabet rather than the post, and 'tense' is the adjective — not the grammatical kind that would give you a T.

17 across HUMBLER

Comparatively modest height at which acrobat has no head (7)

Definition: Comparatively modest · charade + deletion

HUMBLER is the comparative of humble, so 'Comparatively modest' asks for 'more modest' and gets it in a single word.

  • height → H H is the abbreviation for height in any table of measurements
  • acrobat → UMBLER an acrobat is a tumbler, here beheaded
  • has no head — Having no head means losing the letter at the top, which is the first one the word is written with.

A height at which an acrobat loses his nerve, or his head.

'Comparatively' is doing grammar rather than describing anything: it tells you the definition is a comparative, which is where the -ER on the end comes from.

20 across ROADING

Roman speciality laid out on a grid (7)

Definition: Roman speciality · anagram

ROADING is road-building and the road system it produces, and building roads is what the Romans were famous for.

  • on a grid → ONAGRID seven letters of fodder, the little words counting as letters like any others
  • laid out — Laying something out is putting its parts down in a fresh arrangement, and letters laid out will not stay in the order the clue prints them.

Roman engineering set out in the neat squares of a town plan.

Roman towns really were laid out on a grid, which is why the fodder reads as history rather than instruction — the surface is telling the truth about the wrong thing.

22 across TAR

Dark substance prepared by distilling salt (3)

Definition: Dark substance prepared by distilling · double definition

TAR is the black stuff distilled out of coal or wood, and 'salt' is the old word for a seasoned sailor.

  • Dark substance prepared by distilling → the black residue got by distilling coal or wood
  • salt → an old sailor, in the slang of the sailing navy

A dark substance apparently obtained by distilling salt.

The join is invisible because 'salt' reads as the raw material of the first definition. Jack Tar got his nickname from tarred canvas clothing and tarred pigtails.

23 across FOURTH

Some of our thoroughbreds out of the frame (6)

Definition: out of the frame · hidden word

In British racing the frame is the first three placings, from the board their numbers went up on, so FOURTH is just outside it.

  • of our thoroughbreds → FOURTH six letters in a row, beginning at the start of the first word and stopping inside the last
  • Some — Some of a phrase is a part of it, so a run of letters is wanted rather than the words themselves.

A few of our racehorses finishing nowhere at all.

Every word here is racing talk, which is what keeps the letters invisible: the sentence sounds like a trainer's excuse rather than an instruction.

24 across TEENS

Young people support couple holding hands (5)

Definition: Young people · charade

TEENS names the thirteen-to-nineteens themselves and not just the years, so it answers 'Young people' as a plural noun.

  • support → TEE a tee is the little peg that supports a golf ball
  • couple holding hands → NS the four players at bridge are North, South, East and West, and North and South are the pair who play together

Youngsters cheering on a couple walking along hand in hand.

The card table supplies two of the five letters: the hands in the surface are the ones dealt, and the couple are partners across the table.

27 across ATE

Mischiefmaker was irritating (3)

Definition: Mischiefmaker · double definition

Até is the Greek goddess of ruin and rash folly, and the verb sense is the gnawing in 'what's eating you?'

  • Mischiefmaker → the Greek goddess who drove men to reckless folly
  • was irritating → the sense in 'what's eating you?', a thing gnawing away at somebody

A troublemaker who used to get on everybody's nerves.

Nothing is capitalised to warn you a goddess is coming, and 'was irritating' looks like a description when it is the past tense of a verb.

29 across PENTAGON

Author is apparently identifiable figure (8)

Definition: figure · charade

A PENTAGON is a five-sided figure, so 'figure' is the plain geometric noun — with the building in Washington as the misdirection.

  • Author → PEN to pen a letter is to author it, so the verb hands over three letters
  • apparently identifiable → TAG ON with a tag on it, a thing can be told apart at a glance

A question about whether an author can be recognised on sight.

'Author' is a verb here, which is what hides the pen, and the two words after it have to be read as one phrase about labelling rather than as a description.

30 across BRUTE

Monster course dominated by stand (5)

Definition: Monster · container

A BRUTE is a savage beast, or a savage person, which is what 'Monster' names — the creature rather than the size.

  • stand → BE 'as things stand' is 'as things are', so the verb is simply to be
  • course → RUT a rut is the worn track a wheel runs in, the course it has to follow
  • dominated by — To be dominated by something is to have it standing over you, so the course ends up shut inside the other word.

A brutally hard racecourse with the grandstand looming over it.

'Monster' is a noun, not the adjective the surface wants, and 'stand' is the quiet verb of 'as things stand' rather than anything you sit in.

31 across TOO

The first fifteen letters as well? (3)

Definition: as well · charade

'As well' means in addition, which is the sense of TOO in 'me too' rather than the one in 'too much'.

  • The first → TO start at A and you are travelling: the opening stretch of the alphabet takes you towards something
  • fifteen letters → O count fifteen letters in from A and you arrive at the one the run stops on

Read the answer as two words and it is a map of the alphabet: begin at A, count fifteen, and that is where you have got to. The question mark carries the whole conceit.

32 across LEANNESS

Slimmed manner, not so round? (8)

Definition: Slimmed · container + deletion

LEANNESS is the state of carrying no spare flesh, which is what somebody who has slimmed is left with.

  • not so → LESS not so much of a thing is less of it
  • manner → ANNE the word for a way of behaving, with its outermost letters trimmed away
  • Slimmed — A word that has been slimmed has lost weight at both ends, so its first and last letters come off.
  • round — Going round something is closing over it, so the outer word wraps itself about the trimmed one.

A way of behaving pared down until nothing about it is rounded any more.

'Slimmed' is doing two jobs at once: it defines the answer and it is also the instruction, telling you to take weight off both ends of the word beside it.

33 across NORSE

Spanish gentleman has cycled here from Scandinavia (5)

Definition: from Scandinavia · cycling

NORSE means of ancient Scandinavia — its people and their language — so the definition places the adjective on the map.

  • Spanish gentleman → SENOR señor is the Spanish for gentleman or sir, and crosswords drop the accent
  • has cycled — Cycling sends the letters round a loop: the ones at the front go to the back and every letter keeps its order.

A Spanish gentleman who has ridden his bicycle down from Scandinavia.

Cycling looks like an anagram and is far stricter: no letter passes another, the run simply starts two letters further along.

Down

1 down STUCCO

Plaster-work crosses over with business (6)

Definition: Plaster-work · reversal + charade

STUCCO is the fine plaster spread over walls and moulded into cornices, which is the plaster-work of the definition.

  • crosses → CUTS one road crossing another cuts it, so the verb here is the geometric one
  • business → CO CO is the abbreviation a business writes after its name, short for company
  • over — Turning a thing over sets it the other way round, so the word before it is written out from its last letter back to its first.

Decorative plasterwork spilling over into somebody's commercial affairs.

'Crosses' wants to be an X, a saint or a religious symbol, and it is none of those: it is a plain verb, one line cutting another.

2 down AMELIA

One female crossword compiler, among others (6)

Definition: One female · container

AMELIA is a woman's given name, so 'One female' defines it by example — one of many names a woman might carry.

  • others → ALIA the Latin for others, as in the phrase inter alia
  • crossword compiler → ME the person writing the clue is talking about themselves
  • among — Being among things puts you in the middle of them, so the shorter word goes inside the longer one rather than beside it.

A woman who sets crosswords, named as one of a number of them.

Compiler, setter, author or writer in a clue almost always points at the person who wrote it, and the Latin of inter alia supplies the rest.

3 down BEANO

Possibly vote against party (5)

Definition: party · charade

A BEANO is British slang for a boisterous party or works outing, from the beanfeast an employer once laid on.

  • Possibly → BE possibly unpacks as 'may be', and the wordplay keeps the 'be'
  • vote against → A NO a vote cast against something is a no, and a single one of them is 'a no'

Someone at a meeting who looks likely to vote the motion down.

'Possibly' is the word every solver reads as an anagram signal; here it is 'you might be', and the rest of the phrase spells itself out in plain words.

4 down INSPECTOR

Official quietly breaks into military area (9)

Definition: Official · container

An INSPECTOR is an officer appointed to examine and report, both a police rank and a job title, so 'Official' names the post.

  • into military area → INSECTOR the IN of 'into' in front of SECTOR, a sector being ground assigned to a military unit
  • quietly → P p is piano in music, the direction to play quietly
  • breaks into — Breaking into a place means getting inside it, so the letter that breaks in lands in the middle of what follows.

An official slipping unseen through the wire of an army base.

'Into' has to be pulled in half: the IN is wordplay and only the TO is joinery. That split is what makes the sentence read so smoothly.

5 down MEATHEAD

Dumb-ass guy disturbs writer somewhat (8)

Definition: Dumb-ass · container

A MEATHEAD is a thick-headed, stupid person, which is what the American insult 'Dumb-ass' names.

  • writer → ME the setter writing the clue, referring to himself
  • somewhat → A TAD a tad is somewhat, a small amount of something
  • guy → HE a guy is a he, and this is the pair that breaks in
  • disturbs — To disturb someone is to break in on them, so the guy lands in the middle of the letters rather than alongside them.

A stupid bloke being a mild nuisance to somebody trying to write.

Two of these pieces are conventions worth owning: 'writer' is the setter, and 'a tad' is the small helping that turns up whenever a clue says somewhat.

6 down STOCKTON

Historic train terminus in Co. Durham – not Beds – carrying King north (8)

Definition: Historic train terminus in Co. Durham · container + reversal

Stockton-on-Tees was the terminus of the 1825 Stockton and Darlington Railway, the world's first steam-hauled public line.

  • not → NOT the little word taken at face value, all three letters of it
  • King → K K is the king on a chessboard and in a pack of cards
  • Beds → COTS cots are beds, the small ones, and nothing to do with a county
  • carrying — Carrying something means holding it inside you, so the King rides in among the other letters instead of following them.
  • north — North is up the page, and a down entry read upwards is read backwards, so the whole assembly goes down in reverse.

A railway landmark in the north-east with a royal passenger aboard.

The capital B and the dashes are there to sell you Bedfordshire, an aside about which county this is. Read as an ordinary plural noun instead.

8 down POLL

Take head off/tail off parrot (4)

Definition: Take head off · deletion

To POLL is to cut the top off — a pollarded tree, a polled beast — so 'Take head off' is that verb in ordinary words.

  • parrot → POLLY Polly is the standard name for a pet parrot, as in 'Polly wants a cracker'
  • tail off — A word's tail is its final letter, and taking it off leaves the word one letter shorter.

An instruction to trim a parrot at one end or the other.

The slash is the join between definition and wordplay, which is why both halves read as the same kind of instruction.

13 down WEAR

River Don (4)

Definition: River · double definition

The Wear is a County Durham river, and to don something is to wear it, so the noun and the verb are both covered.

  • River → the north-eastern river that runs through Durham and out at Sunderland
  • Don → to don a coat is to put it on

Two words that look like the name of a Russian or Yorkshire river.

There genuinely is a River Don, in Yorkshire and again in Aberdeen, and reading the two words as one name is the trap the clue is built on.

14 down SOH

Note: not a word – OED editor omitted parts (3)

Definition: Note · container + deletion

SOH is the fifth note of the tonic sol-fa scale — doh, ray, me, fah, soh — so 'Note' names it as a musical one.

  • not a word → SH 'not a word!' is the hiss you use telling somebody to keep quiet
  • OED → O the dictionary's three letters minus the editor's pair
  • editor → editor is ED, the two letters that are dropped
  • omitted — What is omitted is left out, so the editor's two letters never reach the answer.
  • parts — To part two things is to push them away from each other, so the arriving letter comes between them.

A lexicographer ruling that something is not a word and leaving parts of the entry out.

The colon after the first word reads as a heading and is really the seam between definition and wordplay, which is why the clue looks like a memo.

16 down TED HUGHES

Poet wanted hug? He shunned embraces (3,6)

Definition: Poet · hidden word

Ted Hughes was Poet Laureate from 1984 until his death in 1998, so 'Poet' names him by his trade.

  • wanted hug? He shunned → TED HUGHES nine letters in a row, beginning inside the first word and ending inside the last
  • embraces — To embrace something is to have your arms right round it, so the surrounding words are holding what you want between them.

Someone who wanted a hug and was turned down for it.

The question mark and the capital on 'He' exist to make this look like a sentence about rejection, and 'shunned embraces' is the setter telling you the opposite of what he is doing.

18 down UNOPENED

Like the champagne that’s on ice, this is his (8)

Definition: Like the champagne that’s on ice · cryptic definition

UNOPENED describes a bottle still corked, which is what champagne on ice is until somebody decides the moment has come.

  • Like the champagne that’s on ice → the surface picture: a bottle chilling in the bucket while nobody reaches for it
  • this is his → stop reading it as a sentence and look at the two words as words: one is the other with a top still on it

Champagne sitting in the ice bucket with nobody yet moved to pour.

There is no wordplay to take apart. The joke is that THIS is HIS still wearing its cap, and taking the top off is exactly what opening a bottle means.

19 down BARITONE

He’ll sing it through with a single exception (8)

Definition: He’ll sing · container

A BARITONE is the male voice between tenor and bass, and the man who owns it, so 'He'll sing' points at the singer.

  • exception → BAR bar means except, as in 'all bar one'
  • it → IT the word itself, dropped in whole
  • a single → ONE a single of anything is one of them
  • through — Something driven through a thing finishes up inside it, so these two letters end up in the middle of the phrase.

A singer who will get through the whole piece apart from one passage.

The three pieces are the everyday phrase 'bar one', which is why the sentence sounds so innocent — and 'it' is a word being quoted, not a pronoun standing for anything.

20 down RAPT

Transported under cover, thus delivered? (4)

Definition: Transported · homophone

Someone RAPT is carried away — absorbed, enraptured — which is 'transported' in its emotional sense rather than its haulage one.

  • under cover → RAPT anything under cover is wrapped, and that word said aloud is what gets written down
  • thus delivered — A line is delivered when it is spoken, so what matters is the sound of the word and not the way it is spelt.

Goods carried under a tarpaulin and dropped off at the door.

'Transported' is the old figurative sense, carried away by feeling, rather than the lorry the surface loads up; 'delivered' is working in both readings at once.

21 down GAS

Cut short energy supply (3)

Definition: energy supply · deletion

GAS is piped into houses for heating and cooking, so 'energy supply' names it by the job it does rather than by what it is.

  • Cut → GASH a cut in the skin is a gash
  • short — Something cut short stops before it should, so the last letter of the word never gets written.

Somebody cutting off the energy supply.

'Cut short' reads as a single phrase and works as two separate words, which is the whole of the misdirection in three syllables.

25 down ELUARD

French poet read in French; read in translation about it (6)

Definition: French poet · anagram + container

Paul Éluard was a leading French surrealist poet, so the definition places him by nationality and trade.

  • read in translation → EARD the four letters of READ set down in another order
  • read → LU lu is how a Frenchman writes it, the past participle of lire
  • in translation — A translation says the same thing in a different set of letters, which is the licence to reorder these ones.
  • about it — About means round the outside, and 'it' points back to the word just made, so the reordered letters close over it.

A French poet enjoyed both in the original and in somebody's translation.

The clue says 'read' twice and means something different each time: once the French word, once the four letters shuffled. The semicolon is the only warning you get.

26 down NEEDED

Born, then died, one no longer essential (6)

Definition: essential · charade + deletion

Something NEEDED is required, which is 'essential' in the sense of being the thing you cannot do without.

  • Born → NEE née is what stands in front of a married woman's birth name
  • died → DED the past tense with one letter taken out of the middle
  • one → one is the Roman numeral I, and that is the letter that goes
  • no longer — What is no longer there has gone, so the letter named just before it is taken out of the word.

A whole life in two dates, and somebody who is no longer wanted.

'One' is the numeral I, which turns up whenever a clue mentions a single anything — a convention that costs a solver a lot of time the first few times it appears.

27 down ANTS

Hill residents periodically cannot use (4)

Definition: Hill residents · alternate letters

ANTS build and live in the mound called an anthill, so 'Hill residents' names them by the address rather than the insect.

  • cannot use → ANTS run the two words together and keep only what stands in the even places
  • periodically — Something done periodically happens at regular intervals, so letters are taken at a steady step instead of all of them.

People on a hill who cannot use something only at certain times.

'Periodically' is doing arithmetic, not describing how often anything happens, and 'Hill residents' is the setter's straight face about an anthill.

28 down EBONY

Napoleon with energy climbing tree (5)

Definition: tree · charade

EBONY is a tropical tree of the genus Diospyros, so 'tree' defines the plant and not the black timber it is better known for.

  • energy → E E is the physicist's symbol for energy, and it is the letter that does the climbing
  • Napoleon → BONY Boney was the British nickname for Napoleon Bonaparte, and this is what stays behind once the climbing letter has gone up
  • climbing — In a down entry climbing means going up the grid, so the letter that climbs ends up above the rest of them.

Napoleon going up a tree with a good deal of energy.

The trap is 'Napoleon': solvers reach for the brandy, the pastry or the pig from Animal Farm, and the clue wants the nickname Britain gave him through twenty years of war.
