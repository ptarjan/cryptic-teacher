Across

1 across THE SECOND COMING

Just as orgasm prompts poem by Yeats … (3,6,6)

Definition: poem by Yeats · charade

The Second Coming is Yeats's best-known poem, so the definition names the answer by its title rather than by any meaning of the words.

  • Just as → THE SECOND 'The second she walked in' and 'just as she walked in' are the same sentence — the phrase works as a conjunction meaning the very moment
  • orgasm → COMING the vulgar verb for reaching a climax, in its -ing form

An orgasm putting somebody in mind of a bit of Irish poetry.

Yeats wrote it in 1919 — 'Things fall apart; the centre cannot hold' — and took the title from the promised return of Christ, which is the sense the setter is quietly swapping for a cruder one. The dots run the sentence on into 9 across.

9 across MISTAKE

… wrongly read note by misfortunate Keats (7)

Definition: wrongly read · charade + anagram

To mistake something is to take it wrongly — to read a remark or a signal the wrong way — which is what the definition describes.

  • note → MI mi is the third note of the sol-fa scale, after do and re
  • misfortunate Keats → STAKE the five letters of the poet's name, dealt out in another order
  • misfortunate — A name that has come off badly has been knocked out of shape, so its letters do not keep the order they were printed in.

Somebody misreading a note left by a luckless Keats.

'Note' wants to be the thing that was misread and it is the sol-fa syllable. The dots carry the sentence on from 1 across, where a poet's name was doing a completely different job.

10 across RHYMERS

For example, Keats and Yeats (or not) (7)

Definition: For example, Keats and Yeats (or not) · cryptic definition

A rhymer is a writer of verse, and both men are instances of the class — though their names are the one pair that will not rhyme.

  • For example, Keats and Yeats → two poets offered as instances of something, and on the page the names look like a matched pair, one letter apart
  • (or not) → the bracket takes the pairing back: one name is said 'keets' and the other 'yates', so they look alike and do not sound alike

Two famous poets put forward as an example of something, with a bracketed second thought.

There is no wordplay to hunt for here. Both men wrote verse, so both qualify — but only as poets, never as a matching pair, which is what the aside owns up to.

12 across TREACHERIES

Heretics are punished for unfaithful acts (11)

Definition: unfaithful acts · anagram

A treachery is an act of betrayal, a broken faith with somebody who trusted you, and the plural definition matches the plural answer.

  • Heretics are → HERETICSARE anagram fodder, eleven letters
  • punished → anagram indicator
  • punished — A word under punishment is being knocked about, and letters knocked about do not stay in the order they arrived in.

Heretics being punished for breaking faith.

Surface and answer are about the same thing, which is what makes the letters so hard to see: breaking faith is precisely what heretics were burned for.

13 across BANANA SKIN

You might slip on this bats blood (6,4)

Definition: You might slip on this · charade

A banana skin left on the pavement is the classic thing to slip on, so the definition describes the answer by what happens when you tread on it.

  • bats → BANANAS both words are slang for crazy — he's gone completely bats, he's gone completely bananas
  • blood → KIN your blood is your kin, the family you are related to

A witch's recipe of bats' blood, and a warning not to tread in it.

The definition is the whole first half and works by what the thing does to you. In British political talk the same phrase names any small object that trips a career up.

15 across NEON

Light’s non-binding energy (4)

Definition: Light · container

A neon is the glowing tube itself in everyday speech, so 'Light' names the answer by the thing the gas is bought for.

  • non → NON the three letters at the front of the hyphenated word, taken as they stand
  • energy → E e = energy, the E of Einstein's equation
  • binding — Binding goes round and round a thing, so the word in front of it closes over the letter that comes next.

A lamp's energy, offered with no commitment attached.

The chemistry is real: neon is a noble gas and bonds with nothing, so 'non-binding' describes the answer at the same time as it hides the instruction.

18 across ECHO

E … e (4)

Definition: E … e · &lit

The clue prints a letter, a pause, then the same letter fainter — which is what an echo is, a sound coming back quieter than it went out.

  • E → ECHO the NATO alphabet gives this letter a whole word to say over the radio, so the capital on its own is four letters
  • e → no letters from this one: it is the same character again, smaller and a moment later, and the printed dots are the pause in between

Any repeated letter would draw a picture of the thing; only this one also names it, because the radio alphabet turns E into a word. That is why the four-letter enumeration is fair.

20 across EVANGELISM

Bible teaching wrongly maligns Eve (10)

Definition: Bible teaching · anagram

Evangelism is the preaching of the gospel, so 'Bible teaching' names the activity by the book it teaches from.

  • maligns Eve → MALIGNSEVE anagram fodder, ten letters
  • wrongly → anagram indicator
  • wrongly — Done wrongly is done other than it should be, so the letters go down in an order that is not theirs.

A sermon that slanders Eve and gets its scripture wrong.

'Wrongly maligns' hangs together as a phrase, one accusation unfairly made, and only the first of the two words is an instruction. Eve is the sermon's target on the surface and nine letters underneath.

23 across GASTRONOMIC

Gordon’s starter presented to massive foody? (11)

Definition: foody? · charade + first letter

'Foody' means to do with good eating, which is the territory gastronomic covers — meals, cooking and the pleasure taken in them.

  • Gordon’s starter → G the first letter of Gordon
  • massive → ASTRONOMIC astronomic costs are massive ones, on the scale of the stars
  • starter — A starter is what comes first at table, so the letter wanted is the one at the front of the name.
  • presented to — What is presented to somebody is held out in front of them, so this letter goes before the rest.

Gordon Ramsay's first course put in front of an enormous food lover.

The setter signs his own clue: Ramsay is his pseudonym and Gordon Ramsay is the television chef, which is why a starter and a food lover turn up in the same sentence.

25 across SEETHE

That man follows backwash of river froth (6)

Definition: froth · reversal + charade

Water that seethes boils and bubbles at the surface, which is what froth does — verb for verb, with the answer's angrier sense held back.

  • backwash of river → SEET the Tees is a river in north-east England, running out past Middlesbrough, and here it runs the other way
  • That man → HE that man is he
  • backwash of — Backwash is water running back the way it came, so the name beside it is written out from its end.
  • follows — What follows something comes after it, so this piece is written second.

A man standing where the river's backwash throws up froth.

The answer is split across two entries, so the enumeration in the clue counts six letters that the grid gives three at a time. To seethe is to boil and to be furious in the same breath.

26 across IGNOBLE

Playing bingo, the French are mean (7)

Definition: mean · anagram + charade

An ignoble act is a mean one — small-minded, and beneath the person who stooped to it — so the two adjectives swap directly.

  • Playing bingo → IGNOB the five letters of BINGO in a new order
  • the French → LE 'the' in French is le, one of the crossword's standard borrowings
  • Playing — A word played with is a word knocked out of shape, so the letters beside it will not stay where they were put.

The French turning nasty over a game of bingo.

'The French' is not a nationality here but the French word for 'the', which is the commonest borrowing in British cryptics and worth learning in both genders.

27 across CAMELOT

Beast of burden returned to castle (7)

Definition: castle · charade + reversal

Camelot is King Arthur's castle in the legends, so 'castle' defines by example, one named stronghold standing in for the general word.

  • Beast of burden → CAMEL the camel is the desert's pack animal
  • to → OT the clue's own 'to', its two letters turned end for end
  • returned — This entry runs across the grid, so something returned is written back the way it came, right to left.

A pack animal sent back to the castle.

'Returned to castle' reads as one journey, and the smallest word in it is the second piece of the wordplay rather than the preposition it pretends to be.

28 across FRANCISCO FRANCO

Fascist leader controlled Houston with rumba master general (9,6)

Definition: general · charade + first letter

Franco was the general who seized power in the Spanish civil war, so 'general' names him by his rank — one soldier standing for the word.

  • Fascist leader → F the first letter of Fascist
  • controlled → RAN whoever controlled a business ran it
  • Houston → CISCO Cisco Houston was an American folk singer who toured with Woody Guthrie
  • rumba master → FRANCO Franco Luambo was the Congolese bandleader known as the grand master of African rumba
  • leader — A leader is the one out in front, so the letter wanted is the one at the head of the word before it.

A fascist boss running the city of Houston with a rumba bandleader at his side.

'Houston' looks like the Texan city and is a surname, and the same trick is played again on the bandleader. Franco ruled Spain from 1939 until his death in 1975.

Down

1 down TEMPTABLE

Backup part of database liable to corruption (9)

Definition: liable to corruption · charade

Somebody temptable can be led astray, so the corruption meant is the moral kind — being bought — and not data going bad.

  • Backup → TEMP an office short of hands brings in a temp for backup, someone standing in for a while
  • part of database → TABLE a database is built out of tables, one for each kind of record

A spare copy of some data sitting where it could easily be damaged.

A temp table is a genuine database object, so the first two thirds of the clue read as one technical phrase and give no hint that they are two separate pieces.

2 down EASTERN

Sea-sick seabird from China? (7)

Definition: from China? · anagram + charade

China lies in the East, so 'from China?' defines by example — one eastern country asked to stand in for the adjective itself.

  • Sea-sick → EAS the three letters of SEA, no longer in that order
  • seabird → TERN a tern is the slender gull-like seabird, and one of the crossword's regulars
  • sick — Something sick is out of sorts and not itself, so the three letters beside it will not stand in the order they were given.

A queasy seabird that has flown in from China.

'Sea-sick' looks like one word describing the bird, and the hyphen is where the fodder ends and its instruction begins. The question mark earns its place: China is one eastern country among many.

3 down EXACTING

Challenging former lover on stage (8)

Definition: Challenging · charade

An exacting employer demands a great deal and accepts nothing less, which is 'challenging' in its adjectival, hard-to-satisfy sense.

  • former lover → EX your ex is the partner you used to have
  • on stage → ACTING what a person up on the stage is doing

An old flame being difficult up on the stage.

'Challenging' looks like the verb, somebody squaring up to their ex, and it is the adjective — the sense in which a job or a boss makes demands you can barely meet.

4 down OBESE

Huge honour to sit on banks of Seine (5)

Definition: Huge · charade + outer letters

Obese is the medical adjective for grossly overweight, so 'Huge' is the everyday word for the same bulk.

  • honour → OBE the OBE, Officer of the Order of the British Empire, is a rank of honour handed out twice a year in Britain
  • banks of Seine → SE the first and last letters of Seine, the two edges of the word
  • to sit on — This entry runs down the grid, so whatever sits on another thing is written above it.
  • banks of — A river's banks are its two edges, so the letters wanted are the ones at either end of the word with nothing from between.

A grand award being presented on a Paris riverbank.

'Huge honour' hangs together as a phrase — the sort of thing a recipient says on the day — and only the first word is the definition, describing a body rather than an award.

5 down DIRECTION

Re-entering language course (9)

Definition: course · container

A ship or a career holds a course, which is the direction it is set on, so the two nouns swap straight over.

  • language → DICTION diction is a speaker's language, the words chosen and the way they are delivered
  • Re → RE the clue's own opening syllable, its two letters taken just as they are printed
  • entering — What enters a thing ends up inside it, so these two letters are planted within the word named next.

Going back into a language class.

'Re-entering' reads as a single word meaning going back in, and the hyphen is the seam: the prefix is letters and everything after it is instruction.

6 down OXYGEN

Gone wild with axes splitting something needed for fire (6)

Definition: something needed for fire · anagram + container

Fire cannot burn without oxygen, so the definition names it by the job it does in combustion rather than by any synonym.

  • Gone wild → OGEN the four letters of GONE in a new order, then prised apart after the first
  • axes → XY the x and y axes of a graph
  • wild — Something gone wild is beyond all control, and letters in that state keep none of the order they arrived in.
  • splitting — To split a thing is to open a gap in the middle of it, and that gap is where the next piece goes.

Someone rampaging with axes through the wood for the fire.

'Axes' is the plural of axis, not of axe, and that single swap is what keeps the woodpile in front of you when the wordplay is really drawing a graph.

7 down ITEMISE

Provide detailed account of quite miserable passage (7)

Definition: Provide detailed account of · hidden word

To itemise is to set a thing out item by item, which is what providing a detailed account of something amounts to.

  • quite miserable → ITEMISE seven letters in a row, starting two into the first word and stopping four into the second
  • passage — A passage is a way running straight through something, and the letters wanted run straight through the two words in front of it.

A rather gloomy stretch of writing being gone through line by line.

Hidden runs are hardest to see across a word break, and 'passage' passes for part of the description — a miserable passage of prose — while doing the work of the instruction.

8 down GASES

Donates, as replacing 4, 6 and 15 (5)

Definition: 6 and 15 · substitution

6 down is OXYGEN and 15 across is NEON, so the definition names them by the class both of them belong to.

  • Donates → GIVES to donate is to give, and the clue's tense makes it the third person
  • 4 → IV the Roman numeral for four, and it is sitting in the middle of the previous word
  • as → AS the small word of the surface, handed over as two letters to fill the gap
  • replacing — One thing replacing another takes its place exactly: the letters named move out and the letters beside the instruction move in.

A donor's gifts standing in for three items further up the list.

The numbers do two different jobs in one short clue: 4 is Roman numerals inside a word, while 6 and 15 are grid references pointing at other answers.

14 down SEVEN SEAS

International waters still surrounded by dumb-asses (5,4)

Definition: International waters · anagram + container

The seven seas are the world's oceans taken together, the waters no nation owns, so the definition names them by their legal standing.

  • dumb-asses → SSEAS the five letters of ASSES re-sorted, then opened up after the first to take something in
  • still → EVEN still in the sense of 'still better' — swap in 'even better' and the phrase is unchanged
  • dumb — Dumb here is the American sense, stupid rather than silent, and a word made stupid loses whatever sense its order gave it.
  • surrounded by — What surrounds a thing closes round it on both sides, so this piece ends up inside the letters named after it.

A stretch of calm water with fools standing all round it.

'Still' looks like a description of the water and means even. The seven seas is the old sailors' phrase for all the oceans there are, which is why one country cannot own them.

16 down NUMBER TWO

Dropping dead, wrote frantically (6,3)

Definition: Dropping · charade + anagram

A dropping is a piece of excrement and number two is the nursery word for it, so the two nouns name one thing at different levels of politeness.

  • dead → NUMB a dead leg is a numb one, with no feeling left in it
  • wrote frantically → ERTWO the five letters of WROTE, scribbled down in another order
  • frantically — Writing done frantically comes out in a scramble, so the letters beside it land in no particular order.

Somebody dropping dead after a frantic bout of writing.

'Dropping' is a noun and not the verb the surface offers — the sort of thing a bird leaves on a car roof — and the answer is the nursery euphemism for the same article.

17 down NEWCOMER

Fresher report of any toilet more sullied (8)

Definition: Fresher · homophone + charade + anagram

A fresher has just arrived and knows nobody, so the definition works by example: one particular kind of newcomer standing for all of them.

  • report of any → NE say the letters N and E one after the other and you have said the word aloud
  • toilet → WC WC = water closet, the label on a lavatory door
  • more sullied → OMER the four letters of MORE, put back in a different order
  • report of — A report of something is that thing as it was heard, so what follows is judged by ear and written down however the sound allows.
  • sullied — Something sullied has been messed about with, and letters messed about with do not keep the order they came in.

A student's account of a lavatory that has got dirtier.

Spelling a word out of letter names — enn, ee — is the step to spot, and it hides because 'report of any' reads as ordinary reporting. In Britain a fresher is a first-year university student.

19 down HOSANNA

Take end from communion wafer – on reflection, an exaltation! (7)

Definition: exaltation · deletion + charade + reversal

A hosanna is a shout of praise to God, and an exaltation is exactly that: the lifting of something high in worship.

  • communion wafer → HOS the wafer taken at communion is the host, and the instruction has just lifted its final T away
  • an → AN the small word of the surface, handed straight over as it stands
  • on reflection → NA those same two letters shown in a mirror, so they come back the other way round
  • Take end from — The end of a word is its final letter, and taking that away leaves the word one letter short.
  • on reflection — A reflection shows a thing the other way round, so the two letters just handed over are written back to front.

A worshipper taking the last of the communion wafer and bursting into praise.

One tiny word does the job twice, forwards and then mirrored, which is the whole difficulty of the second half. Hosanna is the cry the crowd raised on Palm Sunday.

21 down INSULIN

Slight temperature dropping off with cool hormone (7)

Definition: hormone · deletion + charade

Insulin is the pancreas's hormone for controlling blood sugar, so the definition names the class the answer belongs to.

  • Slight → INSULT a slight is an insult, a snub delivered to somebody's face
  • temperature → T t = temperature, and it is this letter that comes off the previous word
  • cool → IN what is in is what is fashionable, the cool thing this season
  • dropping off — Whatever drops off has come away and is gone, so the letter named goes out of the word in front of it.

A mild fever passing off and the patient cooling down.

'Slight' looks like an adjective attached to the temperature and it is a noun meaning a snub — two words that read as one symptom are two separate pieces.

22 down ARABIC

A right-beginning, dotted-letter-including alphabet (6)

Definition: A right-beginning, dotted-letter-including alphabet · &lit + charade + first letter + container

Arabic script runs right to left and distinguishes many letters by dots, so the whole clue is a true description of the alphabet it also spells.

  • A → A the clue's first word, taken as the single letter it is
  • right-beginning → R the first letter of RIGHT
  • alphabet → ABC the ABC is the alphabet, and here it is opened up before its last letter
  • dotted-letter → I in lower case this vowel is written with a dot on top of it
  • beginning — The beginning of a word is the letter it starts with, and that single letter is all that is wanted.
  • including — What a thing includes sits inside it, so the letter named goes within the piece beside it.

Every word is doing both jobs at once: Arabic really is written from the right, and its letters really are told apart by dots above and below them.

23 down GRIEF

Sadness as building terminally fire-damaged (5)

Definition: Sadness · charade + last letter + anagram

Grief is the sadness that follows a loss, so the two nouns are plain synonyms, with the answer carrying the sharper sense of bereavement.

  • building terminally → G the last letter of buildinG
  • fire-damaged → RIEF the four letters of FIRE, left in a different order
  • terminally — What happens terminally happens at the very end, so the letter wanted is the word's last.
  • damaged — Damaged goods have lost their shape, and four letters damaged in a fire come out in a different order.

Sorrow at a building burnt out from end to end.

'Terminally' reads as part of the damage report — a building terminally damaged is beyond saving — and it is the instruction picking out one letter.

24 down MACHO

Like Top Gun pilot’s taxiing speed (approximately) (5)

Definition: Like Top Gun pilot · charade

Macho means aggressively masculine, all swagger and bravado, which is how the film draws its pilots — the definition describes them by type.

  • speed → MACH aircraft speed is given in Mach numbers, multiples of the speed of sound
  • taxiing → O a plane crawling round the airfield is doing nought, and the nought is written as a letter
  • approximately — The bracket admits the figure is not exact: round the speed to the nearest whole number and it comes out at nothing at all.

How fast a Top Gun pilot is going while still trundling round the airfield.

Top Gun is italicised because it is the film, and its fighter pilots are exactly the swaggering sort the definition wants. Mach 1 is the speed of sound, so taxiing barely registers.
