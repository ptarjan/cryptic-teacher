Across

7 across NO CHANCE

Part of Badenoch ancestry that you'll never see! (2,6)

Definition: that you'll never see! · hidden word

that you'll never see! -> NO CHANCE: the exclamation says a thing will never happen, so an ancestry nobody will ever lay eyes on is one that does not exist.

  • Badenoch ancestry → NOCHANCE eight consecutive letters run across the gap between the two words, starting partway into the first
  • Part of — Naming a part rather than the whole says the letters wanted are a run sitting inside the words that follow.

Badenoch is both a district of the Scottish Highlands and a Conservative leader's surname, so the eye reads it as a fact to check rather than a string of letters.

8 across IN-CALF

Old American lift regularly expected to drop a little lower (2-4)

Definition: expected to drop a little lower · charade + alternate letters

expected to drop a little lower -> IN CALF: a cow in calf is pregnant, due to drop her calf, and a lower is a beast that lows.

  • Old American → INCA the Inca ruled Peru long before Columbus arrived, so they are Americans of the oldest kind
  • lift → LF the first and third letters of the word, taken in that order
  • regularly — Letters taken regularly come at a fixed interval, and the interval here is every second one, counting from the first.

'Old American lift' reads as a museum piece and is two separate pieces of wordplay. Everything after it is the definition, which is a farmer's phrase rather than an instruction.

10 across ISLE

E.g. Lewis given wrong information, obscuring case (4)

Definition: E.g. Lewis · deletion

E.g. Lewis -> ISLE: Lewis is an island off the Scottish coast, so the clue defines by example, naming one instance instead of the class it belongs to.

  • given wrong information → MISLED anybody given wrong information has been misled, six letters before anything is hidden
  • case → MD the case of a word is its outer shell, one letter at each end, and both of those go out of sight
  • obscuring — To obscure a thing is to put it out of sight, so the letters named next disappear from the word in front.

Lewis is the northern part of the largest island in the Outer Hebrides, and 'E.g.' is the warning that the answer is the general word rather than the name.

11 across ANTAGONISE

Turn against one? (10)

Definition: Turn against one? · &lit + anagram

The whole clue defines: to antagonise somebody is to turn them against you, with 'one' the formal pronoun standing in for yourself.

  • against one → AGAINSTONE ten letters of raw material, exactly the length wanted, read for their spelling and not their sense
  • Turn — To turn something is to change it into something else - turned milk is no longer milk - and the letters beside the word are changed round.

An all-in-one: read straight, the three words describe what the answer does to a person, and read again those same three words are the ten letters to rearrange.

12 across HURTLE

In your ear, pain will go quickly (6)

Definition: go quickly · homophone

go quickly -> HURTLE: to hurtle is to move fast and barely under control, so it is going quickly with the recklessness built into the word.

  • pain → HURT a pain is a hurt, the noun, and this is the part said first
  • pain will → HURTLE English contracts 'will' to 'll and hangs it on the word before; say the two together and the apostrophe vanishes
  • In your ear — Something put in your ear is heard rather than read, so what is wanted is the spelling of a sound and not the words on the page.

Say it rather than read it. Two words become one, and the six letters you write down are not the letters of either of them.

14 across GARNACHA

How lovely, toilet paper turned red in Spain (8)

Definition: red in Spain · charade + reversal

red in Spain -> GARNACHA: the Spanish name for the grenache grape, the country's great red-wine grape, so this is a red as Spain spells it.

  • How lovely → AH the small noise of pleasure somebody makes at the sight of something sweet
  • toilet → CAN the can is American slang for the lavatory
  • paper → RAG a rag is a newspaper, named with contempt for what is printed in it
  • turned — Letters that have been turned are read the other way along the row, so the whole assembly comes out back to front.

'Turned red' looks like one phrase, a blush. 'Turned' belongs to the wordplay and 'red' to the definition, which is where the sentence quietly stops being English.

16 across THE COAST IS CLEAR

Easter's Catholic originally, one might safely venture (3,5,2,5)

Definition: one might safely venture · anagram

one might safely venture -> THE COAST IS CLEAR: the phrase comes from smugglers watching a shore, and means nobody is in sight to stop you.

  • Easter's Catholic → EASTERSCATHOLIC fifteen letters of raw material, the exact length required, handed over for their spelling
  • originally — To be original is to be unlike whatever came before, so the letters are put into an arrangement they were not in.

'Catholic' looks like the crossword's usual single letter and is fodder entire, apostrophe included. Count the letters first and the fifteen come out exactly.

18 across TRASH CAN

New York garbage collector spots 100 leather jackets (8)

Definition: New York garbage collector · container + charade

New York garbage collector -> TRASH CAN: the American name for a dustbin, the container that garbage is collected in rather than the man who empties it.

  • leather → TAN tan is both the colour of leather and what is done to a hide to make it, and it opens up after its first letter
  • spots → RASH a crop of spots on the skin is a rash
  • 100 → C C is the Roman numeral for a hundred
  • jackets — A jacket goes round whatever it covers, so the word before this one closes over the two pieces named between them.

The definition sounds like a person and is a thing: American English calls the bin this, and 'New York' is the flag saying the word is not the British one.

20 across ASTUTE

Clever like two thirds of private pupils (6)

Definition: Clever · charade + deletion

Clever -> ASTUTE: astute is clever in the shrewd, practical sense, quick to see where an advantage lies, rather than clever at book learning.

  • like → AS 'quick like lightning' and 'quick as lightning' say the same thing
  • private pupils → TUTE pupils taught one to one are tutees, and only four of those six letters are wanted
  • two thirds of — Two thirds of a six-letter word is four letters, and a fraction counts from the front unless the clue says otherwise.

'Private' pulls towards a soldier or a fee-paying school and is neither: it describes how the pupils are taught, one at a time by a tutor.

21 across SEASONABLE

Marine against wearing fur appropriate for the time of year (10)

Definition: appropriate for the time of year · charade + container

appropriate for the time of year -> SEASONABLE: seasonable weather is what the season leads you to expect, unlike 'seasonal', which only means it happens then.

  • Marine → SEA marine means belonging to the sea, and the three letters are laid down first
  • fur → SABLE sable is the dark fur of a marten, prized for coats, and it opens after its first letter
  • against → ON an attack on somebody is an attack against them, so the small word swaps straight in
  • wearing — You are inside what you wear, so the word in front of this one is put on by the word after it and ends up in the middle.

The animal-rights surface is all misdirection: nobody is wearing any fur, and 'Marine' is the adjective for the sea rather than a soldier in uniform.

24 across RUFF

Long hair round neck unshaven, you'd say (4)

Definition: Long hair round neck · homophone

Long hair round neck -> RUFF: a ruff is the collar of longer hair or feathers standing out round the neck of an animal or a bird.

  • unshaven → RUFF a chin nobody has shaved feels rough to the touch, and that word said aloud is spelled another way entirely
  • you'd say — What you would say is the spoken version, so the answer is written from the sound of the word beside it rather than its spelling.

The Elizabethan collar is the picture most solvers reach for, and the definition wants the animal sense: a lion or a bird carries one too.

26 across ESPIAL

Notice doctor sip ale (6)

Definition: Notice · anagram

Notice -> ESPIAL: espial is the noun for catching sight of something, the act of espying it, so it is a notice in the sense of noticing.

  • sip ale → SIPALE six letters of raw material, two ordinary words handed over for their spelling
  • doctor — To doctor something is to tamper with it, and letters that have been tampered with do not stay in the order they were given in.

Four words and nothing wasted: one defines, two are letters, and the fourth is a person in the surface and a verb in the wordplay.

27 across COURTIER

Attendant first to confirm the level we're on? (8)

Definition: Attendant · charade + first letter

Attendant -> COURTIER: a courtier attends a monarch at court as part of the royal household, so attendance is the whole of the job.

  • confirm → C the opening letter of the word and nothing else of it
  • the level we're on → OURTIER a level of a building is a tier, and the one we are standing on is ours: two words run together
  • first to — Whoever is first to do a thing is at the head of the queue, so only the opening letter of the word after it is used.

'Attendant first' reads as one phrase, the senior servant of the household. The definition is that first word alone, and the question mark flags the pun on levels.

Down

1 down GODS

Gallery's shadow pointing north and south (4)

Definition: Gallery · reversal + charade

Gallery -> GODS: the gods is the theatre's highest tier of seats, so the word names that balcony rather than a room full of pictures.

  • shadow → DOG to dog somebody is to shadow them, following wherever they go
  • south → S S is the compass point on a map, one letter for south
  • pointing north — North is the top of a map and the top of a grid, so a down entry's letters that point that way are read from the bottom upwards.

'Gallery' sends you to paintings; the theatre is meant, where the cheap seats sit up under the roof. Only the first piece turns - the compass letter stays where it is.

2 down SHEEP TICKS

These sick people initially suspect bugs on lamb (5,5)

Definition: bugs on lamb · anagram + first letter

bugs on lamb -> SHEEP TICKS: these are the parasites that fasten on to sheep, and a lamb is a young sheep, so the definition names the host.

  • These sick → THESESICK nine letters of raw material: both words are there for their spelling, not their sense
  • people initially → P the opening letter of the word, making the tenth
  • suspect — Something suspect is not what it claims to be, so the letters in front of the word cannot be trusted to stay in the order they arrive in.
  • initially — Initially points at the very start of a word, so nothing but its opening letter joins the pile.

'Sick' looks like the shuffle signal and is fodder instead; the word doing the shuffling is 'suspect'. The count only reaches ten once the borrowed P joins in.

3 down IN CAMERA

Painter entered secretly (2,6)

Definition: secretly · charade

secretly -> IN CAMERA: a hearing held in camera is held in the judge's private room with the public shut out, so it happens in secret.

  • entered → INCAME English inverts the verb for a dramatic arrival: somebody who entered is somebody who in came
  • Painter → RA RA is a Royal Academician, an elected member of Britain's Royal Academy of Arts and the crossword's standard artist

'Painter' looks like the definition and belongs to the wordplay; the single word at the end is what defines. The seam falls inside a word, so nothing marks where one piece stops.

4 down NEAT

Compact bag the smallest article fills (4)

Definition: Compact · container

Compact -> NEAT: both describe something trim and closely packed with nothing spilling over the edges, the way a compact summary is a neat one.

  • bag → NET a net is a bag for catching things, and it opens up between its second and third letters
  • the smallest article → A English has three articles and this is the one that is a single letter long
  • fills — Whatever fills a thing sits inside it, so the letter named here drops into the middle of the piece already built.

'Compact bag' reads as one phrase, a small holdall you might carry. The definition is the first word on its own, and the bag belongs to the wordplay.

5 down LINGER

Large cat cross about new delay (6)

Definition: delay · container

delay -> LINGER: to linger is to stay on longer than you need to, which is delaying by hanging about rather than by putting something off.

  • Large cat cross → LIGER a liger is what you get by crossing a lion with a tiger, and the five letters part company after the second
  • new → N N is the standard single letter for new
  • about — One thing set about another is wrapped round it, so the single letter that follows finishes up in the middle.

'Cross' looks like an anagram signal and means a crossbreed: the big cat is one animal bred from two, and the bad-tempered cat of the surface never existed.

6 down ICON

Picture fraudster's brief résumé? (4)

Definition: Picture · charade

Picture -> ICON: an icon is a painted image of a saint, and now also the little symbol on a screen, so it is a picture in both senses.

  • fraudster's → I the crook is the one talking, and anybody summing themselves up starts with the first-person pronoun
  • brief résumé → CON a whole career in three letters, since to con is to swindle people out of their money

The joke is that the answer is a sentence: a swindler asked for a two-word CV would write exactly this, and it happens to spell a small picture.

9 down LOSE HEART

Stop seeing red card become yellow? (4,5)

Definition: become yellow? · charade

become yellow? -> LOSE HEART: yellow is the old colour of cowardice, so turning yellow is losing your nerve, which is what losing heart amounts to.

  • Stop seeing → LOSE lose sight of something and you have stopped seeing it
  • red card → HEART a pack of cards has two red suits, and this is the one that is not diamonds

The surface is a referee's afternoon, with one card replacing another. The cards are the playing kind, and the definition is only the last two words.

13 down UNHARNESS

With dodges Shane runs clear of tackle (9)

Definition: clear of tackle · anagram

clear of tackle -> UNHARNESS: a working horse's tackle is its harness and traces, so freeing the animal of that gear is what the word describes.

  • Shane runs → SHANERUNS nine letters of raw material: the name and the verb are handed over for their spelling
  • dodges — A dodge is a sudden move to a different spot, so the letters beside the word will not be found where they started.

The rugby picture is the disguise. 'Runs clear of tackle' reads as one phrase from a match report, but the break falls after 'runs', and the tackle is a horse's gear.

14 down GET ON

Step aboard to go places (3,2)

Definition: Step aboard · double definition

Both senses: stepping aboard a bus is getting on it, and somebody going places is getting on in life, which is progress rather than boarding.

  • Step aboard → what you do at the door of a bus: foot on the step and climb in
  • go places → said of a career rather than a journey: to be heading somewhere, making progress in the world

Two everyday senses laid side by side, one about a bus and one about a career, with a single word of joinery between them and no letters to build.

15 down ALLITERATE

Make similar sounds everyone repeats (10)

Definition: Make similar sounds · charade

Make similar sounds -> ALLITERATE: to alliterate is to begin a run of words with the same sound, the way 'sing a song of sixpence' does.

  • everyone → ALL everyone in a room is all of them
  • repeats → ITERATE to iterate is to say or do a thing over again, the formal verb for repeating

'Sounds' is not a signal to listen here - it belongs to the definition. The wordplay is a plain two-piece build, and both pieces are ordinary words.

17 down SPACE OUT

Separate pilot with superior skills boards jet (5,3)

Definition: Separate · container

Separate -> SPACE OUT: to space things out is to set them further apart with gaps between them, which is separating them in the physical sense.

  • jet → SPOUT a jet of water is a spout, and these five letters part company after the second
  • pilot with superior skills → ACE a flier of exceptional skill has been called an ace since the first war fought in the air
  • boards — To board is to step inside a vehicle, so the piece named before this word goes into the middle of the one named after it.

'Separate pilot' reads as an adjective and a noun, one flier on their own. The first word is the definition and a verb, which is the thing to fix before the jet turns into water.

19 down CANDLE

Stick with end of twisted line coming through? (6)

Definition: Stick with end of twisted line coming through? · &lit + container

The whole clue reads straight: a stick of wax with a twisted thread running up inside it and poking out of the top is exactly what the answer names.

  • Stick → CANE a cane is a walking stick, and it splits between its third and fourth letters
  • twisted → D the final letter of the word, and nothing else of it
  • line → L L is the standard abbreviation for line, as in a page reference
  • end of — The end of a word is the letter it finishes on, and that single letter is all this asks for.
  • coming through — Whatever comes through a thing passes into the middle of it, so the letters named go inside the piece already built.

An all-in-one: every word builds the letters and describes the object at the same time. The twisted line is the wick, which really does run up through the middle.

22 down SNIB

Catch brother or sister pinching postman's bottom (4)

Definition: Catch · container + last letter

Catch -> SNIB: a snib is the Scots and northern English word for the small catch or bolt that fastens a door, so the definition is the plain noun.

  • brother or sister → SIB a sib is a sibling, brother or sister alike, and the three letters open after the first
  • postman's → N the last letter of the word, taken on its own
  • pinching — To pinch a thing is to grip it between two sides, and the letters here close on the one letter that follows.
  • bottom — The bottom of a word is where it finishes, so only its final letter is taken.

The saucy surface is the misdirection. 'Bottom' is not the joke it looks like but an instruction, and 'brother or sister' is one short word rather than two people.

23 down BACH

Church musician sands down middle of pew (4)

Definition: Church musician · deletion

Church musician -> BACH: he spent his working life as an organist and cantor writing for Lutheran services, so the definition names the job rather than the fame.

  • sands → BEACH a stretch of sands at the seaside is a beach
  • pew → E the central letter of the three, and it is the one that goes
  • down — Something that has gone down is no longer standing where it was, so the letter named next drops out of the word before it.
  • middle of — The middle of a three-letter word is its second letter, the one with a letter on either side.

'Sands down' reads as one verb, a carpenter at work. It is a noun and an instruction bolted together, which is the whole trick of the clue.

25 down FLEE

Suddenly take off pound, cutting price (4)

Definition: Suddenly take off · container

Suddenly take off -> FLEE: to flee is to run away in a hurry from something dangerous, and 'take off' is the informal way of saying the same thing.

  • price → FEE the price charged for a service is a fee, and it opens after its first letter
  • pound → L L stands for a pound sterling, from the Latin libra that also gives the crossbar of the pound sign
  • cutting — To cut into a thing is to open a gap in it, and the letter named before this word slots into the gap.

'Cutting price' is the phrase the surface wants you to read as one, a shop slashing what it charges. It is two separate jobs, and 'take off' is the runner's sense.
