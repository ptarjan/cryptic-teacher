Across

8 across PRIMATE

Senior clergyman intimate with very becoming maidens (7)

Definition: Senior clergyman · substitution

Senior clergyman → PRIMATE: a primate is the chief bishop of a national church, so the definition names the office rather than any one holder.

  • intimate → PRIVATE an intimate detail is a private one
  • very → V V is the everyday shorthand for very, as in 'v good'
  • maidens → M in cricket a maiden is an over off which no runs are scored, and the scorebook marks it M; the plural belongs to the surface
  • becoming — One thing becoming another has changed into it, so the letter named first is not added but replaced by the letter named second.

'Becoming maidens' reads as a compliment and is an instruction. The clergyman is an archbishop — Canterbury holds the title Primate of All England.

9 across SPANISH

European sleeps on Channel Tunnel line, going back (7)

Definition: European · charade + reversal

European → SPANISH: an adjective defined by the wider class it belongs to, since anything Spanish is European — definition by example.

  • Channel Tunnel line → HSI HS1 is High Speed 1, the rail line from London to the tunnel, and its 1 does duty as the Roman numeral
  • sleeps → NAPS someone who sleeps briefly naps
  • on — In an across clue one thing sitting on another comes after it, so the sleeper follows the railway rather than leading it.
  • going back — An across entry that goes back runs right to left, so the assembly is written out the other way round.

'Sleeps on' sounds like a bad night on a train, which is the disguise. The line wanted is not the Eurostar service but the track it runs on.

10 across PUT PAID TO

Scotch added on drinks dad and I had (3,4,2)

Definition: Scotch · container + charade

Scotch → PUT PAID TO: to scotch a rumour is to finish it off for good, which is exactly what putting paid to something does.

  • added on → PUT TO to put one thing to another is to add it on, and the phrase opens up between its two words
  • dad → PA pa is the familiar word for a father
  • I → I the clue's own word, taken at face value
  • had → D had shrinks to the 'd of I'd, the same contraction spoken aloud every day
  • drinks — To drink something is to take it right inside you, so the phrase beside it swallows the letters that follow.

'Scotch' is a verb here rather than a whisky, which is what the drinking family is there to hide.

11 across BADGE

Harry deprived of king's insignia (5)

Definition: insignia · deletion

insignia → BADGE: insignia are the emblems worn to show rank or membership, and a badge is one such emblem pinned or sewn on.

  • Harry → BADGER to harry someone is to nag and pester them — the verb English borrowed from the black-and-white animal that digs setts
  • king → R R is rex, Latin for king, the letter a monarch signs after their name
  • deprived of — Being deprived of something means it has been taken away from you, so the letter named next never appears.

Harry is a name on the surface — a prince, even, given the king standing next to him — and a plain verb underneath.

12 across TONDO

Part of bolt on doorframe painted round (5)

Definition: painted round · hidden word

painted round → TONDO: a tondo is a picture made in a circular format, so the definition names the work by the shape that defines the form.

  • bolt on doorframe → TONDO five consecutive letters straddle these three words, beginning inside the first
  • Part of — Part of something is a piece cut out of it, so only a stretch of the words that follow is wanted, not their meaning.

The ironmongery is a decoy: nothing is bolted to anything, and the words are only a place for five letters to sit in a row.

13 across SINCERELY

For real crime, Capone cleared out bank (9)

Definition: For real · charade + outer letters

For real → SINCERELY: 'for real' is the colloquial way of saying something is genuinely meant, which is how anything done sincerely is done.

  • crime → SIN a sin is a wrongdoing — the church's word for a crime
  • Capone → CE the first and last letters of the gangster's name, with everything between gone
  • bank → RELY to bank on someone is to rely on them
  • cleared out — A room cleared out has had its contents removed, so the word keeps the two letters at its ends and loses the middle.

'Bank' comes last and looks like the definition, which is where the Chicago money surface is pushing. It is a verb, and the definition is the first two words.

15 across ARC DE TRIOMPHE

Heroic term interchangeable with "Centre of roundabout west of Paris" (3,2,8)

Definition: Heroic term interchangeable with "Centre of roundabout west of Paris" · &lit + anagram + middle letters + first letter

The whole clue reads straight: the name means 'arch of triumph', and the monument stands at the hub of a huge roundabout in western Paris.

  • Heroic term → HEROICTERM ten letters handed over whole, to be rearranged around the three that follow
  • roundabout → DA the middle pair of a ten-letter word
  • Paris → P only the opening letter of the word is taken, the one standing at its western end
  • interchangeable with — Two things that are interchangeable can swap places freely, and so can these letters.
  • Centre of — The centre of a word is what sits at its middle, equally far from either end — here two letters out of ten.
  • west of — West is the left-hand end of an across entry, so it points at the letter a word starts with.

Read once it is a description of a monument; read again the same words are all letters, two of them prised out of the description itself.

18 across BUNDESTAG

British label on pants giving one away in German's house (9)

Definition: German's house · charade + deletion

German's house → BUNDESTAG: the Bundestag is Germany's elected parliamentary chamber, so 'house' means a legislative body rather than a building.

  • British → B B is the standing abbreviation for British
  • pants → UNDES pants are undies, and the word arrives one letter short
  • one → I one is the Roman numeral I, and this is the letter that goes
  • label → TAG the label sewn into a garment is its tag
  • on — In an across clue a thing on another comes after it, so the label lands behind what it is attached to.
  • giving one away — To give something away is to part with it for nothing, so the letter named here is handed over and never appears.

The surface is a laundry complaint. 'House' is the parliamentary sense — the House of Commons sort — and the German one sits in Berlin.

20 across SPRAT

Fish squirted water from mouths across river (5)

Definition: Fish · container

Fish → SPRAT: a sprat is a small sea fish of the herring family, so the general word defines it by the class it belongs to.

  • squirted water from mouths → SPAT mouths that squirted water spat, in the past tense the clue is using
  • river → R R is the map's abbreviation for a river
  • across — A bridge across a river spans it from bank to bank, so the word beside it is laid over the letter and takes it inside.

'Fish squirted water' reads as one statement about a fountain. The definition is the first word by itself and everything after it is an instruction.

21 across SEVER

Separate intro for song on record (5)

Definition: Separate · charade + first letter

Separate → SEVER: to sever is to cut something apart, the abrupt end of the same idea, and both work as transitive verbs.

  • song → S the opening letter of the word, taken on its own
  • on record → EVER the hottest day on record is the hottest one going
  • intro — An intro is the opening of a piece of music, so the word after it gives up its first letter and nothing more.

'Record' looks like it wants one of the crossword's discs — LP, EP, CD are all four letters or fewer. It belongs to a phrase instead.

23 across REPORTAGE

News in the paper concerning Republican in the soup (9)

Definition: News in the paper · charade + container

News in the paper → REPORTAGE: reportage is journalism taken as a body of reported material, rather than any single article or bulletin.

  • concerning → RE RE heads a memo about something — the crossword's usual word for concerning
  • soup → POTAGE potage is a thick soup, straight off a French menu
  • Republican → R R is the standard abbreviation for a Republican in American politics
  • in the soup — Whatever is in the soup is down inside it, so the letter named beside this phrase lands in the middle of the word for it.

In Britain, being in the soup means being in trouble, which is where the politician appears to be. The soup here is French and on the menu.

25 across DISTURB

Offer to cover tracks reflected trouble (7)

Definition: trouble · container + reversal

trouble → DISTURB: to trouble somebody is to disturb them — 'sorry to trouble you' and 'sorry to disturb you' are the same apology.

  • Offer → BID an offer at auction is a bid, and it opens up to take another word inside
  • tracks → RUTS the tracks worn into a muddy lane are ruts
  • cover — To cover something is to lie over it and hide it, so the word beside it closes around the other one.
  • reflected — A reflection shows you the same thing the other way round, so the assembly is read from its far end.

'Cover your tracks' is one idiom on the surface and two separate instructions underneath: one word to do the wrapping, another to be wrapped.

26 across CHABLIS

French drink tea with delight, mostly (7)

Definition: French drink · charade + deletion

French drink → CHABLIS: Chablis is a dry white wine from the northern edge of Burgundy, so the general phrase points at one instance of it.

  • tea → CHA cha is the British slang word for tea, taken from the Chinese name for it
  • delight → BLIS delight is bliss, arriving one letter short
  • mostly — Mostly means nearly all of it, so the word beside it turns up with its final letter missing.

'French drink tea' reads as a single sentence about national habits, and the definition stops after the first two words.

Down

1 down LISTENER-IN

Difficult crossword arrived? Bugger, you might say (8-2)

Definition: Bugger · charade

Bugger → LISTENER-IN: someone who bugs a room does it to eavesdrop, and a listener-in is precisely that person, secretly on the line.

  • Difficult crossword → LISTENER the Listener is the famously hard weekly barred-grid puzzle in the Times, the one solvers name when they mean difficult
  • arrived → IN a train that has arrived is in

'Difficult crossword' sits where a definition usually sits and is the wordplay instead. The bugger is the one who plants the bug, not an exclamation.

2 down VAGABOND

Five Turkish and British commanders in Rover (8)

Definition: Rover · charade

Rover → VAGABOND: a rover is someone who wanders with no fixed home, and that homeless wandering is what the noun names.

  • Five → V V is five in Roman numerals
  • Turkish → AGA an aga was a commander in the Ottoman army, a Turkish military title
  • British commanders → BOND James Bond's rank in the Royal Navy is Commander, which is how his letters are asked for

The Rover is a car on the surface, and 'commanders' does double duty: one is an Ottoman title, the other a naval rank held by a fictional spy.

3 down DEED

Reversible instrument (4)

Definition: instrument · palindrome

instrument → DEED: lawyers call any formal document an instrument, and the deed is the one that hands ownership of property from one party to another.

  • Reversible → this is the whole of the wordplay — the word has to read identically in both directions, which over four letters means the outer pair matches and the inner pair matches
  • instrument → not a violin: in law an instrument is a formal written document that does something, such as transferring a house
  • Reversible — A reversible garment can be worn either way out and is the same garment, so the letters must work from either end.

Two words and one job each: the first says what shape the answer has to have, the second says what it is. The legal sense is the only one that fits.

4 down ASTOUNDING

Miraculous nun is at God's disposal (10)

Definition: Miraculous · anagram

Miraculous → ASTOUNDING: both describe something so far outside the ordinary that it stops you dead, one by wonder and one by disbelief.

  • nun is at God → ASTOUNDING ten letters handed over whole, to be put back in a different order
  • disposal — Getting rid of something means throwing it out, and what is thrown out lands in whatever order it happens to fall.

The surface is a piece of piety and the letters are the point: a convent, a saint and a miracle are all there to disguise ten characters waiting to be shuffled.

5 down MARBLE

Country walk with first half climbing attractive rock (6)

Definition: attractive rock · reversal

attractive rock → MARBLE: the stone is quarried for its looks, which is why sculptors and architects choose it, so 'attractive' is doing real work.

  • Country walk → RAMBLE a ramble is a walk in the country, and the word splits down the middle so that its front three can be turned
  • first half — Half of a six-letter word is three letters, and 'first' says it is the front three that are wanted.
  • climbing — A down entry climbs from the bottom of the grid to the top, so the letters it points at are read upwards, in the opposite order.

It reads as one hillwalking scene, and only the last two words are the definition — everything in front of them is an instruction.

6 down WILD

Fierce wife is lady every so often (4)

Definition: Fierce · charade + alternate letters

Fierce → WILD: a wild animal is an untamed and dangerous one, so the definition takes the savage sense rather than the uncultivated-countryside one.

  • wife → W W is the standing abbreviation for a wife
  • is lady → ILD the first, third and fifth letters of these two words, counted straight through
  • every so often — Something done every so often happens at intervals rather than continuously, so letters are picked at every other position.

'Wife is lady' is gallantry on the surface and raw material underneath: the first word is an abbreviation, the other two are only a source of letters.

7 down CHEERY

Bright church strangely frightening (6)

Definition: Bright · charade

Bright → CHEERY: of a person or a room, bright means lively and good-humoured rather than well-lit, and that is the sense wanted.

  • church → CH CH is the abbreviation printed against a church on an Ordnance Survey map
  • strangely frightening → EERY something strange and frightening is eery, the older spelling of the word

'Strangely' begs to be an instruction to shuffle. It is nothing of the kind: it joins the word after it to make one plain synonym.

8 down PUPATE

Young animal had to enter cocoon (6)

Definition: enter cocoon · charade

enter cocoon → PUPATE: a caterpillar pupates when it seals itself up to transform, and spinning a cocoon around itself is how many species do it.

  • Young animal → PUP a pup is a young dog or seal
  • had → ATE someone who had lunch ate it — the past tense of the meal, not of owning

Two everyday words laid end to end with no indicator anywhere to say so, which is what makes the join hard to see.

13 down SET STORE BY

Think a lot of groups ran past? (3,5,2)

Definition: Think a lot of · charade

Think a lot of → SET STORE BY: to set store by something is to rate it highly, an idiom that only ever appears whole.

  • groups → SETS things that belong together form a set
  • ran → TORE to tear off somewhere is to run at speed
  • past → BY walking past someone is walking by them

The wordplay's three words are not the answer's three words: the enumeration moves the first join one letter along, and that shift is what makes the phrase hard to see coming.

14 down ETHEREALLY

Editor's lead article in fact without substance (10)

Definition: without substance · charade + first letter

without substance → ETHEREALLY: something done ethereally is done with an airy, insubstantial lightness, which is what having no substance describes.

  • Editor's → E only the opening letter of the word is taken
  • article → THE the definite article, spelled out in full rather than abbreviated
  • in fact → REALLY both phrases introduce the truth behind an appearance
  • lead — The lead is the front position, so the word beside it gives up the single letter that leads it off.

An editor is usually ED in a crossword, which is one letter too many here, and the newsroom surface exists to make you reach for it.

16 down MISTRIAL

Strong cold wind around island impaired hearing (8)

Definition: impaired hearing · container

impaired hearing → MISTRIAL: a trial is a court hearing, and a mistrial is one invalidated by an error in the proceedings — a hearing gone wrong.

  • Strong cold wind → MISTRAL the mistral is the fierce cold wind that funnels down the Rhône valley into southern France
  • island → I I is how an island is labelled on a map
  • around — What goes around something encircles it, so the word beside it opens up and takes the single letter into its middle.

'Impaired hearing' reads as deafness. The hearing is a court's, not an ear's, and the wind has blown in from a weather forecast.

17 down STRESS

States positively upset, losing American accent (6)

Definition: accent · deletion + reversal

accent → STRESS: in speech the accent falls on a syllable, and that emphasis is its stress — the pronunciation sense, not the regional one.

  • States positively → ASSERTS to state something positively is to assert it
  • American → A A is the standard abbreviation for American, and it is the letter that goes
  • upset — Something upset has been tipped over, and in a down entry that means reading the letters from the bottom of the grid to the top.
  • losing — To lose something is to be left without it, so the letter named next never joins the others.

'American accent' is the trap: the two words look like one phrase and belong to opposite halves of the clue, one a letter to drop and one the definition.

18 down BESIDE

Too near (6)

Definition: Too · double definition

near → BESIDE is standing at the side of something; Too → BESIDE is the older 'in addition' sense, the one that usually carries an S today.

  • Too → the sense 'in addition, over and above what has already been said'
  • near → the everyday positional sense: at the side of something, right next to it

Two words and no wordplay at all, which is why the clue looks unfinished: one is an archaic adverb, the other the preposition everybody knows.

19 down EARFUL

Reprimand headless chicken? (6)

Definition: Reprimand · deletion

Reprimand → EARFUL: giving somebody an earful is subjecting them to a loud telling-off, so the noun names the scolding itself.

  • chicken → FEARFUL calling somebody chicken says they are fearful, and the word then loses its first letter
  • headless — A headless word has lost the letter at its front, which in a down entry is the one at the top.

A headless chicken is a proverbial picture of panic, which is exactly what the setter wants you to see instead of an instruction to behead a word.

22 down VAST

End of manoeuvres in tank covering great area (4)

Definition: covering great area · container + last letter

covering great area → VAST: something vast is immense in extent, so a phrase about how much ground it covers stands in for the adjective.

  • tank → VAT a vat is the great tub a brewery keeps its liquid in
  • manoeuvres → S the final letter of the word, taken on its own
  • End of — The end of a word is the letter you reach last, and only that one is taken.
  • in — A piece that goes in something drops inside it, splitting the other word apart rather than joining it at either end.

The definition is the last three words, which read as part of the description of the tank — that is what makes the clue look like one military sentence.

24 down PACE

Lick a bit of expensive cappuccino up (4)

Definition: Lick · charade + first letter + reversal

Lick → PACE: 'at a fair lick' is British for at a fair speed, so the slang noun and the plain one name the same thing.

  • expensive → E the opening letter of the word, and no more of it
  • cappuccino → CAP a cap is what a coffee bar calls a cappuccino, the front of the word standing in for the whole
  • a bit of — A bit of a word is a piece broken off its front, so only the opening letters of what follows are wanted.
  • up — A down entry read upwards runs from the bottom of the grid to the top, so the letters go in the other order.

'Lick' at the head of the clue looks like a verb, someone stealing a mouthful of froth, and it is a noun about speed — the joke the whole surface rests on.
