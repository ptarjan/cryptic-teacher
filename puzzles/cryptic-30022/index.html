8 across FREE-FORM

Unstructured feature of shelf reef or massif (4-4)

Definition: Unstructured · hidden word

Free-form means shaped to no set pattern, the way a free-form jazz solo follows no plan, which is what unstructured says.

  • shelf reef or massif → FREEFORM eight letters run on in order across these three landforms, crossing both gaps
  • feature of — A feature of something is a part sitting inside it, so the words that follow are holding the answer's letters in an unbroken run.

A geologist reeling off landforms: shelf, reef, massif.

Three landforms in a row read as a list, and a list is the easiest thing to skim — which is exactly how eight consecutive letters go unnoticed.

9 across RUFFE

Agitate live, dumped freshwater fish (5)

Definition: freshwater fish · deletion

The ruffe is a small spiny perch found in European rivers and canals, so 'freshwater fish' is its straight dictionary description.

  • Agitate → RUFFLE to ruffle is to agitate — to disturb the smoothness of something
  • live → L l = live, as marked on an electrical wire; this is the letter thrown away
  • dumped — To dump something is to get rid of it, so the letter named just before goes out of the longer word.

A live catch stirred up and then thrown back in the water.

L for live comes off a wire, not out of the water, so the surface's living fish is a decoy. The ruffe itself is a small spiny perch of European rivers.

10 across ATOP

Great opera houses going under? The opposite (4)

Definition: going under? The opposite · hidden word

Atop means on top of, the exact reverse of under, which is what the setter's 'The opposite' tells you to take.

  • Great opera → ATOP four letters sit consecutively across the gap between the two words
  • houses — To house something is to keep it inside, so these two words are holding the answer's letters within them.

Grand opera houses in danger of sinking.

'The opposite' flips the definition, so the answer means over rather than under. 'Houses' looks like the noun in 'opera houses' and is doing a verb's work.

11 across BLANK VERSE

Ken’s verbal rendering of ‘To be, or not to be’, say (5,5)

Definition: ‘To be, or not to be’, say · anagram

'To be, or not to be' is unrhymed iambic pentameter, so it defines the answer as an example of the form rather than a synonym for it.

  • Ken’s verbal → KENSVERBAL anagram fodder, ten letters, the S of the apostrophe included
  • rendering — A rendering is a fresh version of the same material, so the letters are re-set rather than left in the order handed over.

Ken delivering the Hamlet soliloquy aloud in his own style.

'Verbal rendering' pulls hard towards a sound-alike and 'say' towards an example; only the second is doing a job, marking Hamlet's line as one specimen of the form.

12 across SNOBBY

Condescending southern outdated name given to Clark? (6)

Definition: Condescending · charade

Snobby is looking down on people from a supposed social height, which is the everyday sense of condescending.

  • southern → S s = southern, the compass abbreviation
  • outdated name given to Clark → NOBBY Nobby is the traditional British nickname attached to anyone surnamed Clark, the way Chalky goes with White

An old-fashioned nickname handed out to a man called Clark.

Nobby for Clark belongs to the British habit of automatic surname nicknames — Dusty Miller, Chalky White — now heard mostly in old service slang.

14 across THROTTLE

Rubbish, vacuous tutorial fills the silence (8)

Definition: silence · container + charade + outer letters

To throttle is to choke off, and a throttled sound stops, so 'silence' as a verb swaps straight in.

  • the → THE the word itself, used literally — and it is the thing being filled
  • Rubbish → ROT rot = rubbish, in the sense of nonsense talked
  • vacuous tutorial → TL TutoriaL hollowed out keeps its first and last letters only
  • vacuous — Vacuous means empty in the middle, so the word it describes gives up its insides and keeps only its two ends.
  • fills — What fills a thing goes inside it, so everything built up before this word drops into the middle of the word after it.

Nonsense from an empty tutorial filling up what ought to be quiet.

'Fills the silence' reads as one phrase, so the split between 'fills' and 'the' is invisible, and the definition is the verb — to throttle a voice is to silence it.

16 across RECTIFY

City’s upset with ref, right? (7)

Definition: right · anagram

To rectify is to put something right, the verb sense of 'right' as in righting a wrong.

  • City’s → CITY the four letters of the word itself; the ’s is only 'is'
  • ref → REF three more letters taken straight from the clue
  • upset — Upset means knocked over and spilled, and letters spilled out of a word do not land in the order they started in.

Supporters of City furious with the referee, and asking whether they have a point.

'Right?' at the end reads as the surface appealing for agreement, which hides its real job as the definition — and it is the verb, to put right.

18 across ONE-STAR

Martin’s residence in row that’s not highly rated? (3-4)

Definition: not highly rated · container

A one-star hotel or review sits at the bottom of the rating scale, so 'not highly rated' is what the phrase means.

  • row → OAR an oar is what you row with, so the tool stands in for the verb
  • Martin’s residence → NEST a martin is a bird — house martins build under the eaves
  • in — 'In' puts one thing within another, so the piece named before it sits inside the letters of the word that follows.

Martin's house on a terrace that reviewers think little of.

Martin is a bird, not a neighbour, and 'row' is a line of houses on the surface while the wordplay wants the rowing.

21 across ASSIGNED

Transferred in the manner indicated (8)

Definition: Transferred · charade

To assign something is to transfer it to someone else — the legal sense, as when a lease is assigned to a new tenant.

  • in the manner → AS 'as' means in the manner of — 'as a soldier would'
  • indicated → SIGNED to sign to someone is to indicate something to them

Property handed over exactly the way the instructions specified.

The whole thing reads as a single instruction, which is what hides the join between 'manner' and 'indicated'. Signed for indicated is the sense in 'signed to him to stop'.

23 across VIEWER

One struggling to comprehend the Guardian gets Observer (6)

Definition: Observer · container

A viewer observes — someone watching television is an observer of it, which is the general word for anyone looking on.

  • One struggling → VIER to vie is to struggle against a rival, so one who vies is a vier; it opens up to take the insertion
  • the Guardian → WE a newspaper talking about itself says 'we', and the paper here is the one printing the puzzle
  • to comprehend — To comprehend is to take in and hold, the sense inside 'comprehensive', so the next piece goes within the one before it.

A reader who cannot make sense of one newspaper switching to another.

Both titles are set in italics, which sells the newsagent's shelf completely: underneath, the Guardian is only the first-person 'we' any paper uses of itself, and Observer is a plain synonym.

24 across EXPECTANCY

Anticipation’s useless except with name-dropping former First Lady (10)

Definition: Anticipation · anagram + deletion + charade

Expectancy is the state of expecting something to come, which is what anticipation names: the waiting, not the thing waited for.

  • except → EXPECT the six letters of EXCEPT re-set, the C and the P trading places
  • name → N n = name, and this is the letter dropped from what follows
  • former First Lady → ANCY Nancy Reagan was First Lady in the 1980s, and her name arrives minus that initial
  • useless — Something called useless is no good as it stands, and letters written off that way get re-set into another order.
  • name-dropping — 'Dropping' announces a removal and 'name' says which letter goes — the single letter that stands for it.

Anticipation counting for nothing unless you can drop a former First Lady's name.

'Except' looks like a link word carving something out of the clue, and it is the fodder itself — the one word the letters actually come from.

26 across BODY

Stiff, wow, having died inside! (4)

Definition: Stiff · container

A stiff is slang for a corpse, and a body is exactly that — the dead one rather than the living.

  • wow → BOY 'Boy!' is an exclamation of astonishment, doing the same job as 'wow'
  • died → D d = died, as written on a gravestone or in a genealogy
  • inside — 'Inside' says where the letter just named goes: into the middle of the word before it, not on the end.

Astonishment at someone having died indoors.

'Stiff' is the slang noun for a corpse, not the adjective the comma invites, and the exclamation mark keeps you hearing 'wow' as feeling rather than as three letters.

27 across SOLAR

Also diverted river as energy source (5)

Definition: energy source · anagram + charade

Solar means of the sun, and the sun is the energy source meant — the adjective standing in for solar power the way it does in everyday speech.

  • Also → SOLA the four letters of ALSO re-ordered
  • river → R r = river, the standard map abbreviation
  • diverted — A diverted river is sent a different way, and letters diverted likewise leave the course the clue set them on.

A river rerouted to generate power.

Hydro is the power the surface promises and the answer is the other renewable, which is the whole misdirection; the river shrinks to a single map abbreviation.

28 across SAGACITY

Wisdom of imposing flag on a large community (8)

Definition: Wisdom · charade

Sagacity is shrewd practical judgement, so it means wisdom in the sense of knowing what to do rather than of book learning.

  • flag → SAG to flag is to droop or sag, the way energy does late in the day
  • a → A used literally
  • large community → CITY a city is a large community
  • imposing — To impose one thing on another is to lay it over the top, so the piece it names goes in front of everything that follows.

A flag planted over a large town, as a show of authority.

Flag is the verb here in its drooping sense, not the cloth the surface runs up a pole, and the answer's meaning has nothing to do with either.

1 down PRETENCE

Cover figure in fancy crepe (8)

Definition: Cover · anagram

A pretence is a cover — the false front someone puts up to hide behind, the sense in 'cover story'.

  • figure → TEN a figure is a numeral, and ten is the one the setter wants
  • crepe → CREPE five more letters lifted straight out of the clue to join them
  • fancy — 'Fancy' is the adjective for something got up ornately, and letters dressed up that way do not stay in the plain order the clue prints them.

A figure wrapped in expensive crepe cloth, covered up by the fabric.

'Figure' is a body shape on the surface and a number underneath, and 'Cover' is the noun, not the verb the dressmaking picture wants.

2 down BEEP

Live recording’s electronic alert (4)

Definition: electronic alert · charade

A beep is the short electronic note a machine sounds to warn you, so 'electronic alert' is its plain description.

  • Live → BE to live is to be
  • recording’s → EP an EP is a recording, the extended-play disc that sits between a single and an album; the ’s is only 'is'

A gig captured live, electronic warning tone and all.

'Live' reads as the adjective of live music and is the verb here. EP for a recording is worth keeping — setters reach for it whenever music is mentioned.

3 down COWBOY

Devious worker, bully and lout getting a rise (6)

Definition: Devious worker · charade + reversal

Cowboy is British slang for a dishonest or incompetent tradesman, which is what 'devious worker' names.

  • bully → COW to cow someone is to bully them into submission
  • lout → BOY a yob is a lout, and this entry climbs, so YOB is read upwards
  • getting a rise — Something getting a rise is travelling upwards, and in a down entry a word written upwards is read from the bottom, so its letters arrive back to front.

A bully and a lout together, trying to get a rise out of somebody.

In British usage a cowboy is a shoddy, dishonest tradesman — the cowboy builder who takes the money and botches the job — and none of the Wild West is in play.

4 down IMPASTO

Laying it on thick: ‘One’s too old for love’ (7)

Definition: Laying it on thick · charade

Impasto is paint applied in ridges thick enough to stand off the canvas, so 'laying it on thick' is literally what the technique is.

  • One’s → IM 'one' speaking of itself says I'm
  • too old → PAST someone past it is too old for whatever follows
  • love → O love is nothing in tennis scoring, and nothing is written as O

Somebody being written off as too old for romance, and saying so with relish.

The definition is the painter's technique, not the exaggeration the quoted grumble suggests. Love for O comes off a tennis scoreboard, where love is a score of nothing.

5 down TREK

Hiker told about covering arduous walk (4)

Definition: arduous walk · hidden word + reversal

A trek is a long hard journey made on foot — the effort is built into the word, which is why nobody calls a stroll one.

  • Hiker told → TREK four consecutive letters straddle the gap between these two words, running the other way
  • about — 'About' means turned round, as in an about turn, so the letters are read the opposite way from how the clue prints them.
  • covering — One thing covering another conceals it, and these words are lying over the answer's letters rather than saying them.

Someone warning a hiker about the punishing walk ahead.

'Told' invites you to hunt for a sound-alike; it is there so that four letters can run backwards across the join between two ordinary words.

6 down AFTERTASTE

Lingering sensation of behind getting smack (10)

Definition: Lingering sensation · charade

An aftertaste is the flavour that stays in the mouth once the food has gone, which is a sensation that lingers.

  • behind → AFTER behind = after, in the sense of following something
  • smack → TASTE a smack of something is a trace of its flavour — 'a smack of garlic'

The sting that lingers after a slap on the backside.

Smack is the trap: the surface hears a slap, while the word also means a faint flavour, which is the reading the answer needs.

7 down VESSEL

Shelves heaving – husband’s abandoned junk? (6)

Definition: junk · anagram + deletion

A junk is a Chinese sailing ship, so the definition works by example: one particular kind of the thing the answer names.

  • husband → H h = husband, and this is the letter that walks out
  • Shelves → SELVES SHELVES without that H leaves six letters to be rearranged
  • heaving — A heaving deck pitches everything on it about, so the letters it describes will not be lying in the order given.
  • abandoned — What is abandoned is left behind, so the letter named beside it is dropped before anything else happens.

Shelves buckling under a load, and the junk a departed husband left behind.

'Junk' is the trap — not the surface's rubbish but the Chinese sailing ship, which is why the setter adds a question mark.

13 down BOTTICELLI

Excessive to introduce diamonds into construction of Bill the artist? (10)

Definition: the artist · anagram + container

Botticelli painted the Birth of Venus, so 'the artist' names him by example — one member of the trade, flagged by the question mark.

  • Excessive → OTT OTT is over the top — excessive
  • diamonds → ICE ice is thieves' slang for diamonds
  • Bill → BLLI the four letters of BILL rebuilt, and the pair above drop in after the first of them
  • to introduce — To introduce something is to bring it in, so the pieces named before this word are put inside rather than beside.
  • into — 'Into' names where that insertion lands — within the letters of the word that follows, not next to them.
  • construction of — A construction is something built out of parts, so the name after it is re-laid brick by brick instead of kept as it stands.

Diamonds being set into some lavish thing built for a man named Bill.

OTT for excessive spells out over the top, and ICE for diamonds is the slang; both are pieces a setter expects you to keep ready, and the question mark admits the painter is one artist among many.

19 down ANECDOTE

A relative (not I) appropriating little Dorothy’s account (8)

Definition: account · charade + deletion + container

An anecdote is a short account of something that happened, 'account' in the telling sense rather than the banking one.

  • A → A used literally, the letter A
  • relative → NIECE a niece is a relative
  • (not I) → the I named in brackets comes out, leaving four letters to close round the next piece
  • little Dorothy’s → DOT Dot is the everyday shortening of Dorothy, which is what 'little' asks for
  • (not I) — The bracketed 'not' excludes the letter it names, so the I comes out of the word standing in front of it.
  • appropriating — To appropriate something is to take it in and keep it, so what is named next is swallowed inside what has been built.

A relative helping themselves to little Dorothy's savings.

'Account' is money on the surface and a told story underneath — the sense in 'by all accounts' — while the bracketed 'not I' reads as the relative protesting innocence.

20 down ODDNESS

Spare cape’s peculiarity (7)

Definition: peculiarity · charade

Oddness is the state of being strange, and a peculiarity is that same quality in something — both name a departure from the normal.

  • Spare → ODD an odd sock or an odd hour is a spare one, left over
  • cape’s → NESS a ness is a headland or cape; the ’s is only joinery

A spare cape, and whatever is strange about it.

Ness for a headland survives mainly in British place names — Sheerness, Dungeness — and setters use it constantly. 'Spare' meaning left over is the other step.

22 down SEXISM

It is male discrimination? (6)

Definition: discrimination · charade

Sexism is discrimination on grounds of sex, so the definition is the general noun with the ground left for the wordplay to supply.

  • It → SEX 'it' is the coy old euphemism, as in 'she's got It'
  • is → IS the word itself, taken as it stands
  • male → M m = male, the standard abbreviation

Someone insisting that discrimination is a male speciality.

The clue is an argument you can agree or disagree with, and that keeps you reading it as a sentence instead of as a sum of three short pieces.

23 down VOYAGE

Goya worked in extremely venerable passage (6)

Definition: passage · anagram + container + outer letters

A voyage is a passage by sea — 'passage' in the sense of a crossing made, as in booking passage to New York.

  • extremely venerable → VE the extremes of VenerablE — its opening and closing letters
  • Goya → OYAG the painter's four letters reshuffled, then set down between those two
  • worked — Metal that has been worked has been beaten into a different shape, and the name beside this word is reshaped the same way.
  • in — 'In' drops the reshaped letters inside the piece that follows rather than setting them alongside it.
  • extremely — 'Extremely' points at the far ends of the word after it, so only its first and last letters are taken.

Goya at work during an extremely distinguished period.

Goya reads as a painter doing a painter's job, which is why his name looks like a subject rather than four loose letters waiting to be moved.

25 down TURN BACK

Spooner’s Rivulet Sailing Course retreat (4,4)

Definition: retreat · spoonerism

To turn back is to retreat — to give up the advance and go the way you came, which is what a retreat is in plain terms.

  • Rivulet → TURN a burn is a small stream in Scotland and the north of England; said aloud it hands its opening B over and takes a T in exchange
  • Sailing Course → BACK a tack is the line a sailing boat holds between changes of direction; its opening T is the sound that goes the other way
  • Spooner’s — The Reverend Spooner was famous for exchanging the opening sounds of two words, so the pair named next swap the noises at their fronts.

The Reverend Spooner naming a stream and the line a boat is sailing.

Say BURN TACK aloud and move the two front noises across, and the pair comes out as an ordinary phrase — the capital letters on Rivulet and Sailing Course are pure surface dressing.
