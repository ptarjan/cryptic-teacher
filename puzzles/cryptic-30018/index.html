1 across SCIENCE FICTION

Type of novel ice, not scenic if cracked (7,7)

Definition: Type of novel · anagram

'Type of novel' asks for the name of a genre, and the answer is the genre of imagined technology, space travel and futures that have not happened.

  • ice, not scenic if → ICENOTSCENICIF anagram fodder, 14 letters, comma and all
  • cracked — Something cracked has broken apart, and pieces that have broken apart do not stay in the order they were laid out in.

A novel set on ice that is nothing much to look at and has started to break up.

Fourteen letters of fodder is a lot to shuffle, so count them against the enumeration before you start. The comma after 'ice' is punctuation, and a setter is free to ignore it.

9 across REEVE

Ray meeting first lady magistrate (5)

Definition: magistrate · charade

The word names the medieval English officer who kept order in a manor, town or shire, so 'magistrate' fits in its older administrative sense.

  • Ray → RE ray is the second note of the sol-fa scale, and setters spell it RE
  • first lady → EVE the first lady of Genesis
  • meeting — Two things that meet are set side by side, so these pieces are simply laid end to end in the order given.

Ray is introduced to the first lady, who sits as a magistrate.

Doh, ray, me: the sol-fa scale is a standing source of two-letter chunks, and 'first lady' is the oldest one there is rather than a president's wife.

10 across ITINERANT

Vagrant makes one point before shouting (9)

Definition: Vagrant · charade

A vagrant is somebody with no fixed home who moves from place to place, and the answer is the noun for exactly such a wanderer.

  • one → I the Roman numeral for one
  • point → TINE a tine is the point of a fork or an antler
  • shouting → RANT to rant is to shout on and on
  • before — 'Before' fixes the order: what has been named ahead of it goes in front of what is named after it.

A drifter gets one argument in before the shouting starts.

'Point' looks like a compass letter — N, S, E or W, a setter's usual meaning — and it is a prong instead, which is four letters rather than one.

11 across OVERSPEND

Concluded special finish to exhaust (9)

Definition: exhaust · charade

To exhaust a fund is to use it all up and then some, and the answer is the verb for running through money past the point where any is left.

  • Concluded → OVER when something is concluded it is over
  • special → SP standard abbreviation
  • finish → END a finish is an end

A special race concluded with a finish that left the runners drained.

Two of the three pieces mean 'end' — 'concluded' and 'finish' — which is what makes the clue read as one idea instead of three instructions in a row.

12 across PAYER

The old boring mean settler (5)

Definition: settler · container

To settle a bill is to pay it, so 'settler' names the person doing the paying — a sense that survives mainly in accounts and in crosswords.

  • mean → PAR par is the mean, the average, the standard figure
  • The old → YE ye is the old way of writing 'the', as in Ye Olde Tea Shoppe
  • boring — To bore into something is to drill into its middle, so the drilling word ends up inside the drilled one.

A dull, stingy old colonist.

'Settler' looks like a colonist and 'mean' like stingy. Both are the wrong sense, and the surface is built so that the two wrong senses agree with each other.

13 across SMASH

Ruins potatoes? (5)

Definition: Ruin · charade

To ruin something is to wreck it, and the answer is the blunter verb for the same act — breaking a thing apart beyond repair.

  • Ruins → S once 'Ruin' has been taken as the definition, the S left on the end of that word is the wordplay's first letter
  • potatoes → MASH mash is what boiled potatoes become

A question about what ruins a potato.

The word 'Ruins' is split down the middle: 'Ruin' is the definition and its final S joins the wordplay. Setters do that with a verb ending whenever the surface will carry it.

15 across MASTERFUL

Expert ship’s captain mostly occupied (9)

Definition: Expert · charade + deletion

'Expert' is an adjective here — skilled, in command of the craft — and the answer is the adjective for doing a thing with complete authority.

  • ship’s captain → MASTER the master of a ship is her captain
  • occupied → FUL FULL means occupied, and it arrives a letter short
  • mostly — Mostly means all but the last bit, so the word it governs turns up with its final letter left off.

A ship's captain who is busy most of the time.

'Mostly' sits in front of the word it cuts rather than behind it, so it reads as part of the captain's timetable instead of as an instruction to trim.

18 across ANTIPASTO

Liking the present, love? It’s a starter (9)

Definition: a starter · charade

A starter is the first course of a meal, and the answer is the Italian name for exactly that course.

  • Liking the present → ANTIPAST somebody keen on now is set against what has gone: ANTI the PAST
  • love → O love is a tennis score of nothing, written as a nought

'Do you like the present, love?' — somebody asking about a gift.

'Present' is a parcel in the surface and an era in the wordplay, and 'love' is a term of endearment right up to the moment it turns into a nought.

19 across SIREN

Alarm son accompanying trim Papas’ woman (5)

Definition: Alarm · charade + deletion

An alarm is the warning noise a device makes, and the answer names that wailing device on an ambulance roof or a factory wall.

  • son → S standard abbreviation
  • trim Papas’ woman → IREN the first name of Irene Papas, cut short by a letter
  • accompanying — One thing accompanying another travels alongside it, so this letter is simply placed next to what follows.
  • trim — To trim something is to cut a bit off its end, so the name arrives with its final letter gone.

A son out walking with a trim woman of the Papas family.

Irene Papas was the Greek actress of Zorba the Greek and The Guns of Navarone. A surname pointing at a first name is a standard way of getting a common word out of a famous person.

21 across TUTOR

Coach tyres unable to operate reliably at the beginning (5)

Definition: Coach · first letters

To coach someone is to instruct them one to one, and the answer is the noun for the person who does that private teaching.

  • tyres unable to operate reliably → TUTOR the initial letters of these five words, taken in order
  • at the beginning — The phrase is about where to look rather than what to think: the opening letter of every word it governs.

The tyres on a coach that will not work properly when it first sets off.

'Coach' does two jobs: the bus the tyres belong to, and the person who teaches. It is the only word in the clue not lending a letter, which is what makes it the definition.

23 across TARANTISM

Lacking love, Chinese philosophy embraces yelling and uncontrolled dancing (9)

Definition: uncontrolled dancing · deletion + container

The answer names that epidemic dancing mania, so 'uncontrolled dancing' describes the condition itself rather than standing in as a synonym.

  • Chinese philosophy → TAOISM Taoism is the Chinese philosophy of the Way
  • love → love is nothing in tennis, so the letter taken out is the O
  • yelling → RANT a rant is sustained yelling
  • Lacking — To lack something is to be without it, so the named letter is simply missing from the word that lacks it.
  • embraces — An embrace closes round a person, so the word doing the embracing wraps itself round the other one.

A loveless Chinese philosophy taking in a good deal of yelling and wild dancing.

Tarantism was the dancing mania of southern Italy, blamed on the tarantula's bite and treated by dancing it out of the system. The tarantella comes from the same story.

25 across MARACAIBO

Instrument associated with West African city and lake (9)

Definition: city and lake · charade

The name belongs to both a Venezuelan city and the vast lake beside it, so one word answers 'city and lake' at once.

  • Instrument → MARACA a maraca is the gourd rattle shaken in Latin American bands
  • West African → IBO the Ibo are a people of southern Nigeria

A musical instrument with some West African connection.

Maracaibo is Venezuela's second city, standing on the great lake of the same name, so 'city and lake' is one word doing two jobs rather than two answers.

26 across DODGE

Move out of the way of car (5)

Definition: Move out of the way · double definition

The verb means to move sharply aside to avoid something; the same word is an American car marque, so 'car' defines by naming one make.

  • Move out of the way → the verb: to swerve aside to avoid something
  • car → a make of American car, the second definition

Stepping smartly aside to avoid a car.

The clue reads as one continuous action — a person avoiding a vehicle — and it is two separate definitions with nothing but 'of' holding them together.

27 across THERMAL SPRINGS

Sounds right wearing underwear at first in hot baths (7,7)

Definition: hot baths · charade + container

'Hot baths' describes naturally heated pools people bathe in, which is what these are: water warmed underground and rising to the surface.

  • underwear → THERMALS thermals are the insulating long johns and vests worn against the cold
  • Sounds → PINGS pings are small sharp sounds
  • right → R standard abbreviation
  • wearing — You put clothes on around yourself, so the piece that is wearing something finishes up inside it.
  • at first — 'At first' is about position and not about letters: the piece it follows is placed at the front of the answer.

It sounds right to keep your underwear on, at least at first, in the hot baths.

'Sounds' wants to be a homophone instruction, the commonest trap in the language of these clues. It is a plural noun instead — the small noises a machine makes.

1 down SERIOUS ATTEMPT

Determined effort to meet upstairs for massage (7,7)

Definition: Determined effort · anagram

A determined effort is a try made in earnest: the first word of the answer carries 'determined', the second carries 'effort', both as plain synonyms.

  • to meet upstairs → TOMEETUPSTAIRS anagram fodder, 14 letters
  • massage — To massage figures is to work them over until they come out looking different, which is what happens to these letters.

Two people arranging to meet upstairs for a massage.

'Upstairs' in a down clue looks like an instruction to run letters up the grid towards the top. It is fodder, and every word after the definition goes into the mix.

2 down INELEGANT

Unsophisticated member enthralled by trendy European worker (9)

Definition: Unsophisticated · charade + container

Plain synonym: 'unsophisticated' and the answer both describe a person or thing that lacks polish, grace and social finish.

  • trendy → IN in = fashionable, trendy
  • European → E standard abbreviation
  • member → LEG a member is a limb
  • worker → ANT the worker of crosswords is the ant
  • enthralled by — To be enthralled is to be held captive, so the captive word sits inside the words that are holding it.

An unpolished club member being captivated by a fashionable European colleague.

'Worker' is the ant of a thousand crosswords and 'member' is a limb rather than a person. Two disguises worth owning, because setters reach for both every week.

3 down NEEDS

Press’s outspoken demands (5)

Definition: demands · homophone

'Demands' is the plural noun here — the things somebody must have — and the answer is the everyday word for requirements of that kind.

  • Press’s → NEEDS to press dough is to knead it, so 'presses' is 'kneads', whose opening K is silent
  • outspoken — Outspoken splits into 'out spoken' — said aloud — so this word is handed to you by its sound rather than its spelling.

The newspapers being blunt about what they want.

The apostrophe-s is a verb ending, not a possessive: 'press's' is 'presses'. Bakers press dough under another name, and that name starts with a letter you never hear.

4 down EPIDERMIS

Skin rash oddly deep, I’m starting to itch, unfortunately (9)

Definition: Skin · anagram + alternate letters + first letter

Skin is the everyday word for the body's outer covering, and the answer is the anatomist's name for that same outermost layer.

  • rash oddly → RS the odd letters of R-a-S-h
  • deep, I’m → DEEPIM these six letters as they stand, apostrophe ignored
  • starting to itch → I the first letter of itch
  • oddly — Taken oddly means taken at the odd positions — first, third and so on — so only half the word's letters are handed over.
  • starting to — A word that is only starting has not got past its opening letter, and that letter is the one you take.
  • unfortunately — Something that has gone unfortunately has gone wrong, and letters that have gone wrong are letters out of their proper order.

Someone's deep rash has begun to itch, and it is not going well.

'Skin rash' wants to be the definition and only the first word is. Three separate scraps of wordplay then feed the shuffle, which is why the letter count matters more than the reading.

5 down FRIED

Type of food (French) that is delicious starter (5)

Definition: Type of food · charade + first letter

'Type of food' points at a whole class of dishes named by how they were cooked, which is the sense the answer carries on any menu.

  • (French) → FR standard abbreviation
  • that is → IE i.e. = that is
  • delicious starter → D the first letter of delicious
  • starter — A starter is whatever comes first, so the letter taken is the one that starts the word beside it.

A French dish that is a delicious way to begin a meal.

'That is' is Latin i.e. in disguise, one of the small workhorses of this language. The brackets round 'French' are the setter flagging an abbreviation rather than making an aside.

6 down CHEAPNESS

Guy with cape swallowing drug that 12 might like (9)

Definition: that 12 might like · charade + container

12's answer is someone settling a bill, and low prices are what such a person hopes for — the answer is the noun for goods costing little.

  • Guy → CHAP a chap is a guy, a bloke
  • drug → E E is ecstasy, the crossword drug of choice
  • cape → NESS a ness is a headland — Sheerness, Orford Ness
  • swallowing — Swallowing takes something down inside you, so whatever gets swallowed ends up in the middle of the word doing the swallowing.

A man in a cape gulping down a pill.

The cape in the surface is the superhero kind, and in the wordplay it is coastline: a ness is a headland, which is how British setters clue 'cape' without leaving the country.

7 down ITALY

I can show one score, not 150 (5)

Definition: I · charade + deletion

'I' is the international vehicle registration letter carried by cars from this country, so the plate stands in for the country — definition by abbreviation.

  • one → I the Roman numeral for one
  • score → TALLY a running score is a tally
  • 150 → read it aloud as 'one fifty' — that is one L, since L is the Roman fifty, and one L comes out
  • not — 'Not' is doing subtraction here: whatever follows it is taken back out of what has just been put together.

Somebody offering to produce a scoreline: one, not a hundred and fifty.

'150' is not a numeral to convert but a number to say out loud. One fifty is one L, and a single L is exactly what has to leave.

8 down NATURAL NUMBERS

Maybe Cocaine’s indicative of normal songs (7,7)

Definition: Maybe Cocaine’s · charade

The italics make Cocaine a song title; a song is a number, and this one comes from a plant rather than a lab, so it qualifies by example.

  • normal → NATURAL normal = as nature made it, unforced
  • songs → NUMBERS in showbusiness a song is a number — 'a lively number'

Perhaps the song Cocaine tells you something about ordinary music.

Cocaine is set in italics because it is a title — the JJ Cale song Clapton made famous — and it is a song grown from a plant, which is the whole of the joke.

14 down HYPERICUM

Overexcited setter to noisily approach flowering plant (9)

Definition: flowering plant · charade + homophone

The answer is the botanical genus name of St John's wort, so 'flowering plant' is the general class the genus sits in.

  • Overexcited → HYPER hyper = overexcited
  • setter → I the person setting the clue calls himself I
  • approach → CUM to approach is to come, and that noise is spelt this way in words borrowed from Latin
  • noisily — Something done noisily is done out loud, so this piece reaches you as a sound and has to be respelt before it fits.

An overexcited red setter bounding noisily up to someone.

'Setter' is a gun dog on the surface and the clue's own author underneath. The plant is St John's wort, far better known by that name than by this one.

16 down SHOWROOMS

Places that display musical boards? (9)

Definition: Places that display · charade

'Places that display' is a plain plural definition: the spaces a dealer lays stock out in for customers to walk round and look at.

  • musical → SHOW a musical is a show
  • boards → ROOMS to board somewhere is to room there, to lodge

Somewhere with placards up advertising a musical.

'Boards' looks like the theatre's own word — treading the boards — which keeps it inside the musical's world. It is the lodging verb instead.

17 down FORBIDDEN

In favour of ex-president (Democrat), blocked and debarred (9)

Definition: debarred · charade + container

To be debarred from something is to be shut out of it by rule, which is what the answer says as an adjective: not allowed.

  • In favour of → FOR to be for something is to be in favour of it
  • ex-president → BIDEN Joe Biden, US president until January 2025
  • (Democrat) → D standard abbreviation
  • blocked — A blocked pipe has something stuck part-way along it, and that is where the blocking letter goes.

A former US president, a Democrat, blocked and barred from something.

'Blocked and debarred' reads as a single phrase, so the definition looks two words longer than it is. Only the last word of the clue defines.

22 down TERSE

Some barristers examine brief (5)

Definition: brief · hidden word

Brief means short, and the answer is the adjective for speech pared to the minimum — few words, and a little curt with it.

  • barristers examine → TERSE five consecutive letters straddle the gap between the two words
  • Some — 'Some' asks for a part rather than the whole, so what is wanted is a run of letters inside the words, not their meaning.

Barristers going over a brief between them.

'Brief' is the barrister's bundle of papers on the surface and an adjective underneath, so both readings of the clue stay inside one profession.

23 down TRIAL

Test introduction of tandoori bread in the Middle East (5)

Definition: Test · charade + first letter

'Test' matches the answer's sense of a controlled try-out — a new drug or a vehicle put through its paces — and not the courtroom sense it also carries.

  • introduction of tandoori → T the first letter of tandoori
  • bread in the Middle East → RIAL bread is slang for money, and the rial is the currency of Iran and Oman
  • introduction of — An introduction comes at the front, so what is taken is the opening letter of the word being introduced.

The bread that comes out of a tandoori oven, of the kind eaten in the Middle East.

'Bread' is money, not the naan a tandoori oven promises. Both food words in the surface are there to keep you thinking about dinner.

24 down NADIR

Bottom part of horrid android’s turned up (5)

Definition: Bottom · hidden word + reversal

Bottom is the lowest point of a thing, and the answer is the astronomer's word for the point directly beneath you — the opposite of the zenith.

  • horrid android’s → RIDAN five letters in a row straddle the join between these two words
  • turned up → reversal indicator: those five letters are read back the other way
  • part of — 'Part of' asks for a piece of the words themselves, so the letters come straight out of the text rather than out of the meaning.
  • turned up — This is a down entry, so 'up' means reading from the foot of the answer towards the top — the letters arrive the other way about.

The lower half of a horrible robot, tipped upside down.

'Bottom part of horrid android' reads as one description of a robot's lower half, which is how 'part of' slips past as an instruction rather than as description.
