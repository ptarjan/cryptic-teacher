Across

1 across FORAGES

Hunts persistently? (7)

Definition: Hunts · charade

To forage is to hunt about for food or supplies, so the verb answers 'hunts' in the same third-person present.

  • persistently → FOR doing something persistently is keeping it up over a long stretch of time, and this is the preposition such a phrase begins with
  • persistently → AGES the rest of that same phrase: an age is a very long time, and the plural piles them up

A blunt question about somebody who keeps on searching and will not give it up.

The question mark warns that the one wordplay word is not a synonym at all: it has to be turned into a two-word phrase first, and the space between them then closes up.

5 across WATCH IT

Be careful with cat that’s gone wild (5,2)

Definition: Be careful · anagram

'Watch it' is the spoken warning meaning be careful, an idiom that works whole rather than word by word.

  • with cat → WITHCAT anagram fodder, seven letters
  • gone wild → anagram indicator
  • gone wild — What has gone wild has broken out of the arrangement it was kept in, and letters in that state will not stay in the order handed over.

A warning about a domestic cat that has taken to living feral.

'With' is the trap: it reads as the join between a warning and the thing warned about, and it is material — four of the seven letters come from that one little word.

9 across A AND E

Treatment centre hosted by Pamela Anderson (1,3,1)

Definition: Treatment centre · hidden word

A&E treats casualties as they come through the door, so 'treatment centre' names the hospital department by the job it does.

  • Pamela Anderson → AANDE an unbroken run of letters that starts at the tail of the first name and carries on into the second
  • hosted by — A host has guests staying inside the house, so the words named next are keeping the letters wanted somewhere within them.

A rehab clinic with the actress Pamela Anderson as its celebrity host.

A&E is accident and emergency, the department of a British hospital you are taken to after a crash, and the ampersand is spelled out in full to make three words of two letters.

10 across RETICENCE

Crete: nice, surprisingly – that’s not saying much (9)

Definition: not saying much · anagram

Reticence is the habit of holding back what could be said, so the definition names the quality by the behaviour it produces.

  • Crete: nice → CRETENICE anagram fodder, nine letters
  • surprisingly → anagram indicator
  • surprisingly — What surprises you is not what you were expecting, and letters that come out unexpectedly have not stayed in the order they were given.

A holiday verdict on Crete: pleasant enough, which is hardly a rave review.

'That's not saying much' is a set phrase for damning with faint praise, and the setter means it literally: the definition is a description of how little is said.

11 across BLOOMSBURY

Literary set gets flowers put in the ground (10)

Definition: Literary set · charade

The name of the London district stands for the set of writers and thinkers who lived and met there around Virginia Woolf.

  • flowers → BLOOMS a bloom is a flower in full open state, the florist's word for one
  • put in the ground → BURY to put a thing in the ground and cover it over is to bury it

A circle of writers being handed flowers to plant out in the garden.

The Bloomsbury Group — Virginia Woolf, Keynes, the Bells — met in that district of London, so the definition names the writers by the streets they gathered in.

12 across BOLT

Wolf down sandwich with duck filling (4)

Definition: Wolf down · container

To bolt your food is to swallow it fast and barely chewed, which is exactly what wolfing it down describes.

  • sandwich → BLT the BLT is bacon, lettuce and tomato, the sandwich known by its initials
  • duck → O a batter out for no runs in cricket has made a duck, and the nought written down is this round letter
  • filling — A filling is what lies in the middle of a sandwich, so the letter it names goes inside the ones already there.

Somebody eating a sandwich that has duck in it.

'Wolf down' looks like the opening of a sentence about an animal and is the definition entire, which is why the eating in the surface belongs to the wordplay's sandwich instead.

14 across STRATOSPHERE

Engineer restores path; it’s very high above us (12)

Definition: very high above us · anagram

The stratosphere is the calm layer of air above the weather, ten to fifty kilometres up, which is as high above us as the phrase suggests.

  • restores path → RESTORESPATH anagram fodder, twelve letters
  • Engineer → anagram indicator
  • Engineer — To engineer a thing is to contrive it into a shape it did not have before, which is what happens to the letters beside it.

An engineer repairing a footpath somewhere far above our heads.

'Engineer' reads as the man with the shovel and works as a verb, and the semicolon is the setter's join: everything before it is wordplay, everything after it is definition.

18 across MARCEL PROUST

Time-poor French author misinterpreted as nostalgic? (6,6)

Definition: Time-poor French author misinterpreted as nostalgic? · cryptic definition

The whole clue describes the writer through his one great novel: its French title means lost time, and its old English title reads as nostalgia.

  • Time-poor French author → the surface offers a novelist with no hours to spare; taken as a title it points at the seven-volume novel called, in French, a search for lost time
  • misinterpreted as nostalgic → that same title went into English for decades as 'Remembrance of Things Past', which turns a search into a fond look backwards

A French novelist with no hours to spare, taken by his readers for a sentimental one.

Nothing splits into letters here: the clue works entirely on the two English titles of one book. The old one, 'Remembrance of Things Past', is a line lifted from Shakespeare's thirtieth sonnet.

21 across NAVE

Where religious crowd congregate to see villain beheaded (4)

Definition: Where religious crowd congregate · deletion

The nave is the long central hall of a church where the congregation sits, so the definition names it by who gathers there.

  • villain → KNAVE a knave is a villain in the old sense, the word Shakespeare uses for a rogue
  • beheaded — Beheading takes the head off the word beside it, and a word's head is the letter standing at its front.

A congregation gathering in church to watch a villain lose his head.

'Beheaded' serves the surface's execution and the spelling at once: what loses its head is the word, and the letter it loses is silent anyway.

22 across BALDERDASH

It’s more obvious having race later is nonsense (10)

Definition: nonsense · charade

Balderdash is nonsense spoken or written, the word for a stream of rubbish talk that nobody should take seriously.

  • more obvious → BALDER bald means plain and undisguised — a bald statement of fact — and this is its comparative
  • race → DASH a dash is a short sprint: a hundred-metre dash, a dash for the bus
  • later — What comes later comes along afterwards, so these letters are written after the ones already assembled.

Somebody insisting it is plainly daft to hold the race at a later hour.

'More obvious' looks like a comment on the whole sentence and is a piece of wordplay: the comparative wanted has nothing to do with hair.

25 across BOOMERANG

Carnage regularly follows elderly American – and it returns repeatedly (9)

Definition: it returns repeatedly · charade + alternate letters

A boomerang is the throwing stick that curves back to the thrower, so the definition points at it by the one thing it is famous for.

  • elderly American → BOOMER a baby boomer was born in the years just after the war, so one is now well into old age
  • Carnage → ANG the second, fourth and sixth letters of the word, which are A, N and G
  • regularly — Letters taken regularly come at an even beat through the word, here every second one, with the rest left behind.
  • follows — What follows comes along behind, so these letters are written after the ones already in place.

News of bloodshed that keeps on following an elderly American around.

The definition never names the object at all: it only says what the thing does once thrown, which is why the last clause reads as more surface rather than as the other half of the clue.

26 across PINTO

Keep in touch, having stabled horse (5)

Definition: horse · hidden word

A pinto is a horse with large patches of white and another colour, the American word for what the British call a piebald.

  • Keep in touch → PINTO the run starts at the tail of the first word, swallows the little word after it whole, and stops two letters into the last
  • stabled — A horse stabled for the night is shut inside, so what is wanted is shut inside the words beside it.

A parting request to stay in contact, made after putting the horse away for the night.

'Stabled' does the surface's stabling and the hiding at once, and 'Keep in touch' is a phrase the eye reads as a farewell rather than as a row of letters.

27 across ESSENCE

Pure quality of German city church (7)

Definition: Pure quality · charade

The essence of a thing is what is left when everything else is stripped away, which is quality in its pure state.

  • German city → ESSEN Essen is the industrial city of the Ruhr valley, a steel and coal town
  • church → CE CE is the Church of England, the two letters a crossword means whenever it says church

A remark about the unmixed quality of a church in a German city.

'Of' is the only join, and it is doing the work of an equals sign: everything after it is wordplay and everything before it is the definition.

28 across STEPHEN

Name of female acquired on re-marriage? (7)

Definition: Name · charade

Stephen is a man's first name, so the definition works by example: one name standing in for the whole class of them.

  • acquired on re-marriage → STEP the prefix that makes a stepmother or a stepson: the relation you gain when a parent marries again
  • female → HEN a hen is the female bird, and a hen night is the women's one

A new relative picked up when somebody married for the second time.

'Name' does nothing but point at a category — any name at all would satisfy it — so the wordplay has to do the whole job of identifying which one, and the question mark concedes it.

Down

1 down FLABBY

Inject vitamins into skin that’s loose (6)

Definition: loose · container

Flabby flesh is slack and out of tone, so 'loose' is the plain adjective for the same want of firmness.

  • skin → FLAY to flay an animal is to skin it, so the noun in the clue is really a verb, and this word opens up
  • vitamins → BB B is the vitamin named by a single letter, and the plural asks for two of them
  • Inject — Injecting drives something in under the surface, so the letters named beside it finish up inside the word that follows.
  • into — 'Into' names a destination rather than a place, so what has just been named travels within the word after it.

A cosmetic jab of vitamins going into sagging skin.

'Skin' reads as the thing being injected and is the verb for taking a hide off, which is what lets a word of four letters open up wide enough to hold two more.

2 down RANDOM

Published party minute – that’s irregular (6)

Definition: irregular · charade

Random and irregular both describe what follows no pattern — turning up at no set interval and answering to no rule.

  • Published → RAN a paper ran the story: 'ran' is the newspaper's own word for putting something into print
  • party → DO a do is a party in British usage — a works do, a bit of a do
  • minute → M M is the standard abbreviation for minute, as on a stopwatch

A party written up in the paper, timed to the minute and oddly scheduled.

The dash is the only join between wordplay and definition, and it hides how ordinary the three pieces are: a verb from the newsroom, a noun from the pub, and a unit of time.

3 down GREY MATTER

Dull count showing some capacity for thinking (4,6)

Definition: some capacity for thinking · charade

Grey matter is the tissue of the brain, and the phrase does duty in ordinary speech for brains or intelligence.

  • Dull → GREY a dull sky and a dull person are both this colour, which is the sense that carries over
  • count → MATTER what counts is what matters: to count for something is to be of consequence

A tedious nobleman who turns out to have a certain amount of brains after all.

The count is a European aristocrat on the surface and a plain verb underneath, which is the whole disguise: capital or no capital, the word that means the same as it is a verb too.

4 down SCRUB

Stand-in accepts credit for clean-up (5)

Definition: clean-up · container

A scrub is a hard clean with brush and water — 'give it a good scrub' — so the noun names the clean-up itself.

  • Stand-in → SUB a sub is the reserve who comes on in place of somebody else
  • credit → CR CR is the bookkeeper's shorthand for credit, written at the head of a column
  • accepts — To accept a thing is to take it in and keep it, so the word before opens up and holds the letters named next.

A substitute taking the credit for tidying up after everybody else.

'Clean-up' looks like the purpose of the credit and is a noun standing alone: the definition is the last word of the clue and nothing else.

5 down WATER POLO

Game played mostly by those out of their depth (5,4)

Definition: Game played mostly by those out of their depth · cryptic definition

Water polo is the game whose players cannot stand up in the pool, so the clue defines it by that one physical condition.

  • Game played mostly by → the surface offers a sport taken up by people who are not good enough for it — floundering, in over their heads
  • those out of their depth → read literally instead: these are players in water too deep to stand up in, which is where the game is held

A jibe at a sport whose players are hopelessly out of their league.

Nothing here separates into wordplay: 'out of their depth' means floundering on the surface and means water over your head in fact. Players tread water for most of a match and never touch the bottom.

6 down TACO

Starts to trudge around coffee outlet providing food (4)

Definition: food · first letters

A taco is the folded Mexican tortilla with a filling, so 'food' names it by the broad class it belongs to.

  • trudge around coffee outlet → TACO the opening letters of four consecutive words
  • Starts to — A start is where a word begins, so what comes away from each word after it is the single letter it opens with.

Somebody plodding round to the coffee shop and coming away with something to eat.

'Around' is the word to watch: it looks like an instruction to turn letters round or to wrap one piece about another, and it is simply the second of the four words donating a letter.

7 down HANGOVER

Afghan governor hides suffering in the morning? (8)

Definition: suffering in the morning · hidden word

A hangover is the morning-after headache and nausea that follow a night's drinking, so the definition names it by when it hurts.

  • Afghan governor → HANGOVER the run begins partway through the nationality and finishes partway through the job title, straddling the space
  • hides — What hides is out of sight but still there, so the letters wanted are sitting unbroken inside the words beside it.

A provincial governor in Afghanistan concealing his distress at daybreak.

The question mark admits the definition is a joke rather than a dictionary sense, and 'suffering' is a noun in it — the state you are in, not something you are doing.

8 down TWENTIES

Scores left during games (8)

Definition: Scores · container

A score is a count of twenty, so the plural of the number answers the plural of that old counting word.

  • games → TIES a tie is a match in a knockout competition — a cup tie — and this is the word that opens up
  • left → WENT he left the room, he went: the past tense of going is the past tense of leaving
  • during — What happens during something happens inside it while it is going on, so these letters sit within the word that follows.

Points picked up in the matches while somebody was away.

'Scores' reads as the points in those games, which is exactly what it is not: it is the old counting word, the one preserved in 'four score and ten'.

13 down APOSTROPHE

Bananas perhaps too often what’s misplaced by grocers? (10)

Definition: often what’s misplaced by grocers · anagram

It is the punctuation mark greengrocers are mocked for dropping into plurals, so the definition points at it by its most notorious misuse.

  • perhaps too → PERHAPSTOO anagram fodder, ten letters
  • Bananas → anagram indicator
  • Bananas — To go bananas is to go out of your mind, and letters sent out of their minds come back in an order nobody chose.

A complaint that grocers get bananas wrong more often than they get anything else.

The greengrocer's apostrophe is the stray mark in 'banana's' chalked on a market stall, so the first word of the clue is the joke and the instruction in one.

15 down ALPHA MALE

Top guy provides upset pal with meat and drink (5,4)

Definition: Top guy · charade + anagram

The alpha male is the dominant animal of a group, so 'top guy' says the same thing in ordinary words: the one in charge.

  • pal → ALP three letters of a friend, tipped over into a mountain
  • meat → HAM ham is the cured meat off the pig's hind leg
  • drink → ALE ale is beer, the shortest drink in the setter's cupboard
  • upset — Something upset has been knocked over and its contents spilled out anyhow, so the three letters beside it come back in a different order.

A team's top man consoling an upset friend with something to eat and drink.

In a down clue 'upset' usually means read the letters back up the column, which would give LAP; here it is the anagram sense instead, and only the other order fits what follows.

16 down AMENABLE

Final word, effective and quite open (8)

Definition: quite open · charade

Somebody amenable is open to persuasion and easily talked round, which is the willingness 'quite open' describes.

  • Final word → AMEN the word every prayer finishes on, a final word in the most literal sense
  • effective → ABLE an able administrator is an effective one, good at getting the thing done

A closing remark that gets results and keeps nothing back.

'Final word' is not the last word of anything in the clue, which is where the eye goes first: it is the four letters said at the end of a prayer.

17 down PREVIOUS

Vicar delving into religious sins of the past (8)

Definition: sins of the past · container

'Previous' is the police word for a record of earlier convictions, which is exactly what sins of the past amount to.

  • religious → PIOUS a pious man is a religious one, and this is the word that opens up
  • Vicar → REV Rev is how a vicar's title, Reverend, is written on an envelope
  • delving into — To delve into a thing is to dig down inside it, so the letters named first are buried within the word that comes next.

A vicar digging around in somebody's religious wrongdoing.

In British police usage a suspect who 'has previous' has earlier convictions on the record, so the definition wants a noun where the word is normally an adjective.

19 down LAUNCH

Open fire! (6)

Definition: Open · double definition

To launch a venture is to open it, and to launch a missile is to fire it — two everyday senses of the same verb.

  • Open → to open a shop, a campaign or a product line is to set it going for the first time
  • fire → firing a rocket sends it up off the pad, which is the sense the exclamation mark keeps out of sight

An order shouted across a battlefield to start shooting.

'Open fire!' is a single order on the surface and two unrelated verbs underneath, one from the business pages and one from the rocket range.

20 down SHOO-IN

Violent gangster endlessly engages in evil? It’s almost guaranteed (4-2)

Definition: It’s almost guaranteed · container + deletion

A shoo-in is the candidate or horse everybody expects to win, so the definition claims near-certainty rather than the absolute kind.

  • evil → SIN sin is evil in three letters, and this is the word that opens up to take something in
  • Violent gangster → HOOD a hood is a hoodlum, the violent end of the gangster trade, and it turns up here a letter short
  • endlessly — What goes on endlessly never reaches its end, so the word before it arrives here without its last letter.
  • engages in — To engage in something is to take part right inside it, so the letters just named go within the word that follows.

A thug with no scruples who is mixed up in something wicked.

The question mark is doing the joining, and it hides how much work 'endlessly' does: it belongs to the wordplay, not to the gangster's career.

23 down DEGAS

Dead, for example, like this old artist (5)

Definition: old artist · charade

Degas was the nineteenth-century French painter of dancers and racehorses, so 'old artist' names him as one instance of the class.

  • Dead → D D is the standard abbreviation for dead, as written on a family tree
  • for example → EG e.g. is the Latin exempli gratia, the two letters written for 'for example'
  • like → AS 'as' is the little word that stands in for 'like': pale as milk, thin as a rake

Somebody pointing at a dead painter as an example of the type.

The commas make the clue read as one halting sentence, and each scrap between them is separate shorthand. The painter meant is the one who did the ballet dancers and the racecourse crowds.

24 down SEWN

Stitched up in every direction (4)

Definition: Stitched up · charade

Sewn is the past participle of sew, so it means stitched, and a deal sewn up is one stitched up tight.

  • every direction → S the four points of the compass are the material here, and south is the one that comes first
  • every direction → E east, the second of the four points
  • every direction → W west, the third
  • every direction → N north, and those four cover every direction there is

A tear repaired with stitches running every which way.

'In every direction' is not saying the letters wander about the grid: it is naming the four points of the compass, which the crossing letters then have to put in order, since the clue names no order at all.
