9 across LIONESSES

Football team 1-1 after leading at first, idiots missing header (9)

Definition: Football team · charade + first letter + deletion

Lionesses is the nickname of England's women's football team, so the definition names them by exactly what they are.

  • leading → L the leading letter of that word, taken from the word itself
  • 1-1 → I the first of the pair as a Roman numeral
  • 1-1 → ONE the second of the pair written out as a word
  • idiots missing header → SSES idiots are asses, and the word arrives without the A at its front
  • at first — Taken at first means only the very front of the word is wanted, which is one letter of it.
  • after — What comes after something is written down behind it, so these two numbers follow the letter already found.
  • missing header — A header is the top or front of a thing, so the word turns up here without its opening letter.

A side draws 1-1 after going ahead early, and some fools miss a header.

The Lionesses are England's women's football team. Every word of the surface stays inside one match report — the score, the lead, the missed header — while the wordplay quietly counts letters.

10 across USHER

Old teacher’s pronouns (5)

Definition: Old teacher · charade

Usher is the archaic word for an under-master or assistant schoolteacher, a sense now kept alive mainly by crosswords.

  • pronouns → US the first of two, the object form of we
  • pronouns → HER the second, the object form of she, laid straight after the first

A retired schoolmaster listing the pronouns he goes by.

'Pronouns' reads as the modern habit of stating which ones you use, and it is two of them written end to end. An usher was the assistant master in an old grammar school.

11 across CHAOS

Charlie brings in bowl for dog’s dinner (5)

Definition: dog’s dinner · container

A dog's dinner is British idiom for a shambles, and chaos is that state at full strength — the noun, not the adjective.

  • Charlie → CHAS Chas is the familiar short form of Charles
  • bowl → O a bowl is a round vessel, and O is the round letter
  • brings in — Bringing a thing in carries it inside what is already there, so the round object ends up within the name.

Charlie carries a bowl in and the dog gets its dinner.

'Charlie' looks like the radio alphabet's C and is a man's nickname instead. A dog's dinner is British for a mess: make a dog's dinner of a job and you have botched it.

12 across VALENTINE

Card for One Gentleman of Verona (9)

Definition: Card · double definition

A valentine is the card sent on 14 February, and Valentine is the name of one of the two Veronese gentlemen in Shakespeare.

  • Card → the one that goes anonymously through the letterbox on 14 February
  • One Gentleman of Verona → Shakespeare's play has two of them, and this is the name of just one

Somebody posting a card to one of the two gentlemen in Shakespeare's play.

The Two Gentlemen of Verona are Valentine and Proteus, so 'One' is doing arithmetic on a title. The capital G is the play's, and no letters change hands anywhere.

13 across THIN AIR

Feature of High Peak in which to disappear (4,3)

Definition: Feature of High Peak · double definition

Climb high enough and the air thins, which is a feature of any high peak, while the idiom has people vanish into thin air.

  • Feature of High Peak → go up far enough and there is measurably less of the stuff to breathe
  • in which to disappear → the idiom for vanishing without trace, as if the air had swallowed somebody

The Derbyshire hills have a quality that makes people go missing up there.

High Peak is the Derbyshire district at the top of the Peak District, and the capital letters make it a place name; climb it and the air really does thin.

14 across IMPOSED

Imogen’s model daughter required to be paid (7)

Definition: required to be paid · charade

A tax, a fine or a levy that is imposed is one you are required to pay: the levying sense of the word, not the intruding one.

  • Imogen’s → IM Imogen is the setter, so her own name taken as her voice is I'm
  • model → POSE to model for a painter or a camera is to pose
  • daughter → D d is the family-tree abbreviation for daughter

The setter's daughter does modelling work and is waiting to be paid for it.

Imogen is the setter's own pseudonym, so 'Imogen's' is her saying I'm — the setter writing herself into her clue, which is a habit worth expecting on a Saturday.

17 across WALTZ

Dance music from Disney on air (5)

Definition: Dance · homophone

A waltz is the ballroom dance in three-four time, so 'Dance' names the answer by its plainest category.

  • music from Disney → WALTZ Disney's first name was Walt, so music that belongs to him is Walt's — and that possessive is what you say aloud
  • on air — Something on air is heard rather than read, so what counts is how the words sound, not how they are spelt.

Music from the Disney studio being played on the radio.

'On air' is broadcasting in the surface and sounding in the wordplay. Said aloud, the possessive S hardens into a Z, and that hardening is the whole mechanism.

19 across CAT

Whippet? (3)

Definition: Whippet? · cryptic definition

A cat is the commonest household pet, and the cat-o'-nine-tails was the navy's whip, so one split word covers both senses.

  • Whippet? → read as one word it is a breed of racing dog, which is all the surface offers
  • Whippet? → split it after the fourth letter and it asks for two things at once, a whip and a pet

A whippet, the slender racing dog.

The question mark is the only signal you get. Break the word in two and both halves define the answer: a cat-o'-nine-tails is a whip, and a cat is a pet.

20 across NEWEL

Changing hands, more recent post (5)

Definition: post · substitution

A newel is the upright post carrying a staircase handrail, so 'post' names it by the everyday word for that upright.

  • more recent → NEWER the comparative of new
  • hands → L the R that ends that word is right, and the hand it changes to is left
  • Changing hands — Changing hands swaps one for the other, and the two letters that stand for right and left do the swapping.

A more up-to-date job that has just passed to somebody else.

A newel is the post at the foot or the turn of a staircase, the one the handrail is bolted to. R and L are right and left throughout crosswords, which is what makes a hand a letter.

21 across THEORBO

Boot her out? There are strings attached to that (7)

Definition: There are strings attached to that · anagram

A theorbo is a long-necked bass lute, so a sentence about strings hanging off something is a fair description of it.

  • Boot her → BOOTHER seven letters of raw material, the two words run together
  • out → anagram indicator
  • out — Boot somebody out and they finish up anywhere but where they were, which is what happens to these letters.

Evicting her comes with conditions — there are strings attached.

'Strings attached' is the idiom for conditions in the surface and a literal statement in the answer, which is a bass lute the size of a person.

22 across RECORDS

Regularly take free measures of wood cut as logs (7)

Definition: logs · charade + alternate letters

A ship's log is its written record and to log something is to write it down, so logs and records are the same plural noun.

  • Regularly take free → RE the second and fourth letters of the four-letter word, taken at every other step
  • measures of wood cut → CORDS a cord is a stacked measure of cut firewood, 128 cubic feet of it
  • Regularly — Doing a thing regularly means at even intervals, so every second letter is picked up and the ones between are left.

Somebody regularly helping themselves to free timber and sawing it into logs.

Both halves of the clue are standing in the same woodpile, so nothing marks where the wordplay stops. To log an event is to record it, which is the sense wanted.

24 across SWINGEING

Huge energy in description of 60s London (9)

Definition: Huge · container

Swingeing means severe or very large — a swingeing tax rise is a huge one — and it is a different word from swinging.

  • description of 60s London → SWINGING the city of the 1960s was called swinging, after its music and fashion boom
  • energy → E E is energy in physics, as in Einstein's equation
  • in — 'In' drops the letter just named inside the word that follows, between one half of it and the other.

London in the sixties, described by its enormous energy.

Swinging London was Carnaby Street and the King's Road, the tag the magazines gave the city. One extra E separates that word from the one meaning severe.

26 across LOCAL

A number in the vicinity (5)

Definition: A number · double definition

A local is the anaesthetic that numbs one part of you, and local also means nearby — the pub sense comes from that same closeness.

  • A number → not the counting kind but a thing that numbs, which the dentist injects
  • in the vicinity → the ordinary adjective for whatever is close at hand

A figure somewhere close by.

'Number' is not the counting one but a thing that numbs. Say it numb-er, with the B sounded, and the anaesthetic the dentist gives you is what the clue is asking for.

28 across WISPY

Flimsy Caribbean agent (5)

Definition: Flimsy · charade

Something wispy is thin and insubstantial, a wisp of hair or smoke, which is what calling a thing flimsy says about it.

  • Caribbean → WI the West Indies are the Caribbean islands, WI on a cricket scoreboard
  • agent → SPY an agent working undercover is a spy

A frail-looking secret agent from the islands.

'Caribbean' is the West Indies, shortened the way cricket shortens it. The definition is the single word at the front, which reads as part of the picture and is not.

29 across GET WISE TO

See to twig confused for a different twig (3,4,2)

Definition: a different twig · anagram

To twig something is British slang for suddenly understanding it, which is exactly what getting wise to a thing means.

  • See to twig → SEETOTWIG nine letters of raw material, the three words run together
  • confused → anagram indicator
  • confused — Something confused is in disarray, and letters in disarray have lost the order they were handed over in.

One twig gets mistaken for another twig entirely.

'Twig' appears twice and means something different each time: the first is raw material, the second is the definition, British slang for catching on.

1 down ALEC

Total eclipse enthralling smart guy (4)

Definition: smart guy · hidden word

A smart Alec is a know-all, and the name carries that sense on its own, so 'smart guy' points straight at the man in the phrase.

  • Total eclipse → ALEC four letters in a row here, starting partway through the first word and crossing the gap
  • enthralling — To enthral is to hold somebody captive, and these two words are holding four consecutive letters captive between them.

A complete blackout of the sun holding some clever man spellbound.

'Enthralling' does nothing to the letters except hold them still, which is the quietest kind of instruction. Alec is the crossword's clever one, from the phrase smart Alec.

2 down POLARI

Star’s name shortened, in slang (6)

Definition: slang · deletion

Polari is one particular slang — the coded vocabulary of gay and theatrical Britain — so the definition names it by its category rather than by a synonym.

  • Star’s name → POLARIS the north star is named for the pole it stands over
  • shortened → the final S of that name is the letter that goes
  • shortened — Shortening a word takes something off its end, so the name arrives one letter lighter than it was given.

A famous star's name, clipped the way street talk clips things.

Polari was the coded slang of gay Britain and the theatre, kept alive on radio by Julian and Sandy in Round the Horne. The setter needs only one letter off a star.

3 down BELSHAZZAR

Feasting king has a small herbal brew, interrupted by short nap? (10)

Definition: Feasting king · anagram + container

Belshazzar is remembered for one thing, the great feast at which the writing appeared on the wall, so 'feasting king' names him by it.

  • a small herbal brew → BELSHAAR 'a', plus S for small, plus the six of HERBAL — eight letters stirred into a new order
  • short nap? → ZZ a sleeper in the comic strips gives off Zs, and a nap cut short is a couple of them
  • brew — Brewing stirs everything together until nothing sits where it started, and letters treated that way lose their order.
  • interrupted by — An interruption arrives in the middle of something already under way, so the next piece lands inside the letters just made.

A king breaks off from his feast for a herbal drink and a quick doze.

Belshazzar is the Babylonian king of the Book of Daniel, the one whose feast was interrupted by writing appearing on the wall — so the surface's interruption is the story's too.

4 down AS EVER

Like the usual answer (part) (2,4)

Definition: Like the usual · charade

As ever means as always, so 'like the usual' says the same thing as a comparison and slots into a sentence in the same place.

  • answer → A A is the standard short form of answer, the one that sits opposite Q
  • part → SEVER to part two things is to sever them, cutting one clean from the other

The same old reply, with a bit of it put in brackets.

'Part' is a verb here, not a portion — the parting of two things, which is severing. The brackets look like a note about the enumeration and hold half the wordplay.

5 down PSALMIST

For one, David succeeded opening a handy reader (8)

Definition: For one, David · container

David is credited with the Psalms, so he is one psalmist among several — the definition works by example, which 'for one' declares.

  • a handy reader → PALMIST someone who reads hands for a living is reading palms
  • succeeded → S s. is the genealogist's mark for succeeded, as in a king who s. his father
  • opening — Opening a thing means cutting a gap into it, and the single letter named before it is what slips into the gap.

David managed to get a convenient little book open.

'A handy reader' is a fortune teller rather than a paperback, the hand being the thing she reads. 'For one' is the setter admitting David is an example, not the whole class.

6 down TURN

Opportunity coming round to change colour, artist has no hesitation (4)

Definition: Opportunity coming round · double definition + deletion

A turn is the opportunity that comes round to you, and milk or leaves that turn have changed colour — two everyday senses of one short word.

  • artist → TURNER JMW Turner, the English painter of light, steam and storms
  • hesitation → er is the noise people make while hesitating, and those two letters come off the end
  • has no — Having none of a thing means it is absent, so the two letters spelling that noise never make it into the painter's name.

A chance to change colour comes round, and the painter takes it without pausing.

Milk turns, leaves turn, and your turn comes round: three senses are stacked up before the wordplay even starts, and then Turner loses his er.

7 down CHAINSAW

Round hospital, murderer noticed massacre weapon that was filmed (8)

Definition: massacre weapon that was filmed · container + charade

The Texas Chain Saw Massacre put this tool on screen as a murder weapon, so the definition names it by the film that made it notorious.

  • murderer → CAIN the Bible's first killer, who murdered his brother Abel
  • hospital → H H is hospital on road signs and in the radio alphabet
  • noticed → SAW noticed in the past tense is saw
  • Round — Going round something means wrapping about it, so the letter for the building ends up enclosed by the name beside it.

On a hospital round somebody notices a murderer holding the weapon from a horror film.

'Round' wants to be the doctor's round of the wards and is the wrapping instruction instead. The Texas Chain Saw Massacre is the film the definition has in mind.

8 down ORFE

Penalty would be fit, if this swimmer knocked out (4)

Definition: swimmer · deletion

An orfe is a freshwater fish kept in ponds, and 'swimmer' is the setter's usual way of asking for a fish without naming one.

  • Penalty → FORFEIT the penalty you pay for breaking a rule is a forfeit
  • would be fit → FIT what is left of that seven-letter word once four consecutive letters are taken out of it
  • knocked out — Knocking something out removes it altogether, so the run of letters named is lifted clean out of the longer word.

The punishment would suit, if a certain swimmer were knocked out of the contest.

Nothing is built up here, it is taken away: knock ORFE out of FORFEIT and FIT is all that is left. An orfe is a golden pond fish, the crossword's handiest small swimmer.

13 down TO WIT

In other words, what to do with broken-down car (2,3)

Definition: In other words · charade

To wit introduces a restatement — namely, that is to say — which is the job 'in other words' announces in ordinary English.

  • what to do with broken-down car → TOW a car that will not go has to be pulled away behind another vehicle
  • car → IT the car is the thing being pulled, referred to as it

Your car has broken down and somebody is telling you what happens next.

The two words of the wordplay and the two of the answer are not the same two — the break moves one letter along, and that is the whole joke. TO WIT is the lawyer's 'namely'.

15 down PENICILLIN

Writer covering India with nothing about treatment for infection (10)

Definition: treatment for infection · container + reversal + charade

Penicillin is the mould-derived antibiotic given to fight bacterial infection, so the definition describes it by what doctors use it for.

  • Writer → PENCIL a writer can be the implement rather than the person holding it
  • India → I I is India in the radio alphabet, alongside Hotel and Juliet
  • nothing → LIN nothing is nil, and here it is written back to front
  • covering — To cover a thing is to lie over it and hide it, so the letter that follows is taken inside the writer's word.
  • about — Turning about is facing the other way, and the little word here is written in the reverse of the order it comes in.

A reporter out in India has nothing to say about how infections get treated.

'Covering' is the journalist's word in the surface and the container's instruction in the wordplay, which is why the sentence reads so smoothly past it.

16 down DOLES

Mostly ease off work, getting handouts (5)

Definition: handouts · deletion

Doles are amounts handed out in portions, the dole being Britain's unemployment payment, so 'handouts' matches sense and plural exactly.

  • ease off work → DO LESS easing off at work is doing less of it, and the two words run together
  • Mostly → the final S of that phrase is the letter dropped
  • Mostly — Most of a phrase is all of it bar the end, so the last letter of what follows never gets written down.

Somebody who mostly slacks at work and lives off what other people give them.

The dole is the British word for unemployment benefit, and to dole something out is to hand it round in portions — the same word doing both jobs.

18 down LEERIEST

More wary than anyone of lake, most unearthly (8)

Definition: More wary than anyone · charade

To be leery of something is to be wary of it, so the person warier than anybody else is the leeriest of them.

  • lake → L L is the map-maker's short form for lake
  • most unearthly → EERIEST unearthly is eerie, and 'most' asks for the superlative of it

Nobody is more nervous than this of a lake that looks thoroughly haunted.

'More wary than anyone' is a superlative spelled out the long way, which is the tip-off that the answer ends -EST. Leery is the wary one, as in leery of a deal.

19 down COOLIDGE

Calvin’s business giving off bad smell gets halved (8)

Definition: Calvin · charade + deletion

Calvin was the first name of President Coolidge, so the clue defines the surname by the other half of the man's name.

  • business → CO a business is a company, shortened to Co in any firm's name
  • giving off bad smell → OLID olid is the dictionary's word for foul-smelling, from the Latin for stinking
  • gets → GE the first half of that four-letter word
  • halved — Halving a word keeps just one half of it, and it is the front half that is wanted here.

Calvin's firm stinks, and now it has shrunk to half the size.

Calvin Coolidge was the American president of the 1920s, famous for saying as little as possible. 'Giving off bad smell' is olid, a word that lives mostly in dictionaries and grids.

22 down RAGS TO RICHES

Chorister sang out, missing note in the story of Cinderella (4,2,6)

Definition: the story of Cinderella · anagram + deletion

Cinderella starts in cinders and ends with the prince, which is the rags-to-riches story in its purest form.

  • Chorister sang → CHORISTERSAG thirteen letters of raw material with one struck out, leaving twelve to rearrange
  • note → n. is the abbreviation for note, and that is the letter taken away
  • out — Sing out and the sound goes everywhere at once; letters sent out the same way do not come back in the order they left.
  • missing — What is missing is simply not there, so the letter named is taken out of the material before anything is rearranged.

A choirboy belted it out and fluffed a note, somewhere in the Cinderella story.

Cinderella goes from the ashes to the ball, so the definition can point at the whole tale rather than a word in it. The answer runs on into 23 down.

24 down SEWN

Stitched up by all the cardinals (4)

Definition: Stitched up · charade

Sewn is the past participle of sew, so anything stitched is sewn; the idiom's other sense, being framed, belongs to the surface.

  • cardinals → S the cardinal points are the four compass directions, and south leads off here
  • cardinals → E east is the second of the four, in the order this entry needs
  • cardinals → W west is third, the one facing the direction that started it
  • cardinals → N north closes the set of four

The cardinals have stitched somebody up between them.

'Cardinals' wants to be the men in red hats and means the cardinal points instead — north, south, east and west, all four of them turning up in a fresh order.

25 down GOYA

Artist’s ascetic discipline: cycling backwards (4)

Definition: Artist · reversal + cycling

Goya was the Spanish court painter of the Napoleonic years, so 'artist' defines him as one instance of the trade.

  • ascetic discipline → YOGA the Hindu discipline of posture and breathing, austere in its origins if not in its gyms
  • cycling — Cycling sends the letters round a loop, the one at the front going to the back with the rest keeping their order.
  • backwards — Backwards has a word read from its far end first, in the opposite order to the one it was given in.

An artist keeps up a strict daily practice, and rides a bike in reverse.

Read the discipline backwards to get AGOY, then send its first letter round to the end and the Spanish painter appears. Two instructions are riding in one three-word phrase.

27 down LOOM

Come close, perhaps threatening weaver (4)

Definition: Come close, perhaps threatening · double definition

To loom is to come into view large and close, often menacingly; a loom is also the frame cloth is woven on.

  • Come close, perhaps threatening → the verb for something large drawing near, a headland or a deadline
  • weaver → the frame that holds the warp threads while the weft goes across

Something drawing near in a way that feels like a threat.

The comma is the seam: everything before it is a verb and the single word after it is a machine, which is why the clue reads as one continuous menace.
