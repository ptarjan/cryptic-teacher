Across

1 across LONELY

One in fifties, ultimately unhappy, wanting company (6)

Definition: wanting company · container + charade + last letter

LONELY means wanting company — being on your own and feeling the absence of anyone else.

  • fifties → LL L is the Roman numeral for fifty, and the plural asks for two of them
  • One → ONE the number written out, dropped between the pair
  • ultimately unhappy → Y the last letter of 'unhappy'
  • in — Something placed 'in' another thing sits inside it, so the piece before this word goes between the letters that follow.
  • ultimately — Ultimately means at the very end, so only the final letter of the word beside it is taken.

Someone in their fifties, thoroughly miserable and short of company.

L is the Roman fifty and the plural 'fifties' quietly asks for two of them, one on each side. 'Ultimately' is the crossword's standard word for a final letter.

4 across SABOTAGE

Deliberately damage bog at sea, recklessly (8)

Definition: Deliberately damage · anagram

To SABOTAGE something is to damage it deliberately — wrecking done on purpose rather than by accident.

  • bog at sea → BOGATSEA the fodder, eight letters, exactly the length wanted
  • recklessly — Doing something recklessly means doing it with no care for how it turns out, so the letters land wherever they fall.

Wrecking a stretch of marshland out on the open water without a care.

'At sea' is itself one of the commonest anagram indicators, which is why it looks like the instruction when it is really part of the fodder. 'Recklessly' at the end is the real one.

9 across RINGO

Drummer’s jingle bell ball (5)

Definition: Drummer · charade

RINGO Starr drummed for the Beatles, so 'drummer' defines him by example — one famous holder of the job standing for the name.

  • jingle bell → RING a jingle bell exists to ring, and the noise it makes is a ring
  • ball → O a ball is round, drawn as a single circle

A drummer's festive kit: a jingling bell and a ball.

O for anything round is one of the most reliable equations in cryptics — a ball, a ring, a duck at cricket, love at tennis all come out as the same letter.

10 across SUBMARINE

Transport provider in deep water! (9)

Definition: Transport provider in deep water! · cryptic definition

A SUBMARINE is transport that operates in deep water, so both halves of the clue describe it straight — which is the whole joke.

  • Transport provider → reads as a bus or rail company; the setter means a single vessel that carries people and cargo
  • in deep water → the idiom for being in serious trouble is the trap — read straight, it says the vessel travels below the surface of the sea

A travel firm that has landed itself in serious trouble.

There is no wordplay to take apart: both halves of the description are literally true of the same thing at once. It joins RINGO, HELP and YELLOW in the puzzle's Beatles thread.

11 across CLUB

Hint endlessly and start to badger association (4)

Definition: association · charade + deletion + first letter

A CLUB is an association: a body of people formally joined together around a shared interest.

  • Hint → CLU a hint is a clue, with its last letter taken off
  • badger → B the opening letter of the animal
  • endlessly — Something endless has no end, so the final letter of the word beside it comes off.
  • start to — The start of a word is its opening letter, and that is all this one takes.

Dropping hints forever, then starting to pester someone.

'Hint' is a noun here rather than a verb, and a hint is a clue — the setter winking at the thing you are holding.

12 across FAME

Note setter’s renown (4)

Definition: renown · charade

FAME is renown — both name the state of being widely known and talked about.

  • Note → FA fa is the fourth note of the sol-fa scale
  • setter → ME the setter is the person who wrote the puzzle, and writing about himself he is me

Making a note of how well known the compiler is.

A setter referring to himself as ME is one of the first conventions worth owning, and the sol-fa scale supplies half the short notes in cryptics: DOH, RE, MI, FA, SO, LA, TE.

13 across LATTE

Not finishing most recent coffee (5)

Definition: coffee · deletion

A LATTE is a coffee — espresso lengthened with steamed milk — so 'coffee' defines it by example.

  • most recent → LATTE of two things the more recent is the second one, and the six-letter adjective for that is stopped a letter early
  • Not finishing — Not finishing a word means stopping before its last letter, so the final one never gets written.

Abandoning your latest coffee half-drunk.

'Most recent' is LATTER only where there are two things to choose between, which is the sense the setter needs and the last one a solver reaches for.

15 across SEASIDE

We heard spy let out a long breath at the beach (7)

Definition: the beach · homophone

The SEASIDE is the beach: the stretch of coast people go to in order to be beside the sea.

  • spy → SEA to spy something is to see it, and that word said aloud is written this way when it means the water
  • let out a long breath → SIDE letting out a long breath is to sigh, and the past tense of it sounds identical to a word meaning edge
  • We heard — Heard reports the words by ear, so what matters is the noise they make rather than the way they are spelt.

We overheard a spy exhaling heavily down at the beach.

'We heard' governs both halves of the wordplay rather than just the word next to it — a single sound indicator can cover an entire phrase, which is what makes the second half easy to miss.

16 across IDOL

God’s lazy, by the sounds of it (4)

Definition: God · homophone

An IDOL is a god — the image or figure worshipped as one, and by extension the deity itself.

  • lazy → IDOL lazy means idle, and that word said aloud can be written the other way
  • by the sounds of it — By the sounds of it points at how a word is pronounced instead of how it is written down.

A deity accused of being work-shy.

The two words are pronounced identically and share only two letters, which is exactly the gap a homophone clue lives in.

19 across HELP

Give me a hand – the record’s skipping intro (4)

Definition: Give me a hand · charade + deletion

'Give me a hand' is what HELP means when it is shouted: both are the same request for someone to come and assist.

  • the → HE the definite article with its opening letter skipped
  • record → LP an LP is a long-playing record
  • skipping intro — An intro is the opening, and skipping it means starting the word one letter in.

A plea for assistance while the record jumps its opening bars.

The surface is a scratched vinyl skipping its intro, and the answer is another of the Beatles titles scattered across this grid.

20 across TORNADO

Violent storm split academy party (7)

Definition: Violent storm · charade

A TORNADO is a violent storm — the whirling column of wind that tears its way across the ground.

  • split → TORN something split down the middle has been torn
  • academy → A A is a standard abbreviation for academy
  • party → DO a do is British for a party or social function — a works do, a bit of a do

A fierce storm breaking up a college party.

'Split' looks like the verb the sentence turns on, a storm breaking something up, and it is a piece of the answer instead.

23 across ENEMY

Opponent at first escaped, nevertheless eventually must yield (5)

Definition: Opponent · first letters

An ENEMY is an opponent: the person ranged against you in a fight, a game or an argument.

  • escaped, nevertheless eventually must yield → ENEMY the opening letter of each of these five words, taken in order
  • at first — At first asks for the opening letter of each word that follows and nothing else from any of them.

An opponent who got away at first but has to give in eventually.

'At first' can reach across as many words as the enumeration needs — five of them here — which is what makes an acrostic so hard to spot inside a sentence that reads naturally.

24 across ANON

Name at the end, A N Other, concisely? (4)

Definition: Name at the end, A N Other, concisely? · &lit + charade + first letters

The clue reads straight: ANON is the concise thing written at the end when the author is some unnamed other person.

  • A N Other → ANO A N Other is the British team-sheet placeholder for a player not yet named; take the initial of each of its three parts
  • Name → N N is the standard abbreviation for name, and it is sent to the back
  • concisely — Concisely asks for the shortest possible writing of what it governs, which here means keeping initials and dropping the rest.
  • at the end — At the end says where a piece goes rather than which letters it is, so the one it governs is tacked on last.

Filling in a team sheet with the last name left blank.

The whole sentence is doing both jobs at once: it spells the word out of initials and describes what the word is for. A N Other is what British team sheets print when the eleventh player has not been picked.

25 across BAND

Group broadcasting prohibited (4)

Definition: Group · homophone

A BAND is a group — a set of musicians, or more generally any set of people acting together.

  • prohibited → BAND prohibited means banned, and said aloud that word can be written a letter shorter
  • broadcasting — Broadcasting sends words out over the air, so you judge them by what they sound like.

A group forbidden from going on air.

The doubled N disappears completely when you say it. This entry is also the last word of the Beatles album spread across the grid.

27 across REPAIRERS

Fixers, they get couples back together again? (9)

Definition: Fixers · charade

REPAIRERS are fixers: people whose job is putting broken things back into working order.

  • again → RE RE- is the prefix meaning doing a thing over again
  • they get couples back together → PAIRERS people who put two things into a couple are pairers, and 'they' makes it plural

Menders who also reunite estranged couples.

A hyphen is the joke: re-pairers put two things back into a couple, and run the same nine letters together without it and they mend things instead.

28 across BELCH

Rebel chorister conceals burp (5)

Definition: burp · hidden word

To BELCH is to burp — both name the noisy release of air back up from the stomach.

  • Rebel chorister → BELCH the letters run on from the end of the first word into the start of the second
  • conceals — To conceal something is to keep it out of sight inside, which is where these letters are sitting.

A mutinous choirboy stifling a burp.

A hidden answer that straddles the join between two words is far harder to see than one sitting inside a single word, and the space is what hides it.

29 across SERGEANT

Doctor gets near police officer (8)

Definition: police officer · anagram

A SERGEANT is a police officer: the rank between constable and inspector in a British force.

  • gets near → GETSNEAR the fodder, eight letters, exactly the length wanted
  • Doctor — To doctor something is to tamper with it, so the letters do not come out the way they went in.

A physician drawing closer to a policeman.

'Doctor' is the instruction, not the man the surface puts in the room. The answer joins PEPPER, LONELY, HEARTS, CLUB and BAND — the Beatles album spread across this grid.

30 across YELLOW

Cowardly, shout ‘that hurts!’ (6)

Definition: Cowardly · charade

YELLOW means cowardly — the colour has carried that sense for well over a century, as in 'yellow-bellied'.

  • shout → YELL to shout is to yell
  • that hurts → OW the noise you make when something hurts

A cowardly yelp of pain.

'Cowardly' looks like an adjective describing the shout that follows it, and it is the definition standing on its own at the front.

Down

1 down LYRICIST

Crazy tenor Cyril is songwriter (8)

Definition: songwriter · anagram

A LYRICIST is a songwriter in the sense of writing a song's words; the tune is the composer's half of the job.

  • tenor → T T is the standard abbreviation for tenor on a piece of music
  • Cyril is → CYRILIS the rest of the fodder, seven letters taken as they stand
  • Crazy — A crazy person is out of their right mind, and letters described that way will not stay in the order handed over.

A wild-tempered singer called Cyril who writes songs.

'Tenor' does two jobs at once: it makes Cyril a singer in the surface and hands over the eighth letter the fodder needs to reach the enumeration.

2 down NON-HUMAN

No grandmother accepts bad smell – it’s alien?! (3-5)

Definition: alien · charade + container

Something NON-HUMAN is alien in the adjective's core sense: belonging to another kind altogether, not one of us.

  • No → NO the word itself, two letters lifted straight out
  • grandmother → NAN nan is the everyday British word for a grandmother
  • bad smell → HUM in British usage something that hums stinks — if the bins hum, they smell
  • accepts — To accept something is to take it in, so the word doing the accepting opens up and holds the next piece inside it.

No grandmother will put up with a bad smell, and there is something otherworldly about this one.

'No' looks like the refusal that starts the sentence and it is really two letters standing on their own, which is the hardest kind of piece to see.

3 down LOOT

Raid implement turned up (4)

Definition: Raid · reversal

To LOOT is to raid — both mean stripping a place of its goods by force, usually in the confusion after a fight.

  • implement → TOOL an implement is a tool, and this is the word before it gets turned round
  • turned up — Turned up sends the letters back the way they came, and this entry runs down the grid, so they are read from the bottom upwards.

A burglar's tool showing up after a break-in.

'Raid' looks like the noun any sentence about a robbery would open with, and it is the verb doing the defining. LOOT and TOOL are each other backwards.

5 down ABBREVIATIONS

Short forms of Abba’s no 1 rivet Brahms and Liszt (13)

Definition: Short forms · anagram

ABBREVIATIONS are short forms — the cut-down spellings words are given so they take up less room.

  • Abba’s no 1 rivet → ABBASNOIRIVET the fodder, thirteen letters, with the numeral 1 taken as the letter I
  • Brahms and Liszt — Brahms and Liszt is Cockney rhyming slang for pissed, meaning drunk, and a drunk cannot keep anything in a straight line.

Abba's number one single gripping a couple of nineteenth-century composers.

Brahms and Liszt sitting in a clue about Abba looks like a joke about musical taste, and the numeral 1 slips into the fodder as a letter rather than a number.

6 down ON ALL FOURS

Falls over our Doberman’s tail, crashing down at floor level? (2,3,5)

Definition: down at floor level · anagram + last letter

Someone ON ALL FOURS is down at floor level, hands and knees on the ground, about as low as a person gets.

  • Falls → FALLS straight fodder, five letters as written
  • over → O O is the scorer's abbreviation for an over, the set of six deliveries a cricket bowler sends down
  • our → OUR more fodder, three letters as written
  • Doberman’s tail → N the last letter of the dog's name
  • crashing — A crash leaves everything in a heap, so nothing it lands on keeps the order it arrived in.
  • tail — An animal's tail is at its rear end, so this takes the final letter of the word beside it.

Tripping over the dog's tail and going down in a heap.

'Falls over' reads as one phrase — a tumble — and it is two separate pieces, with 'over' supplying the cricketing O that the count needs.

7 down AVIATE

Fly from America, passing through borders of Timor-Leste (6)

Definition: Fly · charade + outer letters

To AVIATE is to fly — the verb behind 'aviation', used of piloting an aircraft.

  • America → A A is a standard abbreviation for America
  • passing through → VIA via means by way of, passing through somewhere on the route
  • borders of Timor-Leste → TE the outer letters of the country's name
  • borders of — A country's borders are its outer edges, so this takes the first and last letters of the name and nothing between them.

A flight leaving the States and crossing the edges of Timor-Leste.

Timor-Leste counts as one hyphenated name, so its borders are the T at the very front and the E at the very back, not the edges of each half.

8 down ELEVEN

Time for a snack, perhaps bite of Kissabel Eve nicked (6)

Definition: Time for a snack, perhaps · hidden word

ELEVEN o'clock is elevenses in Britain, the mid-morning snack break, so the hour stands for the snack; 'perhaps' flags one example.

  • Kissabel Eve nicked → ELEVEN the letters run on unbroken from the end of the apple's name, through the next word and into the one after
  • bite of — A bite is a small piece taken out of something bigger, which is what is being done to the words that follow.

Eve pinching a bite of an apple.

Kissabel is a red-fleshed apple variety, so the surface is Eve helping herself in the garden. Elevenses is the British mid-morning break the definition is pointing at.

10 down SHAKESPEAREAN

Are snake-shape bananas something to do with the Bard? (13)

Definition: something to do with the Bard · anagram

The Bard is Shakespeare, so anything SHAKESPEAREAN has to do with him: his plays, his language, his period.

  • Are snake-shape → ARESNAKESHAPE the fodder, thirteen letters, hyphen ignored
  • bananas — Going bananas is going mad, and letters described that way will not keep the order they arrived in.

Bananas bent into snake shapes, and whether Shakespeare has anything to do with it.

Thirteen letters of fodder is far too many to shuffle in your head, so count them against the enumeration first — the -EAN ending is where this one starts to give way.

14 down NINETY-NINE

Ice cream, almost a ton! (6-4)

Definition: Ice cream · double definition

A 99 is the British ice-cream cone with a Flake in it, and a ton is a hundred, so one short of a ton is NINETY-NINE.

  • Ice cream → the British cone with a chocolate Flake pushed into it, ordered from the van by its number
  • almost a ton → a ton is British slang for a hundred, so stopping one short of it gives the number wanted

A helping of ice cream so enormous it nearly weighs a ton.

A ton is British slang for a hundred — of miles an hour, or of runs at cricket — so 'almost a ton' is arithmetic rather than weight.

17 down PARALLEL

Spar all-electric ferries running alongside (8)

Definition: running alongside · hidden word

Lines that are PARALLEL run alongside one another at a constant distance and never meet.

  • Spar all-electric → PARALLEL the letters run straight on from inside the first word into the second, across the hyphen
  • ferries — To ferry something is to carry it along, and these words are carrying the letters across inside them.

Electric ferries sailing side by side past a mooring spar.

'Spar all-electric' reads as a boat's specification, and the hyphen does the camouflage: the letters carry on straight through it as though it were not there.

18 down ROADSHOW

Spooner’s displayed fish eggs for mobile exhibition? (8)

Definition: mobile exhibition · spoonerism

A ROADSHOW is a mobile exhibition — a display that travels from town to town instead of staying in one hall.

  • displayed → ROAD displayed means showed; hand its opening sh- over to the other word and take back an r- in exchange
  • fish eggs → SHOW fish eggs are roe; give up its r- for the sh- that has just been swapped across
  • Spooner’s — Spooner was the Oxford don famous for swapping the opening sounds of two words, so his name asks you to trade them here.

A display of fish eggs billed as a touring exhibition.

Say 'showed roe' and trade the sounds at the front of the two words. The vowels drift a little as you write down what you hear, which is normal for a spoonerism and why the spelling looks nothing like the source.

21 down HEARTS

Hot ear, left at last, small signs of love? (6)

Definition: signs of love · charade + last letter

HEARTS are signs of love: the symbol drawn on cards and letters, so the plural names the shapes themselves.

  • Hot → H H is the letter on the hot tap
  • ear → EAR the word itself, three letters as written
  • left at last → T the final letter of 'left'
  • small → S S is the clothing size for small
  • at last — At last points to the very end, so only the final letter of the word before it is used.

Someone complaining of a hot left ear, alongside small tokens of affection.

The definition wants the drawn symbol rather than the organ that beats, which is why the plural sits so oddly at the end of a sentence about one ear.

22 down PEPPER

Energy for every season (6)

Definition: season · charade

To PEPPER food is to season it, peppering being the commonest single act of seasoning there is.

  • Energy → PEP pep is energy, liveliness, get-up-and-go
  • for every → PER per means for each or for every, as in miles per hour

Vitality to see you through any time of year.

'Season' is the verb, not the noun — nothing to do with spring or autumn, everything to do with what you shake over dinner.

26 down OBOE

Instrument to plumb loo pipe ends (4)

Definition: Instrument · last letters

An OBOE is an instrument — the double-reed woodwind — so 'instrument' defines it by example.

  • to plumb loo pipe → OBOE the final letter of each of these four words, taken in order
  • ends — The end of a word is its final letter, and this takes one from each of the words before it.

Sounding the depths of the lavatory pipework.

'Instrument' looks like it will be a plumber's tool in a sentence about pipes, which is the whole of the misdirection — it is the woodwind.
