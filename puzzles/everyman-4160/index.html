Observer Everyman Crossword No 4,160

Setter: Everyman · Published: Saturday 11 July 2026 · Difficulty: Moderate (harder than 20% of the puzzles here) · Grid: 15×15

Across

1 across LEGISLATOR

Allegorist blasted senator? (10)

Definition: senator? · anagram

senator? → LEGISLATOR: definition by example, since a senator is one member of a law-making chamber and MPs and congressmen qualify just as well.

  • Allegorist → LEGISLATOR the ten letters of ALLEGORIST, laid out afresh
  • blasted — Something blasted has been blown apart, and the pieces never come down in the order they went up.

Two ten-letter words made of one set of letters, and the question mark is doing real work: it admits that only some lawmakers are senators.

6 across EMIT

Give out prison sentence - in recess? (4)

Definition: Give out · reversal

Give out → EMIT: the technical verb for sending something out from a source, what a lamp does with light or a chimney with smoke.

  • prison sentence → EMIT a prison sentence is TIME — doing time — and set back it reads the other way round
  • in recess — A recess is a space set back into a wall, so whatever sits in one has been pushed backwards.

TIME is the crossword's standard prison sentence, and a four-letter word that spells something else backwards will nearly always be asked to.

9 across SIDE BY SIDE

Yes, biddies sauntering two abreast (4,2,4)

Definition: two abreast · anagram

two abreast → SIDE BY SIDE: the marching phrase for a pair level with one another rather than one trailing behind.

  • Yes, biddies → SIDE BY SIDE the ten letters of YES BIDDIES, dealt out again
  • sauntering — To saunter is to wander with no route in mind, and letters that wander do not come back in the order they set off in.

'Biddies' does all the misdirection: it summons a pair of gossiping old women strolling along, which is very nearly the picture the answer paints.

10 across STYE

Source of irritation: pious type with Biblical term for you (4)

Definition: Source of irritation · charade

Source of irritation → STYE: the swollen lump on an eyelid, named here by the discomfort it causes rather than by what it is.

  • pious type → ST a pious type is a saint, shortened to ST in front of a name
  • Biblical term for you → YE YE is how the King James Bible addresses you

ST for saint and YE for you are two of the crossword's steadiest abbreviations, and the colon is the only joint the clue needs.

11 across COLLARED DOVE

Arrested pacifist; one might say 'coo!' (8,4)

Definition: one might say 'coo!' · charade

one might say 'coo!' → COLLARED DOVE: the bird is defined by its voice, that soft repeated note being what everyone knows it for.

  • Arrested → COLLARED to collar somebody is to arrest them, the policeman's verb
  • pacifist → DOVE in politics the dove wants peace and the hawk wants war

Hawks and doves are the standing pair for warmongers and peacemakers, and the exclamation at the end is a bird's call rather than surprise.

15 across PRECEDE

Herald in quiet decline (7)

Definition: Herald · charade

Herald → PRECEDE: to herald a thing is to come before it and announce it, and the answer keeps the coming-before while dropping the announcing.

  • quiet → P p is piano on a score, the musician's mark for quiet
  • decline → RECEDE to recede is to decline, as a tide or a hairline does

P for quiet comes from music, where piano means softly — one of the first abbreviations to bank, because it turns up most weeks.

16 across YUMMIER

After year, uncapped premium adjusted: more attractive (7)

Definition: more attractive · charade + deletion + anagram

more attractive → YUMMIER: the nursery word for delicious, stretched by adults to mean good-looking, with the comparative matching the -er.

  • year → Y y is the standard abbreviation for year
  • uncapped premium → UMMIER premium without its cap is REMIUM, and those six letters rearrange to UMMIER
  • After — This says where to stand rather than what to do: everything else falls in behind, so the year goes first.
  • uncapped — A cap sits on top of a thing, so a word uncapped has had the letter at its head taken off.
  • adjusted — To adjust something is to shift its parts until they sit differently, and letters shifted like that come out in a new order.

'Uncapped' beheads rather than shortens, and knowing which end of a word an instruction eats is half the work in clues like this.

17 across CORRUPT

Blimey: point at end of game is rotten (7)

Definition: rotten · charade

rotten → CORRUPT: the moral sense of the word, a rotten official rather than rotten fruit, which is the decay the answer names.

  • Blimey → COR cor is the exclamation blimey stands in for
  • game → RU the game is rugby union, RU on any fixture list
  • point → PT pt is the everyday abbreviation for point
  • at end of — The end of a thing is where it stops, so the piece sent there comes last and not first.

Three scraps of shorthand in a row — an exclamation, a sport and a measurement — while the surface pretends to be somebody watching a match.

19 across ON THE GO

Busy month; egomaniac not seen throughout (2,3,2)

Definition: Busy · hidden word

Busy → ON THE GO: the idiom for somebody moving from one task straight to the next, busy in the sense of never sitting down.

  • month; egomaniac → ON THE GO the letters run consecutively, starting inside 'month' and finishing inside 'egomaniac'
  • not seen throughout — If a thing cannot be seen all the way through, only a stretch of it shows — and a stretch is all you take.

A hidden word straddling a semicolon is beautifully disguised, because punctuation tells the eye to stop at the very place the answer carries on.

20 across HEAVENS ABOVE

Gosh, cast earlier mentioned to include Poles (7,5)

Definition: Gosh · charade + container

Gosh → HEAVENS ABOVE: both are exclamations of surprise, the answer being the full pious version that 'gosh' politely trims down.

  • cast → HEAVE to cast something is to heave it
  • Poles → NS N and S are the two poles, north and south
  • earlier mentioned → ABOVE what a document mentioned earlier is the above, as in the above-named
  • to include — To include is to take a thing in, so the letters named next go inside what has already been built.

Nothing here is theatrical: 'cast' is the verb to throw, and 'the above' is what legal and academic English calls whatever it said earlier.

23 across EVIL

Everyman's beginning to get very off-colour, mostly wrong (4)

Definition: wrong · charade + first letter + deletion

wrong → EVIL: the far end of the same scale, where an act stops being a mistake and becomes wickedness.

  • Everyman's beginning → E Everyman is the setter's name, and it starts with E
  • very off-colour → VIL very off-colour is VILE, of which most is the first three letters
  • beginning — A word's beginning is the single letter it starts with, and the setter has offered his own name to supply one.
  • mostly — Mostly is most but not all, and the bit left behind is the letter on the end.

Everyman signs nearly every puzzle he sets, so his name in a clue is almost always letters rather than a person — here just the one at the front.

24 across TEETOTALER

American lacking spirit - and never stout? (10)

Definition: American lacking spirit - and never stout? · cryptic definition

The whole clue is the definition: somebody who takes no alcohol at all, spirits or beer, with the nationality fixing the single-L spelling.

  • lacking spirit → on the surface this is somebody with no fire or courage in them, which is the reading to let go of
  • never stout → not a remark about anybody's build: stout is a drink, and so is the spirit beside it
  • American → the nationality is really a spelling instruction, since the States write this word with one L where Britain uses two

Two drinks and no letters: spirit and stout both belong in a glass, and the person described is the one who touches neither of them.

25 across SINK

Scuttle, a bathroom fixture? (4)

Definition: Scuttle · double definition

Scuttle → SINK, the verb for putting a ship under on purpose; a bathroom fixture → SINK, the basin, so the clue uses a verb and a noun.

  • Scuttle → to scuttle a ship is to hole her deliberately and send her to the bottom
  • a bathroom fixture? → the plumbed basin, which takes its name from what the water does in it

Both halves are about water going down, which is what hides the split: one is something you do to a ship, the other something bolted to a wall.

26 across BALLADEERS

Dance event, while getting maybe some bucks invested, finding singers (10)

Definition: singers · charade + container

singers → BALLADEERS: singers of ballads, the slow narrative songs, so the general word fairly covers the specialists.

  • Dance event → BALL the dance event is a ball
  • while → AS while means as, the two swapping freely in 'while I was out'
  • maybe some bucks → DEER bucks are male deer, and 'maybe' allows the general animal to stand for the particular one
  • invested — To invest is to clothe or wrap around, so what is invested ends up inside the letters rather than after them.

'Bucks' is the pivot: dollars on the surface, animals in the wordplay, and 'maybe' is the flag saying one kind of deer stands for all.

Down

1 down LIST

Tip one of 13, perhaps (4)

Definition: Tip · double definition

Tip → LIST, the lean of a ship rather than a hint; one of 13 → LIST by example, a spec sheet being one itemised run of entries.

  • Tip → a ship leaning over to one side does this verb — the tilt, not the topple
  • one of 13, perhaps → solve 13 down first: each of those documents is an itemised run of things

Everyman likes a cross-reference, and it always costs you a second clue: 13 down has to fall before the second half of this one means anything.

2 down GODS

Disheartened, merchandise is Mars etc (4)

Definition: Mars etc · deletion

Mars etc → GODS: definition by example in the plural, Mars being the Roman god of war and 'etc' calling up Jupiter, Venus and the rest.

  • merchandise → GODS merchandise is GOODS, and the middle letter of those five is taken out
  • Disheartened — A word that has lost heart has lost the letter at its very centre, not its courage.

Mars is a planet on the surface and a Roman deity underneath, and the little 'etc' is what makes the definition plural.

3 down SUBMOLECULE

Columbus grappling with eel that's very small (11)

Definition: that's very small · anagram

that's very small → SUBMOLECULE: a part of a molecule, so the word names something below even molecular scale.

  • Columbus grappling with eel → SUBMOLECULE the eleven letters of COLUMBUS and EEL, dealt out again
  • grappling with — Two bodies locked together and thrashing about end up in a heap, which is what the letters beside this do.

A long anagram is friendlier than it looks: count the fodder first, and eight letters plus three is exactly the eleven the grid is asking for.

4 down ABSOLVE

Pardon? First couple of letters complete a crossword? (7)

Definition: Pardon · charade + first letters

Pardon → ABSOLVE: the formal sense of clearing someone of a charge, the priest's and the judge's word rather than the polite one.

  • letters → AB the letters meant are the alphabet's own, whose opening pair is A and B
  • complete a crossword → SOLVE to complete a crossword is to solve it
  • First couple of — A couple is two, and the first two of anything are the pair at the very front of the sequence.

Two question marks and neither does the same job: the first is the surface saying 'what?', the second admits that solving is only one way to finish a grid.

5 down ORDERLY

Trim hospital employee (7)

Definition: Trim · double definition

Trim → ORDERLY as an adjective meaning tidy; hospital employee → ORDERLY as a noun, the attendant who keeps a ward running.

  • Trim → the adjective: neat, everything in its place and nothing sticking out
  • hospital employee → the noun: the ward attendant who moves patients and equipment about

A three-word clue splits after the first word, which is the hardest split to see — 'trim' looks as though it belongs to the employee.

7 down METEORITES

Pieces of rock opera with ceremonies in which hero regularly crushed (10)

Definition: Pieces of rock · charade + container + alternate letters

Pieces of rock → METEORITES: the lumps of space debris that survive the fall to earth, so the plain phrase is literally what they are.

  • opera → MET New York's Metropolitan Opera is the Met
  • hero → EO crush out the h and the r and hErO leaves its second and fourth letters
  • ceremonies → RITES ceremonies are rites
  • in which — This points back at everything just assembled, so the next letters drop inside it rather than line up after it.
  • regularly crushed — Crush letters out of a word at fixed intervals and what stays standing is every other one.

The definition stops at 'rock', though the surface wants you to read 'rock opera' as one thing. MET for the Metropolitan is the abbreviation worth banking.

8 down THE YEAR DOT

Spooner's beloved sailing vessel? That was ages ago (3,4,3)

Definition: ages ago · spoonerism

ages ago → THE YEAR DOT: the idiom names the earliest date anyone can imagine, and English uses it to mean as far back as memory goes.

  • beloved → YEAR a beloved is a dear, and Spooner hands its D over in exchange for a Y
  • sailing vessel → DOT a sailing vessel is a yacht, said 'yot', and it takes the D that dear gave up
  • Spooner's — The Reverend Spooner traded the opening sounds of two words, so his name alone is the instruction to swap them.

The article comes free: nobody says 'year dot' without a 'the' in front of it, so the phrase arrives whole once the swap is made.

12 down DEMOTIVATED

Less enthused as date vomited all over the place (11)

Definition: Less enthused · anagram

Less enthused → DEMOTIVATED: the de- prefix strips drive away, so the keenness was there first and has since been taken off.

  • date vomited → DEMOTIVATED the eleven letters of DATE VOMITED, brought back in a new order
  • all over the place — Something all over the place has no arrangement left, which is exactly what the letters beside it are asked to lose.

The setter splits a phrase you know: 'vomited all over the place' reads as one thing, yet only the last four words give the instruction.

13 down SPEC SHEETS

Speech sets out iterated criteria (4,6)

Definition: iterated criteria · anagram

iterated criteria → SPEC SHEETS: a specification sheet runs through, point by point, every standard the product has to meet.

  • Speech sets → SPEC SHEETS the ten letters of SPEECH SETS, re-dealt
  • out — Out of true, out of shape, out of order: this little word carries the sense of an arrangement that no longer holds.

Watch where the instruction sits: 'sets' belongs to the letters and only 'out' does the shuffling, which is why 'sets out' reads so innocently.

14 down RECREATION

Once again, making fun (10)

Definition: fun · charade

fun → RECREATION: the formal word for off-duty time, so 'fun' names the point of the activity rather than the activity itself.

  • Once again → RE RE- is the prefix English puts on anything done over
  • making → CREATION the making of a thing is its creation

The joke is the hyphen you cannot see: built as re-creation it is making something over, read as one word it is what you do on a day off.

18 down TESSERA

Primarily: tile; equally, small stone enhancing Roman art? (7)

Definition: Primarily: tile; equally, small stone enhancing Roman art? · &lit + first letters

The whole clue reads straight: a tessera is the little cube of a mosaic, a tile that is equally a stone, set into Roman floors.

  • tile; equally, small stone enhancing Roman art → TESSERA the opening letters of those seven words, taken in order
  • Primarily — Primarily means first of all, so what is wanted from each word after it is the letter it starts with.

Seven words, seven initials — and the sentence supplying them is also a fair description of the thing, which is what lifts this above a plain acrostic.

19 down ORBITAL

A little alligator bit a lamprey that's going around (7)

Definition: going around · hidden word

going around → ORBITAL: the adjective for anything that circles, whether a satellite's path or the motorway that rings a city.

  • alligator bit a lamprey → ORBITAL the letters run consecutively, spanning the tail of 'alligator', all of 'bit' and 'a', and the front of 'lamprey'
  • A little — A little of something is a portion of it, so only a slice of the words that follow is being offered.

The eye reads whole words before it reads letters, and a word as long as 'alligator' can hide most of an answer in its own tail.

21 down BLUE

Loudly trumpeted: that's indecent (4)

Definition: indecent · homophone

indecent → BLUE: the settled English sense of smut — a blue joke, a blue film — rather than the colour or the low mood.

  • trumpeted → BLUE to trumpet a thing is to blow a horn about it, so trumpeted is BLEW — and that sound is written the other way here
  • Loudly — Said out loud, so what matters is the noise a word makes and not the way it is spelt.

A past tense is a favourite hiding place for a homophone: these two words are four letters apart on the page and identical in the air.

22 down IRIS

Maybe Murdoch is memoirist? Not entirely (4)

Definition: Maybe Murdoch · hidden word

Maybe Murdoch → IRIS: definition by example, one Murdoch among several, with 'maybe' the setter admitting that the surname alone does not pick her out.

  • memoirist → IRIS the letters sit consecutively in the middle of 'memoirist'
  • Not entirely — Not entirely means you are not being handed the whole of it, so take only the part that is wanted.

Iris Murdoch wrote the novels, and 'maybe' is there because a bare surname could just as easily belong to Rupert.
