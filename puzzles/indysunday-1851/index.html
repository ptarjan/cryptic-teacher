1 across GHOSTBUSTERS

Dispiriting picture? (12)

Definition: Dispiriting picture? · cryptic definition

Ghostbusters is a 1984 picture whose heroes remove spirits from buildings for money, which is dispiriting read as two words.

  • Dispiriting → read as one word it means demoralising; broken in two it is an instruction to take the spirits out of a place
  • picture? → a picture is a film, and the question mark warns that what follows is a particular title rather than a synonym

A photograph or film so gloomy that looking at it takes the heart out of you.

'Dispiriting' hides a hyphen: dis-spiriting, the taking away of spirits, which is a job description rather than a mood. The picture is the 1984 comedy about the men who do that job.

9 across HYDRATED

Moisturised skin you hear admired (8)

Definition: Moisturised · charade + homophone

Something hydrated has had water put into it, which is exactly what moisturising skin does.

  • skin → HYD an animal's skin is its hide, and that is the word being said aloud
  • admired → RATED to rate somebody is to think well of them — 'I rate her'
  • you hear — You hear moves the word from the page into the ear, so what counts is the noise it makes rather than how it is spelt.

Somebody's well-moisturised skin drawing audible admiration.

'You hear' does a full homophone instruction in two short words. RATED for admired is ordinary British usage, and the sounded word loses its silent E on the way onto the page.

10 across ACUMEN

Ability to discern Caesar's head among gold pieces (6)

Definition: Ability to discern · container + charade + first letter

Acumen is sharp practical judgement, the knack of seeing quickly and clearly what a situation really is.

  • gold → AU au is the chemical symbol for gold, and it is split apart here
  • Caesar's head → C the head of the word Caesar, its first letter
  • pieces → MEN chess and draughts call their playing pieces men
  • among — Something among other things sits in the middle of them, so the letter named goes between the two beside it.

Caesar's portrait spotted on a coin among a heap of gold pieces.

'Caesar's head' looks like the emperor stamped on a coin and means the first letter of his name. The board-game sense of pieces is the other half of the disguise.

11 across FLATLY

France in recent times barring English outright (6)

Definition: outright · charade + deletion

To refuse flatly is to refuse outright: both say the thing was done plainly, leaving no room for argument.

  • France → F F is the international vehicle code for France, off the sticker on the car
  • in recent times → LATELY lately means in recent times, six letters to work from
  • English → E E is the standard abbreviation for English, and that letter is the one shut out
  • barring — To bar something is to shut it out, so the letter named is kept out of the word it would otherwise sit in.

France has recently shut English out altogether.

The whole clue reads as a row about language policy, which is why 'barring English' passes as politics rather than an instruction. F for France comes off the oval sticker on the back of a car.

12 across ANECDOTE

Narrative one acted out (8)

Definition: Narrative · anagram

An anecdote is a short narrative of something that happened, usually told to make a point or raise a laugh.

  • one acted → ONEACTED anagram fodder, eight letters
  • out → anagram indicator
  • out — Acting something out means performing it in another form, so the letters beside it are re-staged in a new order.

A story about somebody being performed rather than told.

'Out' carries the whole instruction from the end of the clue, where it reads as part of the ordinary phrase 'acted out'. Eight letters of fodder for an eight-letter answer is the check that settles it.

13 across SPAM

Plans to review unopened emails? (4)

Definition: unopened emails? · reversal

Spam is unsolicited bulk email, the stuff that lands in the junk folder and is deleted without ever being read.

  • Plans → MAPS maps are plans in the draughtsman's sense, the drawings of a layout
  • to review — To review is to look back over something, so the word it points at is read from its far end back.

Somebody intending to go back through the mail they never got round to opening.

Spam took its name from the tinned meat by way of a Monty Python sketch in which a cafe served nothing else. In the surface 'to review' is a plan for the afternoon.

14 across CARGO PANTS

Wheels turn - factory turning out large and small trousers (5,5)

Definition: trousers · charade + deletion

Cargo pants are the loose trousers with big patch pockets down the thighs, so the definition names the garment they are a kind of.

  • Wheels → CAR wheels is slang for a car — 'nice wheels'
  • turn → GO your turn at a game is your go
  • factory → PLANT a plant is a factory or industrial works
  • large → L L is the clothing size large, and that letter is the one thrown out
  • and small → S S is the clothing size small, added on at the end
  • turning out — Turning somebody out is putting them out of the building, so the letter named is ejected from the word before it.

A production line rolling while the factory stops making the large and small sizes.

Wheels for a car and go for a turn are both everyday slang doing crossword duty. The factory's 'turning out' reads as production and means eviction, which is what hides the deletion.

16 across PROSECUTOR

Lawyer's basic writing cleverer when spoken (10)

Definition: Lawyer · charade + homophone

'Lawyer' defines by example: a prosecutor is the particular lawyer who puts the case against the accused in a criminal court.

  • basic writing → PROSE prose is plain writing, the basic kind that is not verse
  • cleverer → CUTOR cute means shrewd as well as pretty — a cute operator is a sharp one — so cleverer is cuter, and that is the word said aloud
  • when spoken — When spoken shifts the word from the page into the ear, so the sound is what gets written down instead of the spelling.

A solicitor's plain writing sounding much smarter once it is read out.

The shrewd sense of cute is the one to keep: it survives in 'don't get cute with me'. Said aloud, that comparative lands on an ending English spells another way.

18 across PURE

Son pinched from mom's handbag without guilt (4)

Definition: without guilt · deletion

Somebody pure is unstained by wrongdoing, so 'without guilt' describes the state itself rather than a court's verdict.

  • mom's handbag → PURSE in American English a purse is the handbag itself, five letters to work from
  • Son → S s is the standard abbreviation for son in family trees, and that letter is the one taken
  • pinched from — To pinch is to steal, so the letter named is lifted out of the word that follows and does not come back.

A son helping himself from his mother's handbag and feeling not the slightest guilt.

'Mom' rather than mum is the flag: in American English a purse is the handbag, not the little thing kept inside it. Reading it as British loses the whole five letters.

19 across VENDETTA

500 gatecrashing ground at event prolonged antagonism (8)

Definition: prolonged antagonism · anagram + container

A vendetta is a blood feud handed down between families, so the antagonism is prolonged by definition rather than by accident.

  • at event → ATEVENT anagram fodder, seven letters, re-laid to leave a gap in the middle
  • 500 → D D is 500 in Roman numerals
  • gatecrashing — A gatecrasher gets inside an event nobody invited them to, so the letter named forces its way into the middle of the rest.
  • ground — Ground means milled to powder, so what it points at comes apart and is rebuilt from its letters.

Five hundred uninvited guests turning up at an event on the ground.

'Ground' reads as the field the event is held on and works as the milling instruction, and the two readings sit in the same three words. Roman numerals are the setter's usual way of writing a round number.

21 across SERAPH

Angel shaves around front of her ... (6)

Definition: Angel · reversal + charade + first letter

A seraph is an angel of the highest of the nine orders, the six-winged kind that stands nearest the throne of God.

  • shaves → SERAP to shave a surface down is to pare it, and those five letters are then turned round
  • her → H the letter her begins with
  • ... → surface only — the ellipsis runs the sentence on and carries no letters
  • around — Turning something around reverses the direction it is read in, so those letters come out tail first.
  • front of — The front of a word is the letter it begins with, and that single letter is all that is wanted.

A barber trimming round the front of a woman's hair.

Seraphim are the top rank of angel, and this is the singular. 'Shaves' wants the carpenter's sense of paring something down rather than anything to do with a razor.

22 across BORZOI

Dog bin unfinished pasta goes in (6)

Definition: Dog · container + deletion

The borzoi is the Russian wolfhound, a tall narrow-headed sighthound, so 'Dog' defines the answer by example.

  • bin unfinished → BI BIN stopped short of its last letter, leaving two
  • pasta → ORZO orzo is the small rice-shaped pasta used in soups
  • unfinished — An unfinished word stops before its last letter, so only what comes before that letter is used.
  • goes in — What goes in something ends up inside it, so the letters named are planted in the middle of the word before them.

A dog going through the kitchen bin where the leftover pasta went.

Orzo is worth knowing for the letters it hands over, and 'bin' is doing two jobs at once: rubbish in the picture, three letters minus one in the wordplay.

23 across IDOLATRY

Worship as bride and groom agree, initially like a shot (8)

Definition: Worship · charade + first letter

Idolatry is worship of the wrong object — an image or an idol — and by extension any reverence given to something as if it were a god.

  • as bride and groom agree → IDO what each of them says at the altar, written as two words
  • like → L the first letter of Like, taken on its own
  • a → A the single letter stands for itself
  • shot → TRY a shot at something is a try, as in give it a go
  • initially — Initially wants a word at its start only, so the word beside it gives up its first letter and nothing else.

A wedding where the couple say their vows without a moment's hesitation.

'Initially' governs only the word next to it, and the A that follows stands for itself — the trap is taking the initials of all three words. 'Like a shot' means instantly in the surface.

24 across DERWENTWATER

Wetter wander around part of the Lake District (12)

Definition: part of the Lake District · anagram

Derwentwater is one of the larger lakes in Cumbria, so the definition places the answer as an example of what the region is made of.

  • Wetter wander → WETTERWANDER anagram fodder, twelve letters
  • around → anagram indicator
  • around — A wander around follows no fixed route, so the letters beside it end up anywhere but where they started.

A damper walk than the last one, taken through a corner of the Lake District.

Derwentwater sits just south of Keswick, in the wettest county in England, which is what makes the surface and the answer describe the same afternoon.

2 down HARD TIMES

Book with Romeo in kept by (4,5)

Definition: Book · container + charade

Hard Times is Dickens's 1854 novel of Coketown, so 'Book' defines the answer by example rather than by synonym.

  • kept → HAD somebody who kept a thing had it
  • Romeo → R R is Romeo in the radio alphabet
  • by → TIMES three by four is three times four, so 'by' is the multiplication word
  • in — One thing described as in another sits inside it, so the single letter drops between the letters of the word beside it.

Somebody hanging on to a battered copy of a book with Romeo in it.

'By' is doing arithmetic, not authorship: it looks like the start of a byline and it means multiplied by. Dickens titles are worth knowing, since setters use them as ordinary phrases.

3 down SATAY

Nutty dish dropped onto chair, alas (5)

Definition: Nutty dish · charade

Satay is grilled meat on skewers served with a peanut sauce, so 'nutty' picks out the sauce the dish is known by.

  • dropped onto chair → SAT somebody who dropped onto a chair sat
  • alas → AY ay is the old cry of grief, as in 'ay me'

A plate of food landing on a chair, to everybody's dismay.

AY for 'alas' is old-fashioned crossword vocabulary worth storing: it is the sigh in 'ay me', not the yes of a vote. The surface hides it as an ordinary groan about spilt food.

4 down BID FAIR

Promise boyfriend with cramped identity breathing space (3,4)

Definition: Promise · container

To bid fair to do something is to look very likely to do it, so the phrase means promise in the sense of showing every sign.

  • boyfriend → BF bf is the everyday written shorthand for boyfriend, in texts and small ads
  • identity → ID your id is your identity, as on an id card
  • breathing space → AIR air is what breathing needs, and space full of it
  • cramped — Cramped means squeezed into too little room, so the chunks named are forced in between the letters of the shorthand beside it.

A boyfriend whose sense of himself feels hemmed in, asking for room to breathe.

Bid fair is an old phrase still alive in 'bids fair to succeed', meaning shows every sign of. Two separate chunks are pushed into a two-letter shorthand, which is why the answer looks nothing like its parts.

5 down STAGE DOOR

Wrinkly torso brought about elderly actor's exit (5,4)

Definition: actor's exit · anagram + container

The stage door is the plain door at the back of a theatre that the cast come and go through, so it is literally the way an actor leaves.

  • torso → TORSO five letters to be re-laid, and they end up split into two and three
  • elderly → AGED aged means elderly, and these four letters go in the gap
  • Wrinkly — Wrinkled cloth has been pushed out of its smooth order, so the letters beside it are not to be read in the order printed.
  • brought about — Bringing something about is bringing it round, so the letters just made are wrapped round the word that follows.

A creased old body turned round, and an elderly performer's last departure.

The surface reads as one continuous description of an ageing actor, and the ageing is fodder rather than description. A theatre's stage door is round the back, where the fans wait.

6 down EQUID

Horsey type last to imitate nicker (5)

Definition: Horsey type · charade + last letter

An equid is any member of the horse family — horses, asses and zebras — so 'horsey type' names the group by kind.

  • last to imitate → E the final letter of imitatE
  • nicker → QUID a nicker is British slang for one pound, and so is a quid
  • last to — Last to marks the letter a word ends on, so only that final letter is taken from the word beside it.

A horsey person being the last one to copy a neigh.

A nicker is a horse's soft whinny in the surface and a pound in the wordplay, and only one of those two senses buys anything. Both slang words for a pound sit on the same line here.

7 down SPECTATOR SPORT

Prescott tangling with prat, so fun to watch? (9,5)

Definition: fun to watch? · anagram

A spectator sport is one people go to look at rather than play, so 'fun to watch' is the whole point of the category.

  • Prescott → PRESCOTT anagram fodder, eight letters
  • prat, so → PRATSO six more letters of fodder; a prat is an idiot
  • tangling with → anagram indicator
  • tangling with — Tangled things are knotted out of their proper arrangement, so the words beside it are letters to be unpicked and re-laid.

A politician getting into a scuffle with a heckler, in front of a delighted crowd.

John Prescott was Britain's deputy prime minister, remembered for punching a heckler on camera — which is why the definition points straight back at the surface. Fourteen letters of fodder for a fourteen-letter answer settles the parse.

8 down CYCLE PER SECOND

Hertz essentially opposed to driving on the left behind bike (5,3,6)

Definition: Hertz · charade + first letter

One hertz is one complete oscillation in each second, so the unit's own definition is the answer written out in words.

  • bike → CYCLE a bike is a cycle, and it leads because the rest goes behind it
  • essentially → PERSE per se is the Latin for 'in itself', which is what essentially means
  • opposed to → CON con is the against side of pro and con
  • driving → D the opening letter of Driving, taken because it stands at that word's left
  • on the left — The left of a written word is where it starts, so only its opening letter is taken.
  • behind — Behind names a position, so everything described before it is written after the word it points at.

The car-hire firm, deep down against left-hand driving, stuck behind a bicycle.

Hertz reads as the rental company and is the unit of frequency named after Heinrich Hertz. Per se and con are both standard crossword vocabulary and are worth keeping.

14 down CICATRICE

Scar from treacherous Arctic floes? (9)

Definition: Scar · anagram

A cicatrice is the puckered mark left on skin once a wound has healed over, which is precisely a scar.

  • Arctic → ARCTIC anagram fodder, six letters
  • floes → ICE floes are sheets of floating ice, and those three letters join the fodder
  • treacherous → anagram indicator
  • treacherous — Treacherous ice cannot be trusted to stay where it is, so the letters beside it will not stay in the order given.

A wound picked up crossing dangerous floating ice in the far north.

Cicatrice is the medical word for a healed-over mark, from the Latin, and it turns up in crosswords far more than in conversation. The question mark admits the surface accident is invented.

15 down ASPARTAME

Equally mild, sub-standard additive (9)

Definition: additive · charade

Aspartame is the artificial sweetener put into diet drinks and sugar-free gum, so it is a food additive with its own E number.

  • Equally → AS as tall, as mild — 'as' is the word equally uses to compare
  • standard → PAR par is the standard score a golf hole is meant to take
  • mild → TAME a tame remark is a mild one, and it goes underneath
  • sub- — Sub means under, and in a down entry whatever is under something is written after it.

A tasting note calling something just as mild as the last and not up to standard.

'Sub-standard' looks like one hyphenated word of criticism and is two instructions with a join between them. Par for standard comes from golf, where par is the score a hole is meant to take.

17 down TSARIST

One supports aristocratic bears? (7)

Definition: One supports aristocratic bears? · &lit + hidden word

Read straight, the whole clue describes somebody who backs the rule of the tsars — the aristocratic Russia the bear stands for.

  • supports aristocratic → TSARIST seven consecutive letters, starting near the end of the first word and running on across the gap
  • bears — To bear something is to carry it, so the words beside it are carrying the letters wanted, in order, inside themselves.

Somebody cheering on the grand, well-bred bears of the Russian forest.

'Bears' is the containing instruction and the Russian joke at once, since the bear is the country's animal. Every word does double duty here: the sentence describes the person and spells them.

20 down DOZER

Pushing tractor, one rests briefly (5)

Definition: Pushing tractor · double definition

A dozer is short for bulldozer, the blade-fronted tractor that shoves earth about; a dozer is also somebody dropping off for a short light sleep.

  • Pushing tractor → the machine with a blade on the front that shoves earth ahead of it
  • one rests briefly → somebody having a short light sleep in a chair

Somebody shoving a broken-down tractor while a companion snatches a quick nap.

The two senses come from unrelated words that happen to end up spelt alike, one a shortened bulldozer and one an ordinary agent noun from doze. 'Briefly' belongs to the nap, not to any abbreviation.

21 down SKODA

Bill allows inverted badge on boots (5)

Definition: badge on boots · charade + reversal

Skoda is a Czech car maker, and a maker's name is the badge screwed to the boot lid at the back of the car.

  • Bill → AD a bill is a poster, which is an ad — a billboard is where bills are posted
  • allows → OKS to ok something is to allow it, and the clue wants the third-person form
  • inverted — Inverted means turned upside down, so in a down entry the letters assembled are read back from the bottom.

A man called Bill letting somebody stitch an upside-down badge onto his boots.

'Boots' are footwear in the surface and the back end of a car in the definition, which is where a maker puts its name. Bill for a poster is standard crossword vocabulary.
