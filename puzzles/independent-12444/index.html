Across

1 across WINDINESS

Flatulence of cricket team beginning to subside around November (9)

Definition: Flatulence · container + charade + first letter

Flatulence -> WINDINESS: wind is the polite word for gas in the gut, and windiness is the noun for suffering from it.

  • cricket team → WINDIES the Windies is the familiar name for the West Indies side
  • subside → S the first letter of the word, and nothing more of it
  • November → N November is N in the radio alphabet
  • beginning to — a thing only beginning to happen has got no further than its opening letter, which is all you take
  • around — to go around something is to enclose it, so the letters close up on either side of what follows

Cricket and the weather share a vocabulary, which is why 'cricket team' reads as eleven men rather than a nickname, and 'November' as a month rather than a signaller's letter.

6 across LEMMA

Premise of large book set in Highbury (5)

Definition: Premise · charade

Premise -> LEMMA: in logic a lemma is a subsidiary proposition proved on the way to a theorem, so it functions as a stepping-stone assumption.

  • large → L L is the clothing size and the standard short form of large
  • book set in Highbury → EMMA Jane Austen's novel is set in the village of Highbury

Highbury is a real corner of north London as well as Austen's invented village, and the surface leans on the football ground to keep you off the bookshelf.

9 across LEGAL

Allowed actor initially to enter stage left (5)

Definition: Allowed · container + charade + first letter

Allowed -> LEGAL: the sense is 'permitted by law', so a legal move is one you are allowed to make, not merely one concerning lawyers.

  • stage → LEG a leg is a stage of a journey, a race or a tournament
  • actor → A the first letter of the word, taken on its own
  • left → L L is the standard short form of left, as marked on a pair of gloves
  • initially — initially means at the very start, so only the word's opening letter is wanted
  • to enter — entering is going inside, so the letter is placed within the other pieces rather than after them

'Stage left' is a single theatrical direction on the surface and two separate pieces underneath, which is why the clue can afford to stop there.

10 across APOLOGIST

A game point for champion (9)

Definition: champion · charade

champion -> APOLOGIST: an apologist argues in defence of a doctrine or person, championing a cause in print rather than in a contest.

  • A → A the clue's own opening word, used exactly as it stands
  • game → POLO polo is the game played on horseback with mallets
  • point → GIST the gist of an argument is its essential point

The surface reads like a scoreboard, three words that belong together in a racket sport; underneath, none of them has anything to do with the others.

11 across SET TO MUSIC

Noted work? (3,2,5)

Definition: Noted work? · cryptic definition

Noted work? -> SET TO MUSIC: a poem handed to a composer has had notes put to it, so it becomes 'noted' in the literal, musical sense.

  • Noted → the surface reading is the adjective: celebrated, distinguished, the sort of thing people have heard of
  • work? → the setter's reading needs a piece of writing, and the question mark warns that 'noted' is about to change sense

There is nothing to take apart here. The whole clue is one definition read twice, and the pun is on notes as marks on a stave rather than fame.

12 across LENS

Starting late, foreigners bypassing one French city (4)

Definition: French city · deletion

French city -> LENS: definition by example — Lens is the old mining town in the Pas-de-Calais, best known now for its football club.

  • foreigners → ALIENS aliens are foreigners in the immigration officer's vocabulary
  • one → I one is I in Roman numerals, and it is the letter passed over
  • Starting late — begin the word later than it begins itself, and its own opening letter is left behind
  • bypassing — to bypass a place is to go round without entering it, so that letter is passed over

Two subtractions in a row is unusual, and both instructions read as ordinary travel English — starting late, bypassing somewhere — which is exactly why they hide.

14 across GEORDIE

Perhaps Declan Donnelly content to scavenge or diet (7)

Definition: Perhaps Declan Donnelly · hidden word

Perhaps Declan Donnelly -> GEORDIE: definition by example, flagged by 'Perhaps' — the Tyneside presenter is one native of Newcastle, not the word for all of them.

  • scavenge or diet → GEORDIE seven consecutive letters run straight across the two word breaks in this phrase
  • content — the content of something is what sits inside it, so the letters wanted are already there in the words that follow

Declan Donnelly is the Dec of Ant and Dec, both of them Newcastle men, so the name is an instance of the answer rather than a synonym for it.

15 across GAMELAN

Match tactics having quietly ignored SE Asian players (7)

Definition: SE Asian players · charade + deletion

SE Asian players -> GAMELAN: the tuned-percussion orchestra of Java and Bali, so the definition names it by region and by what it is made of.

  • Match → GAME a match and a game are the same fixture
  • tactics → PLAN a plan is the tactics settled on before kick-off
  • quietly → P piano, marked p on a score, is the instruction to play quietly
  • ignored — to ignore something is to leave it out altogether, so the letter named never reaches the grid

Football all the way through the surface, and the answer is a Javanese percussion orchestra — the distance between the two is the whole pleasure of the clue.

17 across ELLAGIC

Relating to acid derived from gall nuts put into melting ice (7)

Definition: Relating to acid · anagram + container

Relating to acid -> ELLAGIC: the adjective survives almost only in the phrase 'ellagic acid', so 'relating to acid' is as far as a plain definition goes.

  • gall → LLAG gall is bile, or brass-necked cheek, and its four letters are sent out of order
  • ice → EIC the three letters of the word, run together in a new order and then split apart to make room
  • nuts — nuts means out of one's mind, and letters that have lost their reason will not stay in the order printed
  • put into — says that one set of letters is placed inside the other rather than alongside it
  • melting — ice that melts loses its shape entirely, so its letters run into a new arrangement

The surface is a chemistry lesson and every word of it is true: the acid really is extracted from oak galls. Only the first three words are the definition.

19 across GENERAL

Army officer is common (7)

Definition: Army officer · double definition

Army officer -> GENERAL: the senior commissioned rank; common -> GENERAL: widespread or prevailing, as in general opinion, rather than common meaning vulgar.

  • Army officer → GENERAL the senior commissioned rank in an army, above a brigadier
  • common → GENERAL the adjective for something widespread, as in a rule that holds everywhere

Four words and no wordplay: the clue is two meanings laid end to end, with 'is' doing the work of an equals sign between them.

20 across TOWN

Pull close to station in Barnsley? (4)

Definition: Barnsley? · charade + last letter

Barnsley? -> TOWN: definition by example — the South Yorkshire place is one, and the question mark admits it is an instance rather than a synonym.

  • Pull → TOW to tow a caravan is to pull it
  • station → N the final letter of the word
  • close to — close here is the noun meaning an ending, so you want the letter the word closes with

'Close to station' reads as an estate agent's boast, which is the disguise; the question mark on Barnsley is the honest signal that it is only an example.

22 across ENANTIOMER

Awkwardly mention ear — it's one of a pair, the mirror image of the other (10)

Definition: one of a pair, the mirror image of the other · anagram

one of a pair, the mirror image of the other -> ENANTIOMER: in chemistry, either of two molecules whose atoms are arranged as non-superimposable mirror images.

  • mention ear → MENTIONEAR the ten letters of these two words are exactly the material needed
  • Awkwardly — doing a thing awkwardly is doing it clumsily, and letters handled clumsily come out in the wrong order

An ear really is one of a mirrored pair, so fodder and definition are describing the same thing — a rare clue where the surface tells the truth twice.

25 across UNDERMINE

Weaken female leaving to provide finance for fur (9)

Definition: Weaken · deletion + charade

Weaken -> UNDERMINE: literally to dig away what something stands on, and by extension to erode a person's authority or an argument's strength.

  • provide finance → FUND to fund a project is to provide the finance for it
  • female → F F is the standard short form of female, and it is the letter that goes
  • fur → ERMINE ermine is the white winter coat of the stoat, worn on ceremonial robes
  • leaving — what leaves a word is no longer in it, so the letter named walks out before the pieces are joined

The surface is a campaigning story about funding the fur trade, which is the sort of topical sentence that stops you counting letters.

26 across AS YET

So far, simple to cycle in front of Tesla (2,3)

Definition: So far · cycling + charade

So far -> AS YET: both are idioms for 'up to this point in time', and both nearly always sit next to a negative — nothing has happened yet.

  • simple → ASYE easy means simple, and its leading E has been sent round to the back
  • Tesla → T T is the symbol for the tesla, the SI unit of magnetic flux density
  • to cycle — cycling brings you round to where you started, so a letter dropped off one end reappears at the other
  • in front of — tells you which piece leads: the cycled word goes ahead of the single letter, not after it

Tesla is a car, a physicist and a unit; the surface wants the first of those and the wordplay wants the third, with 'cycle' keeping the traffic going.

27 across ERRED

Made a mistake about western wine (5)

Definition: Made a mistake · reversal + charade

Made a mistake -> ERRED: to err is the formal verb for going wrong, and the past tense of it matches the past tense of the definition.

  • about → ER about is RE in the crossword's shorthand, as in 're your letter', set down westward
  • wine → RED red is the wine you order by colour rather than by grape
  • western — west is to the left on a map, and an across answer written westward is read from its right-hand end back

'Western wine' sounds like a region — Rioja, Napa — and neither word is doing what it looks like: one is an instruction, the other a colour.

28 across MAGNETRON

Nursing officer welcoming revolutionary English microwave source (9)

Definition: microwave source · container + reversal

microwave source -> MAGNETRON: the vacuum tube that generates microwaves, whether in a radar transmitter or in the back of a kitchen oven.

  • Nursing officer → MATRON a matron is the senior nurse in charge of a hospital ward
  • English → GNE English shortens to ENG, and it is turned round before it goes in
  • welcoming — to welcome someone is to take them in, so the outer word opens up and receives the inner one
  • revolutionary — a revolution turns a thing right round, so these letters are written down from the end backwards

'Revolutionary English' reads as a person — Cromwell, Paine — and is only an instruction plus an abbreviation, which is the whole of the disguise here.

Down

1 down WILTS

County flags (5)

Definition: County · double definition

County -> WILTS: the accepted abbreviation for Wiltshire; flags -> WILTS: a plant losing turgor droops, and enthusiasm that flags sags the same way.

  • County → WILTS the recognised short form of Wiltshire, as written on an address
  • flags → WILTS a plant that flags is drooping for want of water, and so is a flagging spirit

Two words, two definitions, no wordplay at all — and the trap is 'flags', which reads as bunting on a county show stall rather than as a verb.

2 down NIGHT SOIL

Things struggling with fossil fuel waste (5,4)

Definition: waste · anagram + charade

waste -> NIGHT SOIL: night soil is the old term for human excrement carted away after dark, so it is waste in the most literal sense.

  • Things → THINGS the six letters of this word are the material to be rearranged
  • fossil fuel → OIL oil is the fossil fuel that is drilled rather than mined
  • struggling — something struggling is thrashing about and will not hold still, so its letters do not stay where they were put

'Fossil fuel waste' hangs together far too well as a phrase, and that is the trap: only the first two words of it belong to the wordplay.

3 down ISLE OF DOGS

Reportedly, I adore residents of Battersea, part of London (4,2,4)

Definition: part of London · homophone + charade

part of London -> ISLE OF DOGS: the Thames peninsula in Tower Hamlets, now dominated by Canary Wharf — named here by category, not by name.

  • I adore → ISLE OF to adore is to love, and said at speed those two words come out as two quite different ones
  • residents of Battersea → DOGS the home in Battersea has been taking in strays of the barking kind since 1860
  • Reportedly — what is reported is spoken rather than written, and the match asked for is a run-together of speech, not an exact pairing of sounds

The whole surface is one fond sentence about animal rescue, and it has to be said out loud before it turns into a place on the Thames.

4 down ERASURE

Times regularly queried showing synth-pop duo (7)

Definition: synth-pop duo · charade + alternate letters

synth-pop duo -> ERASURE: definition by example, since Vince Clarke and Andy Bell's band is one such duo rather than the class of them.

  • Times → ERAS an era is a stretch of history, so eras are times
  • queried → URE the second, fourth and sixth letters of the word, in that order
  • regularly — at regular intervals means every second one, so you count off alternate letters and let the rest go

The italics point at the newspaper and the capital is the surface's doing; the word is wanted as a plain noun meaning ages.

5 down SMOKING

Family stopping small cat doing unhealthy activity (7)

Definition: doing unhealthy activity · container + charade

doing unhealthy activity -> SMOKING: the -ing form names the act of using tobacco, the textbook example of a habit that damages health.

  • small → S S is the label on the smallest shirt in the pile
  • cat → MOG a mog, or moggie, is an ordinary household cat
  • Family → KIN your kin are your family, near and distant
  • stopping — to stop somewhere is to lodge there for a while, so this word takes up residence inside the other

The surface is a cat with an unfortunate habit. Mechanically, MOG is the crossword's standing three-letter cat, worth filing alongside TOM and PUSS.

6 down LOOP

Accumulate boomerangs in circle (4)

Definition: circle · reversal

circle -> LOOP: a loop is a line that comes round to meet itself, which is a circle in the loose everyday sense rather than the geometer's.

  • Accumulate → POOL to pool money or effort is to gather it into a common store
  • boomerangs — a boomerang is thrown out and comes straight back, so the word it marks is read from its far end

'Boomerangs in circle' reads as a picture of Australia while doing two jobs at once: naming the turn, and in 'circle' giving the definition.

7 down MAIZE

Crop cloth covering snooker table, with money to replace balls, at first (5)

Definition: Crop · substitution

Crop -> MAIZE: maize is the cereal grown as sweetcorn and cattle feed, named by species where the definition names the category.

  • cloth covering snooker table → BAIZE baize is the green woollen cloth stretched over the slate
  • balls → B the opening letter of the word, and the one that has to go
  • money → M the opening letter of the word, moving in to take that place
  • to replace — replacing means one thing stands where another stood, so nothing is merely added and nothing is merely dropped
  • at first — reaches back over both nouns, taking the opening letter of each rather than the whole word

The surface is a snooker table being sold off, cloth and balls priced separately. The wordplay wants two initials and one swap between them.

8 down ARTISANAL

Negative critique of creative work regarding skilled worker (9)

Definition: regarding skilled worker · charade

regarding skilled worker -> ARTISANAL: the adjective formed from artisan, a craftsman working by hand, now stamped on bread and cheese to promise exactly that.

  • creative work → ART painting, sculpture and the rest are creative work
  • Negative critique → IS ANAL the sour verdict itself, spelled out as a flat statement that the thing is obsessively fussy

Split the answer three ways and it becomes a critic's dismissal of a whole gallery. The sneer and the craftsman's adjective are spelt with the same nine letters.

13 down EMANCIPATE

European chap worried about withdrawn photo's release (10)

Definition: release · charade + container + reversal

release -> EMANCIPATE: to emancipate is to set free from legal or social bondage, a formal, institutional kind of release rather than opening a door.

  • European → E E is the standard short form of European
  • chap → MAN a chap is a man, in the slightly dated register the surface wants
  • photo → CIP a photo is a pic, written down here from its back end forwards
  • worried → ATE what is eating you is what is worrying you, so worried gives ate
  • about — here it means round rather than concerning, so one piece closes on either side of the other
  • withdrawn — to withdraw is to draw back the way you came, so these letters are set down in reverse

The surface is a scandal about a picture being pulled, and every word of it is pulling its weight elsewhere: chap, worry and photo are three separate pieces.

14 down GREAT OUSE

Have to stop game in river (5,4)

Definition: river · container

river -> GREAT OUSE: the Great Ouse runs from Northamptonshire to the Wash, so the definition names it by category and leaves you the name.

  • game → GROUSE grouse is the game bird shot on moors from the twelfth of August
  • Have → EAT to have lunch is to eat it
  • to stop — to stop somewhere is to break your journey and stay there, so this word halts inside the other

Grouse is worth filing away: 'game' in a crossword is almost always either a bird or a three-letter sport, and both readings sit in this surface.

16 down LORD MAYOR

Royal dorm upset Dick Whittington? (4,5)

Definition: Dick Whittington? · anagram

Dick Whittington? -> LORD MAYOR: definition by example, and the question mark says so — the pantomime hero turned again and ended up holding the office in London.

  • Royal dorm → ROYALDORM the nine letters of these two words are the whole of the material
  • upset — upsetting something tips it over and spills what was in it, so the letters land in a different order

Dick Whittington is a pantomime as well as a real fifteenth-century merchant, and 'Royal dorm' is the sort of phrase that only ever exists to be rearranged.

18 down CONFIRM

Prove bit of negligence in two companies (7)

Definition: Prove · container + charade + first letter

Prove -> CONFIRM: the everyday sense of establishing that something is true, as a test confirms a diagnosis, rather than the mathematician's kind of proof.

  • two companies → CO CO is the abbreviation printed after a business name
  • negligence → N the opening letter of the word, and no more of it
  • two companies → FIRM the second of the pair is a firm, the plain word for a business
  • bit of — a bit is a fragment rather than the whole, and the smallest fragment of a word is its opening letter
  • in — not joinery here but a position: the letter goes between the two abbreviations rather than after them

'Two companies' asks for two different words meaning one company, not the same word twice, which is the small trick the plural is playing.

19 down GINSENG

Scrambled eggs in back of van can be restorative (7)

Definition: restorative · anagram + last letter

restorative -> GINSENG: the root is taken as a tonic in Chinese medicine, so 'restorative' is the herbalist's sense rather than a medical one.

  • eggs in → EGGSIN the six letters of these two words are most of the material
  • van → N the last letter of the word joins the rest of the fodder
  • Scrambled — scrambling eggs breaks them up and stirs them together, which is exactly what happens to these letters
  • back of — the back of a word is its final letter, so only that one is taken from it

A van with scrambled eggs in the back is a complete picture, and it is made entirely of letters — even the small word 'in' is fodder rather than joinery.

21 down WADER

Shorebird is nameless stray (5)

Definition: Shorebird · deletion

Shorebird -> WADER: waders are the long-legged birds — redshanks, avocets, curlews — that feed by wading in shallow water at the tide line.

  • stray → WANDER cattle that stray wander off the pasture
  • nameless → N N is the short form of name, and it is the letter dropped here
  • nameless — without a name, and name is N in the crossword's shorthand, so that is the letter which goes missing

'Nameless stray' is a sad little picture of a lost dog, and it is two instructions: a word meaning stray, with one letter of it taken out.

23 down RAT ON

Helping to release one in desert (3,2)

Definition: desert · deletion

desert -> RAT ON: to rat on your friends is to betray and abandon them, the informer's version of deserting a cause.

  • Helping → RATION a helping is a measured share, which is what a ration is
  • one → I one is I in Roman numerals, and it is the letter let go
  • to release — releasing something is letting it go, so the letter named is set loose from the word holding it

Prison food on the surface, and underneath it is what one inmate does to another. 'Desert' is the verb here, so read it as abandon rather than as sand.

24 down GRID

Lament almost lying about network (4)

Definition: network · deletion + reversal

network -> GRID: the sense in 'the National Grid' — an interconnected network of cables or pipes, rather than a pattern of ruled squares.

  • Lament → DIRG a dirge is a slow song of mourning, here docked of its last letter
  • almost — almost the whole word means all but the end of it, so the final letter never gets written
  • lying about — something left lying about has been tipped onto its side, and these letters are set down the other way round

'Lying about' is doing two jobs at once: it is the instruction to turn the letters, and it keeps the surface's picture of clutter on the floor.
