1 across CROSSWORD PUZZLE

Would rozzers (PCs) suspect this? (9,6)

Definition: Would rozzers (PCs) suspect this? · anagram + &lit

Read straight the clue asks whether police would suspect one, and in 1944 MI5 really did investigate a Telegraph setter whose grids kept throwing up D-Day codewords.

  • Would rozzers (PCs) → WOULDROZZERSPCS fifteen letters taken exactly as printed; 'rozzers' is British slang for the police and a PC is a police constable
  • suspect — Something suspect is not what it presents itself as, so the letters in front of the word cannot be trusted to stay in the order printed.

Detectives eyeing up an innocent newspaper pastime as though it were evidence of something.

Every word does both jobs at once: read straight it is a question about police suspicion, and those same fifteen letters spell the thing being asked about. The question mark is the setter owning up.

9 across DISROBE

Bride so excited to take off clothes (7)

Definition: take off clothes · anagram

To disrobe is to undress — the formal verb used of judges and ceremonies rather than of getting ready for bed.

  • Bride so → BRIDESO anagram fodder, seven letters
  • excited — An excited person will not keep still, and letters described as excited will not keep to the order they were handed over in.

An eager bride who cannot wait to get out of the dress.

The fodder is the first two words and nothing more: 'so excited' reads as one phrase, which is exactly what hides the seam between the letters and the instruction on them.

10 across SCOURGE

Search outside of Google for cause of affliction (7)

Definition: cause of affliction · charade + outer letters

A scourge was literally a whip, and the word now names anything that inflicts widespread suffering: the cause of the affliction rather than the affliction itself.

  • Search → SCOUR to scour a place is to search it thoroughly
  • Google → GE the two letters on the outside of the word, G and E
  • outside of — 'outside of' asks for the letters on the outer edges of the word named, its first and its last, leaving everything between them behind.

Googling to work out what has been making you ill.

'Search outside of Google' reads as one sensible instruction — look somewhere other than the search engine — and it is two pieces of wordplay with nothing to do with each other.

11 across INTEL

Acquired secrets via home telephone (5)

Definition: Acquired secrets · charade

'Acquired secrets' is service slang for gathered intelligence — the material an agency has collected, not the department that collects it.

  • home → IN somebody at home is in
  • telephone → TEL tel is the standard short form of telephone, as printed on a business card

Confidential material picked up over a landline at home.

'home telephone' is one object in the surface and two separate pieces in the wordplay. The definition is reaching for spy-fiction shorthand, not for the chip company the capital letter suggests.

12 across SNOWBOARD

Ride going downhill as Spooner's lover was loud in bed? (9)

Definition: Ride going downhill · spoonerism

'Ride going downhill' names the plank you stand sideways on to come down a snowy slope, defining the object by what you do with it.

  • lover → SNOW a lover is a beau; hand it the /sn/ from the other word and what is left is the white stuff on the slope
  • was loud in bed → BOARD someone loud in bed snored; it gives its /sn/ away and takes the /b/ in exchange
  • Spooner's — The Reverend Spooner was an Oxford don famous for swapping the opening sounds of two words, so his name is the standing signal to trade the two front noises.

A partner who used to make a terrible racket in bed, remembered on the piste.

Say 'beau snored' aloud and swap the noises at the front — the /b/ and the /sn/ change places, and the vowels drift a little as they land. Spoonerisms are heard, never spelled.

14 across MOVIE

Picture that is appended to second volume (5)

Definition: Picture · charade

'Picture' is the everyday British word for a film — as in going to the pictures — and the answer is the American word for the same thing.

  • second → MO a mo is a moment — 'hang on half a mo' — which is a second
  • volume → V v is the standard abbreviation for volume
  • that is → IE i.e. is short for the Latin id est, meaning that is
  • appended to — 'appended to' hangs the piece named before it onto the end of the pieces named after it, so the chunk mentioned first lands last.

A photograph stuck into the second volume of a set.

'second volume' is one thing off a bookshelf in the surface and two unrelated abbreviations in the wordplay, and the piece the clue names first is the one that ends up last.

16 across OFF-KILTER

Volunteer adopting traditional dress that's not quite right (3-6)

Definition: not quite right · container

Something out of kilter is tilted away from where it ought to sit — not broken, just not quite right.

  • Volunteer → OFFER to volunteer something is to offer it
  • traditional dress → KILT the kilt is the pleated knee-length skirt of Scottish national dress
  • adopting — 'adopting' is the container instruction: one word takes the other in and keeps it inside, the way a family takes in a child.

Somebody offering to help has taken to wearing national costume.

'Volunteer' looks like the willing person the surface paints and is a verb, which is where the outer letters come from. The hyphen in the answer is not signalled anywhere in the clue.

18 across SALTPETRE

A letter P's incorrectly signifying potassium compound (9)

Definition: potassium compound · anagram

'Potassium compound' is the chemistry: saltpetre is potassium nitrate, the oxidiser that makes gunpowder burn, so the definition is technical rather than everyday.

  • A letter P's → ALETTERPS anagram fodder, nine letters taken exactly as printed
  • incorrectly — 'incorrectly' says the letters have been set down in the wrong order, which is an invitation to find the right one.

A P typed by mistake turns out to stand for something to do with potassium.

The fodder hides in plain sight because 'A letter P's' reads as a description of a typo rather than as a string of letters waiting to be shuffled.

19 across GIRTH

Bit of maths going over height and circumference (5)

Definition: circumference · reversal + charade

Circumference is the distance round a solid thing — a tree trunk, a waist, a horse's belly — which is what girth measures.

  • Bit of maths → TRIG trig is short for trigonometry, one branch of school maths
  • height → H h is the standard symbol for height in a formula
  • going over — 'going over' turns the piece before it end for end, so those four letters are read back along the row before the last one is added.

A topic in maths that covers height as well as the distance round.

'Bit of maths' looks like an instruction to take a few letters out of a longer word, and it is a synonym instead: a whole school subject in four letters.

21 across CANADIANS

Maybe Toronto team are able to spot one answer (9)

Definition: Maybe Toronto team · charade

The definition works by example: a side from Toronto would be made up of people of that nationality, though hardly all of them play there.

  • are able to → CAN 'are able to' is can
  • spot → AD a spot is an advertising slot on television or radio
  • one → I the Roman numeral for one
  • answer → ANS ans is the standard short form of answer, as at the back of a textbook

A Toronto side manages to pick out a single reply.

'spot' is not seeing something here but an advertising slot, and 'answer' sits in the definition's usual place at the end of the clue while belonging to the wordplay.

23 across ROSTI

Starter of savoury flatbread wraps and Swiss-style fried potato (5)

Definition: Swiss-style fried potato · container + first letter

The Swiss dish is grated potato pressed into a flat cake and fried until crisp, so the definition is a plain description of the thing.

  • flatbread → ROTI a roti is the unleavened Indian flatbread
  • savoury → S the opening letter of the word
  • Starter of — 'Starter of' asks for the letter a word starts with, while the food sense of a starter keeps the instruction out of sight.
  • wraps — 'wraps' is the container instruction: the flatbread closes round the single letter the way a wrap closes round its filling.

The first mouthful of a savoury flatbread parcel.

Three foods in one sentence and only one of them is the definition. 'wraps' does the container's work while reading as part of the menu, which is why the clue seems to be about a sandwich throughout.

25 across EXISTED

Were split screens central part of episode? (7)

Definition: Were · container + middle letter

'Were' is past-tense 'be', and things that existed are simply things that were — the two verbs swap straight over in 'the dodo was' and 'the dodo existed'.

  • split → EXITED to split is to leave, and the past tense of that slang verb is exited
  • episode → S the middle letter of the seven in the word, with three either side
  • screens — To screen something is to hide it behind something else, so one word closes over the letter the other supplies.
  • central part of — 'central part of' asks for the letter at the exact middle of the word, not for any letter that happens to be inside it.

A question about whether split screens were the heart of a television episode.

'split screens' is a single piece of television jargon in the surface and two separate instructions underneath, one supplying letters and one telling you what to do with them.

27 across YOUTUBE

After collapse, buy out electronic tech company (7)

Definition: tech company · anagram

'Tech company' names it by class: the video-sharing site has been one of the big technology firms since Google bought it in 2006.

  • buy out → BUYOUT anagram fodder, six letters
  • electronic → E e is the standard abbreviation for electronic, as in e-mail
  • collapse — A collapse leaves the parts of a thing lying in a heap in no particular order, which is what happens to the letters standing next to it.

A firm bought up cheaply once the market has crashed.

'buy out electronic' reads as business news and is seven letters waiting to be shuffled, while 'tech company' sits quietly at the end of the clue being the definition.

28 across SHOTGUN WEDDINGS

Unions that are forced to raise issue legitimately? (7,8)

Definition: Unions that are forced to raise issue legitimately? · cryptic definition

A marriage hurried through because the bride is pregnant is forced in exactly that sense, and it is what makes the child born inside a marriage.

  • Unions that are forced to raise → the surface reading: trade unions under pressure to bring a matter up for debate
  • issue legitimately → the setter's reading: 'issue' is the old legal word for offspring, and children count as legitimate when their parents are married

Trade unions being leaned on to table a motion by the proper procedure.

'Unions' are marriages and 'issue' is children, both in their older legal senses, and the pressure in the surface turns into the shotgun of the phrase.

1 down CADMIUM

Silent about one going after rogue element (7)

Definition: element · charade + container

'Element' names cadmium by class rather than by synonym: it is one of the chemical elements, a soft bluish metal used in batteries and pigments.

  • rogue → CAD a cad is a rogue, the old word for a man who behaves badly
  • Silent → MUM to keep mum is to say nothing
  • one → I the Roman numeral for one
  • about — 'about' is used in its positional sense of round the outside, so the silent word closes over the single letter instead of merely referring to it.
  • going after — 'going after' sends the wrapped-up chunk behind the scoundrel, fixing which of the two pieces starts the word.

A tight-lipped type is thought to be trailing round after a scoundrel.

'Rogue element' reads as a single phrase and is two separate jobs: 'rogue' hands over the opening chunk while 'element' is the definition, stranded at the end of the clue.

2 down ONSET

Start to regain consciousness, despite anaesthesiologist's conclusions (5)

Definition: Start · last letters

The onset of a thing is the moment it begins, so as a noun it swaps straight in for 'start' — a shade more formal, used of illness and winter.

  • to regain consciousness, despite anaesthesiologist → ONSET the last letter of each of those five words, taken in the order they stand
  • conclusions — 'conclusions' asks for the point at which each word concludes, which is its final letter, and it applies to every word in the run.

A patient coming round on the table even though the anaesthetist expected otherwise.

The definition is the single word at the front, and 'to' belongs to the wordplay rather than to it — a five-word run is longer than most letter-picking clues risk.

3 down SHOULDER PAD

Jacket accessory ought to drape differently (8,3)

Definition: Jacket accessory · charade + anagram

A jacket's squared-off line comes from a stiffened insert sewn in under the lining at the shoulder, so the definition names the thing by the garment it belongs to.

  • ought to → SHOULD 'ought to' and 'should' are interchangeable ways of stating an obligation
  • drape → ERPAD the five letters of DRAPE in a new order
  • differently — 'differently' asks for the same letters set out another way, which is what turns the word beside it into raw material instead of a synonym.

Advice that a coat really ought to hang some other way.

'ought to drape differently' reads as one instruction about how a coat should hang, and that smoothness hides the join: the first two words are a synonym and only the third is fodder.

4 down OVERSHOOT

Deliveries by comedian go too far (9)

Definition: go too far · charade

To overshoot is to carry on past the mark you were aiming at — a pilot on a runway, a car at a junction — which is going too far.

  • Deliveries → OVERS in cricket an over is the set of six deliveries bowled from one end, so deliveries come in overs
  • comedian → HOOT someone who keeps you laughing is a hoot

Balls bowled by a stand-up comic sail miles past the target.

Cricket is the trap: 'deliveries' looks like parcels being handed over and is the bowler's word. A person who is a hoot is funny, so the second piece is a comedian rather than a noise.

5 down DISCO

Group of Chicago CSI detectives going over nightclub (5)

Definition: nightclub · hidden word + reversal

A disco is a club where recorded music is played for dancing, which has been the everyday word for a nightclub since the 1970s.

  • Chicago CSI detectives → OCSID five letters standing consecutively across the three words, from the tail of the city to the head of the last word
  • Group of — 'Group of' points at a run of letters standing together in the words that follow, while the surface offers it as a band or a squad.
  • going over — 'going over' tips that run end over end, so it has to be read the opposite way before it is written into the grid.

Investigators from an American crime lab turning up at a club.

'Group' wants to be a band and 'detectives' a job, and both are only strings to lift letters out of. Even then the run spells nothing until it is turned round.

6 down UNO

Card game using queen but not jack (3)

Definition: Card game · deletion

'Card game' names it by class — a proprietary game played not with a standard pack but with its own deck of coloured number cards.

  • queen → JUNO Juno was queen of the Roman gods and wife of Jupiter
  • jack → J J is the letter printed on the jack in a pack of cards
  • but not — 'but not' names something and then withholds it, so the letter it introduces is the one taken out of what came before.

A hand played out with the queen in it and the jack missing.

Two court cards in the surface and only one of them is a card in the wordplay: the queen is a Roman goddess, while the jack really does hand over the letter from the pack.

7 down ZOROASTER

Prophet from Australia upset critic? (9)

Definition: Prophet · charade + reversal

Zoroaster, also called Zarathustra, is the ancient Persian prophet whose teachings became Zoroastrianism, so 'Prophet' names him by his role.

  • Australia → OZ Oz is the standard nickname for Australia
  • critic → ROASTER to roast someone is to criticise them savagely, so a roaster is a critic
  • upset — 'upset' means tipped over, and a piece tipped over in a down entry is read back up the column instead of down it.

A seer has arrived from Australia having thoroughly annoyed a reviewer.

'upset critic' reads as one cross reviewer, and the two words are doing different jobs — one turns a country's nickname round, the other is a synonym standing on its own. The question mark marks how loose that synonym is.

8 down E-READER

Words displayed here preceding a German article (1-6)

Definition: Words displayed here · charade

An e-reader is the handheld screen whose only job is showing the words of a book, so 'here' points at the device the words appear on.

  • preceding → ERE 'ere' is the poetic word for before, so it means preceding; the letters are a whole word, not an abbreviation
  • a → A the word is taken straight into the answer as itself
  • German article → DER 'der' is the masculine form of the German definite article

Some text on show just ahead of a German word for 'the'.

'Words displayed here' points at the object rather than the writing, and 'preceding' is not joinery holding two halves together — it is a synonym doing letter work.

13 down BRING AROUND

Convince Google cofounder with revolutionary "silver bullet", perhaps (5,6)

Definition: Convince · charade + reversal

The phrasal verb means talking somebody out of their position and into yours, which is precisely what convincing them does.

  • Google cofounder → BRIN Sergey Brin founded Google with Larry Page
  • silver → AG Ag is the chemical symbol for silver, from the Latin argentum
  • bullet → ROUND a bullet is one round of ammunition
  • revolutionary — A revolutionary turns things round, and what gets turned here is the two-letter symbol beside it rather than any institution.
  • perhaps — 'perhaps' warns that the word after it is one instance of a wider category, so the piece wanted is the general term it belongs to.

Talking a tech billionaire into it with a radical and supposedly foolproof fix.

'Silver bullet' is the idiom for a single magic solution, which is why it sits inside quotation marks — and neither of its two words means anything like that in the wordplay.

15 down VALENTINO

Matinée idol from Depression not in broadcast (9)

Definition: Matinée idol · charade + anagram

'Matinée idol' names Rudolph Valentino by trade: the Italian-born star of silent romances whose 1926 funeral brought hysterical crowds onto the New York streets.

  • Depression → VALE a vale is a valley, a depression in the land; the capital letter is surface dressing
  • not in → NTINO the five letters of NOT IN in a new order
  • broadcast — To broadcast is first of all to scatter seed widely, and letters scattered that way land in no particular order.

A screen heart-throb of the slump years who was kept off the airwaves.

'Depression' wears a capital letter and has nothing to do with the 1930s: it is a dip in the ground. 'not in broadcast' then reads as an absence from the airwaves while quietly being fodder plus its instruction.

17 down FREESTYLE

Swimming event runs from Lebanese port after left/right mix-up (9)

Definition: Swimming event · charade + substitution

'Swimming event' points at a discipline rather than offering a synonym: freestyle is the race in which any stroke is allowed and everybody chooses front crawl.

  • runs from → FREES to run from something is to flee it, and FLEES hands its L over in exchange for an R
  • Lebanese port → TYLE Tyre is the ancient port on the Lebanese coast, and it takes in the L that its R has just paid for
  • left/right mix-up — Left is L and right is R, and mixing them up makes the two pieces swap those letters, each taking the one the other gave away.

A swimming race setting off from a Lebanese harbour after left and right get confused.

Both halves are altered rather than one: a single L and a single R change places across the two words. Tyre is one of the world's oldest ports, on the coast south of Beirut.

18 down SUCCESS

Win this kind of board game after losing hard twice (7)

Definition: Win · charade + deletion

'Win' works as a noun — the outcome achieved rather than the act of achieving it — and the answer is its more formal equivalent.

  • hard → H H is hard, the grade stamped on a pencil, and it is the letter that leaves
  • this kind of → SUC SUCH means this kind of, and it goes without its H
  • board game → CESS CHESS is the board game, and it goes without its H as well
  • losing — 'losing' is the deletion instruction: whatever it names drops out of the piece it was sitting in.
  • twice — 'twice' says the same letter comes out on two separate occasions, so both pieces give one up rather than one piece giving up two.

Beating somebody at this sort of board game after two hard defeats.

The same letter comes out of both words, which is the job 'twice' is doing at the end. 'Win' is a noun here, not the verb the surface keeps offering.

20 down HEIRESS

That man angers leader of suffragists—she has it coming (7)

Definition: she has it coming · charade + first letter

The 'it' on its way to her is money left in a will: an heiress is a woman who inherits, or stands to inherit, a fortune.

  • That man → HE 'that man' is he
  • angers → IRES to ire somebody is to anger them, and the verb keeps its third-person s
  • suffragists → S the opening letter of the word
  • leader of — 'leader of' takes only the letter that leads the word after it, the one standing at its head.

A man's behaviour infuriates the head of the suffragists, who thoroughly deserves what is coming.

'she has it coming' sounds like a threat and means money is due. The suffragists campaigned peacefully for votes for women, as opposed to the suffragettes and their direct action.

22 down ADD-ON

Returned to sign agreement and District Attorney's supplement (3-2)

Definition: supplement · charade + reversal

A supplement here is an extra part bolted onto something already complete — a feature added to software, an accessory sold separately.

  • to sign agreement → NOD you nod to signal that you agree
  • District Attorney → DA DA is the district attorney, the prosecutor in an American court
  • Returned — 'Returned' sends the finished assembly back the way it came, so both pieces are read from their last letter to their first.

Somebody came back to sign a deal along with the district attorney.

'sign agreement' is not a document but a gesture, the one you make with your head. The two pieces are built in clue order and only then is the whole thing turned round.

24 down SPURN

Reject fiancée, essentially following incentive (5)

Definition: Reject · charade + middle letter

To spurn something is to reject it with contempt — the disdainful end of the verb, used of offers, lovers and advice.

  • incentive → SPUR a spur is an incentive, the thing that drives someone on
  • fiancée → N the middle letter of the seven in the word, with three either side
  • essentially — 'essentially' asks for the essence of the word, the letter sitting at its exact centre rather than any letter inside it.
  • following — 'following' puts the letter it has just produced behind the piece named next, so the incentive comes first.

Dropping a partner because a reward is on offer elsewhere.

'Reject fiancée' reads as one action and splits: the first word is the definition and the second is a word to take a letter out of, so the engagement belongs entirely to the surface.

26 down TUG

Hamburger's good, on reflection, for Yank (3)

Definition: Yank · reversal

'Yank' is the noun meaning a short sharp pull, so it matches the same sudden action a tug describes.

  • Hamburger's good → GUT a Hamburger is a native of Hamburg, and gut is German for good
  • on reflection — 'on reflection' is a mirror as much as it is second thoughts, so the three letters come back the other way round.

A man from Hamburg gives something his approval after a moment's thought.

Two nationalities in three letters and neither one counts: a Hamburger is a citizen of Hamburg rather than a burger, and 'Yank' is a pull rather than an American.
