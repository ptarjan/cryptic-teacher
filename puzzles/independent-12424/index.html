Across

1 across PLUM JOB

Attractive employment opportunity in testicular enhancement surgery? (4,3)

Definition: Attractive employment opportunity · charade

a plum job is the pick of the posts going: 'plum' has meant the choicest item since plums were the best bit of the pudding.

  • testicular → PLUM plums is the schoolboy slang for testicles
  • enhancement surgery → JOB a nose job or a boob job is cosmetic surgery, so the surgery is a job

'Job' for cosmetic work is the piece worth banking — nose job, boob job — and the question mark is the setter admitting this particular operation is his own invention.

5 across TEA LEAF

Burglar in Shoreditch that’s likely to end up in hot water (3,4)

Definition: Burglar in Shoreditch · double definition

tea leaf is the Cockney rhyme for thief, and the same two words also name the literal leaf that gets brewed, so the phrase carries both senses.

  • Burglar in Shoreditch → TEALEAF Cockney rhyming slang, spoken in Shoreditch, supplies a two-word term for a burglar built to rhyme with thief
  • likely to end up in hot water → TEALEAF the flat literal reading — what goes into the pot once the kettle has boiled

'In hot water' works both halves at once: trouble for the burglar, and the teapot for the leaf.

9 across MOPES

Roaming Epsom Downs (5)

Definition: Downs · anagram

'the mopes' is an old plural noun for a fit of low spirits, so it answers 'downs' in the ups-and-downs sense rather than the hills.

  • Epsom → EPSOM five letters taken as letters, not as a town
  • Roaming — something roaming keeps to no fixed route, so its letters are free to wander into another order

Epsom Downs is the racecourse, which is the entire trick: two words that name one place are really fodder and definition standing next to each other.

10 across DALMATIAN

Matilda reworked by an Adriatic resident (9)

Definition: Adriatic resident · anagram

Dalmatia is the Croatian coast of the Adriatic, so a Dalmatian is a person who lives there; the spotted dog is the same word's other sense.

  • Matilda → MATILDA the italics mark a title, and titles are fair fodder
  • an → AN two more letters joining the mix, doing no grammatical work at all
  • reworked — a thing reworked has been taken apart and made again in another shape, which is what happens to these letters

'An' looks like the article introducing the definition, which is why it is easy to leave out of the count — and the anagram is two letters short without it.

11 across LITMUS TEST

Touchstone's requirement to be inspired by working titles (6,4)

Definition: Touchstone · anagram + container

a touchstone was the dark stone a jeweller rubbed gold against to assay it, so both words name a single trial that settles a question outright.

  • titles → LITEST the six letters of 'titles' kneaded into a new order, which then parts after three
  • requirement → MUST a requirement is a must
  • working — worked in the sense dough is worked, kneaded into a different shape rather than employed
  • to be inspired by — to inspire is literally to breathe in, so whatever is inspired ends up inside the other piece

Touchstone is also the clown in As You Like It, so the apostrophe-s reads as his possession when it is really standing in for 'is'.

12 across SODA

What could follow on from club's spinning difficulties (4)

Definition: What could follow on from club · reversal

club soda is the bar-trade name for fizzy water, so the definition points at the phrase the answer completes rather than at any meaning of its own.

  • difficulties → ADOS an ado is a fuss or a difficulty, and the plural gives four letters to turn
  • spinning — a thing spinning comes round to face the other way, so the letters are read in the opposite direction

The surface is pure cricket — 'follow on' and 'spinning' both belong to the game — while the answer is behind the bar.

14 across TAYLOR SWIFT

A flirty swot developed into international chart topper (6,5)

Definition: international chart topper · anagram

her albums go to number one in country after country, so the definition is by example: one named artist standing in for the description.

  • A flirty swot → AFLIRTYSWOT eleven letters taken at face value, the whole phrase and nothing else
  • developed — grown into something else, so the fodder does not keep the shape it arrives in

A living person's name is fair game as an answer, and the surface — a schoolgirl who turned out well — is doing all the work of hiding a phrase that is already in plain sight.

18 across LAMMERGEYER

Perhaps chicken crosses major road to join vulture (11)

Definition: vulture · container + charade

the lammergeyer is the bearded vulture of Alpine and Himalayan crags, so 'vulture' names the family this particular bird belongs to.

  • Perhaps chicken → LAYER a hen kept for eggs is a layer, and 'perhaps' owns up that not every chicken is one
  • major road → M M is what a motorway is numbered with — the M1, the M25
  • join → MERGE traffic joining a road merges with it
  • crosses — what crosses something lies over it from side to side, so this word ends up on both sides of what follows

The oldest joke in the book is doing the work here: the chicken really does cross the road, and it is the wordplay rather than the punchline.

21 across CHEW

Champ in essentially atrocious shape (4)

Definition: Champ · charade + middle letter

to champ is to chew noisily — the horse's word, as in champing at the bit — which is why the plain synonym feels old-fashioned.

  • atrocious → C the fifth of its nine letters, with four on either side
  • shape → HEW to hew is to shape stone or timber by cutting it
  • essentially — the essence of a word is at its very centre, so only the letter in the middle is wanted

'In' reads as a preposition and is only joinery, which leaves 'essentially' looking like an adverb of emphasis rather than the instruction it is.

22 across ASTRINGENT

Die-cast ring entrepreneur imports acid (10)

Definition: acid · hidden word

an astringent taste is sharp and puckering, which is 'acid' used of flavour rather than of chemistry.

  • Die-cast ring entrepreneur → ASTRINGENT the letters run unbroken from the middle of the first word: die-cASTRINGENTrepreneur
  • imports — to import is to carry something in from outside, so the words are carrying the answer inside themselves

Ten letters is a long thing to hide, and the hyphen helps by breaking your eye exactly one letter before the answer starts.

25 across BRICK WALL

Barrier erected as a result of Spooner's fight in Northern Scotland (5,4)

Definition: Barrier · spoonerism

a brick wall is the immovable barrier of the idiom, the thing you hit when progress stops dead.

  • Northern Scotland → BRICK Wick is the town on the far north coast of Scotland, and it takes in the BR the other word gives up
  • fight → WALL a brawl is a fight, and it hands its BR over in exchange for the W
  • Spooner's — the Reverend Spooner swapped the opening sounds of two words, so naming him is the order to trade them over

Spooner clues stand or fall on the place name, and this one is a real town rather than a region — the north of Scotland has only so many.

26 across IRONY

Heard why seaweed from the East is rich in essential element (5)

Definition: rich in essential element · homophone + charade + reversal

irony is also the adjective from iron, meaning full of it, and iron is an element the body must have, so the sarcasm sense never comes into it.

  • why → Y say the letter Y aloud and you have said 'why'
  • seaweed → NORI nori is the seaweed sushi is wrapped in, which is why 'from the East' reads as its origin
  • Heard — asks for the word as your ear takes it rather than as the page spells it
  • from the East — what comes from the east travels westward, so in an across entry the letters run right to left

Everything in the clue is pointing east — the seaweed, the direction — and the direction is the only word doing cryptic work.

27 across SALAMIS

Setter is regrettably backing square sausages (7)

Definition: sausages · charade + reversal

salami is the cured Italian sausage, and the plural covers several of them or several varieties, which is what 'sausages' asks for.

  • Setter is → IM the setter is the person who wrote the clue, so 'setter is' is I'm
  • regrettably → ALAS alas is the word you say when reporting something regrettably
  • square → S S abbreviates square, as in a street address
  • backing — going the other way, so everything named before it is written from right to left

Setter means the person writing, not the dog and not the sun — one of the first conventions worth owning, since it turns up in every puzzle.

28 across REGIMEN

Government trimmed part of the army (7)

Definition: Government · deletion

regimen carries the old sense of rule or government — the same idea as the regimen a doctor governs your diet with.

  • part of the army → REGIMENT a regiment is the army unit, and its last letter is what gets trimmed
  • trimmed — trimming takes a little off the end, so the word beside it arrives one letter short

Regime and regimen are close cousins from the same Latin root, so the definition sounds like a typo until you remember a regimen is a rule you live by.

1 down PIMPLE

Afternoon retreats in mountain spot (6)

Definition: spot · reversal + container

a pimple is a spot in the skin-blemish sense, the word a teenager uses, rather than a place or a dot.

  • mountain → PILE a mountain of work is a pile of it, and this pile opens after two letters
  • Afternoon → PM afternoon is PM on a clock, and it goes in backwards
  • retreats — an army that retreats goes back the way it came, so those two letters are written in the opposite order
  • in — the instruction to bury one piece inside the word that follows rather than set it alongside

A mountain of work is a pile of it — that everyday sense is the step to find, because the clue's mountain is doing its best to look like a real one.

2 down UPPITY

Elevated feeling of empathy is difficult to control (6)

Definition: difficult to control · charade

uppity means getting above yourself and refusing to be told, which is exactly the person a boss calls difficult to control.

  • Elevated → UP elevated means raised, and raised is up
  • feeling of empathy → PITY pity is the fellow feeling you have for someone worse off

The surface reads as one description of a person, so the seam between the two halves is invisible until you notice that 'elevated' is doing a word's work and not a mood's.

3 down JOSHUA TREE

Liberal judge hates Euro-American national park (6,4)

Definition: American national park · anagram

Joshua Tree National Park is a real park in the California desert, so the definition names the class the proper noun belongs to.

  • judge → J J is how law reports abbreviate a judge
  • hates Euro → HATESEURO nine letters taken at face value, thrown in with the J
  • Liberal — a liberal translation is a loose one, bound by no letter of the original, so nothing here has to stay in the order it is given

The hyphen is the trap. 'Euro' is fodder and 'American' has already crossed over to the definition, where it settles which country's parks are on offer.

4 down BIDET

Item to assist washing behind ears trialled initially under offer (5)

Definition: Item to assist washing behind · charade + first letters

a bidet is the low bathroom basin you sit astride to wash your bottom, so the definition names it by the job it does.

  • offer → BID an offer at auction is a bid
  • ears trialled → ET the letters those two words start with
  • initially — at the outset — so only the letter each word starts with is taken
  • under — places one piece below the other, and in a down entry below means later along the answer

Everything turns on where 'behind' belongs: the surface has you scrubbing behind your ears, and the definition means the other behind entirely.

5 down TALL STORY

Brief violent disturbance in match report? That is probably exaggerated (4,5)

Definition: report? That is probably exaggerated · container + deletion

a tall story is an account stretched well past the truth, so the definition is the whole idiom rather than a synonym for either word in it.

  • match → TALLY figures that tally are figures that match, and this one splits before its last letter
  • violent disturbance → STOR a storm is a violent disturbance, docked of its final M
  • Brief — brief means cut short, so the word it governs arrives a letter shorter than it should be
  • in — says one piece is planted inside the other rather than laid beside it

'Match' looks like the game and is really the verb, the one figures do when they agree — which is the step the scuffle-at-a-match surface exists to hide.

6 down AFAR

Discovered wildlife trip a long way away (4)

Definition: a long way away · deletion

afar survives mainly in the phrase 'from afar', where it means at a great distance, which is what 'a long way away' says plainly.

  • wildlife trip → SAFARI a safari is a trip to see wildlife; lose the S at the front and the I at the back
  • Discovered — read it as dis-covered, stripped of its covering, so the letters wrapped round the outside come off

'Discovered' is a lovely piece of misdirection — it looks like a past tense reporting a find, and it is an instruction to take the wrapping off.

7 down EPISODIC

Prepared cod pies, eating one from time to time (8)

Definition: from time to time · anagram

something episodic happens in separate bursts with gaps in between, which is occasional rather than continuous — the pattern 'from time to time' describes.

  • cod pies → CODPIES seven letters taken at face value, not as words
  • one → I one is the Roman numeral I, the commonest single letter in the language of clues
  • Prepared — food that has been prepared has been chopped and mixed, so nothing stays in the state it arrived in
  • eating — what is eaten goes inside, so that letter is swallowed into the mixture

The surface is a cook at work, which is exactly what an anagram indicator wants to look like — 'prepared' sounds like an account of dinner rather than an order.

8 down FINE ARTS

Trump's trousers in English painting? (4,4)

Definition: painting? · container + charade

the fine arts are those practised for beauty rather than use — painting, sculpture, music — so the clue offers one member of that group.

  • Trump's → FARTS to trump is to break wind, so this reads as the verb trumps
  • in → IN taken at face value, two letters of the filling
  • English → E E is the standard abbreviation for English
  • trousers — to trouser something is to pocket it, to take it inside, so the word before swallows what comes after

The politician is a decoy: nothing about this Trump is a surname, and once the word turns into a verb the trousers stop being clothing and become a hiding place.

13 down ESTRANGING

Separating from Esther? The woman's away and phoned up during golf! (10)

Definition: Separating · charade + deletion

to estrange is to drive people apart until they stop speaking, so the -ing form matches 'separating' in the sense of a relationship coming apart.

  • Esther → EST drop the HER from ESTHER, minus the woman, and EST is what stays
  • phoned up → RANG to ring someone up is to phone them
  • during → IN during and in are the same preposition of time
  • golf → G golf is the radio alphabet's word for G, alongside alpha and bravo
  • The woman's away — the woman is HER, and 'away' is the order to take those three letters out of the name in front

The exclamation mark sells the surface as gossip on a fairway, which is why nobody looks at 'golf' and sees a single letter of the radio alphabet.

15 down YUGOSLAVS

It's your turn, we're told, to toil endlessly ahead of South Balkans race (9)

Definition: Balkans race · homophone + charade + deletion

the Yugoslavs were the peoples of the federation that occupied much of the Balkans, so 'race' is doing its loose sense of a nation's people.

  • It's your turn → YUGO if it is your turn then you go, and 'you go' said aloud is written this way
  • toil → SLAV to slave is to toil, and the final E never gets said
  • South → S S is the compass abbreviation for south
  • we're told — reported speech: take the words as they sound rather than as they are spelt
  • endlessly — without its end, so the last letter of the word never arrives
  • ahead of — puts the piece in front of what follows rather than behind it

A whole country is buried in the pun: the first four letters are nothing but two everyday words heard rather than read, which is why they look like a foreign spelling.

16 down PLACEBOS

Site manager detailed ineffective medication (8)

Definition: ineffective medication · charade + deletion

a placebo is the dummy pill given to a trial's control group, so 'ineffective medication' names it by the very thing it deliberately lacks.

  • Site → PLACE the site of something is the place it happens
  • manager → BOS a manager is a BOSS, and the tail comes off
  • detailed — read as de-tailed, robbed of its tail, so the word in front loses its last letter

'Detailed' is the whole clue in one word: it reads as an adjective describing thorough paperwork and works as a verb meaning docked.

17 down IMPERIAL

Academician put in danger rings London college (8)

Definition: London college · container

Imperial College is one of London University's science institutions, so the definition names the place by category and leaves you to supply the name.

  • put in danger → IMPERIL to imperil someone is to put them in danger, and the word opens before its last letter
  • Academician → A A is the abbreviation for an Academician
  • rings — to ring something is to draw a circle round it, so one piece closes about the other

'Rings' looks like a verb about telephones sitting between two nouns, which is what keeps the sentence upright while it quietly gives an instruction.

19 down REFORM

Party hire for merchant banks (6)

Definition: Party · hidden word

Reform is the name of a British political party, so 'Party' identifies the word as a proper noun rather than defining what it means.

  • hire for merchant → REFORM the letters run unbroken across hiRE FOR Merchant, straddling three words
  • banks — to bank something is to stow it away for safe keeping, so the letters are being kept inside the words around them

'Merchant banks' is the least suspicious pair of words in the clue, which is exactly why the answer is lying across them.

20 down STAY ON

Ban on sailing vessels turning up and dropping off children is to remain after all (4,2)

Definition: remain after all · charade + deletion + reversal

to stay on is to remain when everyone else has left, as a teacher stays on past retirement, so 'after all' means after all the rest have gone.

  • Ban → NO a ban is a flat no
  • children → CH ch is the abbreviation for children, and this is the pair that falls away
  • sailing vessels → YATS sailing vessels are YACHTS, and without that CH you are left with YATS
  • turning up — a down entry read from the bottom comes up the grid, so the letters are written in reverse
  • dropping off — something dropping off falls away and is gone, which is what happens to the pair named next to it

The surface is the school run — dropping children off — while every one of those words is really an instruction. Only 'up' tells you the assembly is read backwards.

23 down RULER

Where lurchers rounded, guarding the King? (5)

Definition: the King? · hidden word + reversal

a king is one kind of ruler, so the question mark marks a definition by example — the class named by one of its members.

  • Where lurchers → RELUR the letters sit unbroken across wheRE LURchers, waiting to be read the other way
  • rounded — turned about, so the buried letters are read from the far end back to the near one
  • guarding — to guard something is to keep it safe within your own walls, so these words are holding a word inside them

A lurcher is a poacher's crossbred dog, which is why 'Where lurchers' reads as a place to look for one rather than as a hiding place.

24 down SKIM

Read superficially, Jagger's missing constant "rolling" (4)

Definition: Read superficially · deletion + anagram

to skim a page is to take in only its surface, catching the gist and none of the detail.

  • Jagger's → MICKS Jagger is Mick, so his name plus the S of 'Jagger's' gives five letters
  • constant → C c is the physicist's constant, the speed of light, and it is the letter that goes
  • missing — what is missing has gone, so the letter named beside it is not in the answer
  • rolling — letters set rolling come to rest in a different order, and Jagger's own band name is what makes the word look innocent here

Quotation marks around a word are usually the setter pointing at it, and here they point at a Rolling Stone: 'rolling' is the instruction and the joke at once.
