1 across GAMBLE

Risky venture drinking barrel – up for it? (6)

Definition: Risky venture · container

A gamble is a risky venture: money, time or reputation staked on an outcome nobody can be sure of.

  • up for it? → GAME Somebody up for anything is game, and the word is opened up to take a swallow.
  • barrel → BL bl is the trade short form for a barrel, as written on a shipping manifest.
  • drinking — Drinking takes a thing down inside you, so the letters named are swallowed by the word around them.

A daredevil knocking back a whole barrel and asking who else fancies a go.

'Up for it' is the willing sense of game rather than the animal or the sport, and 'risky venture' is a noun, so the answer has to be one too.

4 across DAUGHTER

Don't start chuckling after passing of dead relative (8)

Definition: relative · charade + deletion

'Relative' is the general word for a member of a family, and a daughter is one of them — the definition names the category, the answer an instance.

  • passing of dead → D d is the standard short form for dead or died, and 'passing' is the euphemism carrying it in.
  • chuckling → AUGHTER Chuckling is laughter, handed over minus the letter it begins with.
  • Don't start — An instruction not to start means the word hands itself over without the letter it opens on.
  • after — After places the docked word behind the letter already laid down, rather than in front of it.

Someone who cannot keep a straight face at a family funeral.

The surface is a bereavement and the definition is the one cold word in it: 'relative' does plain family duty while everything around it mourns.

10 across DENIM

Material from pit steed finally brought back (5)

Definition: Material · charade + last letter + reversal

Denim is the heavy cotton twill jeans are cut from, so 'material' names it by its class — the cloth, not the garment.

  • pit → MINE A pit is a mine, in the coal sense.
  • steed → D The letter this word ends on.
  • finally — Finally asks for the letter a word ends on and no other.
  • brought back — An across answer brought back is read right to left, so the assembled letters are written in reverse.

A horse being led back up out of a colliery.

Pit ponies really did work underground, which is what holds the surface together while its two pieces are quietly being turned round.

11 across STRANGERS

Murderers doing away with fifty foreigners? (9)

Definition: foreigners · deletion

A stranger is someone from elsewhere, the old sense kept alive in 'a stranger in a strange land', which is why the question mark softens 'foreigners'.

  • Murderers → STRANGLERS People who kill by throttling.
  • fifty → L L is fifty in Roman numerals, and it is the letter to lose.
  • doing away with — Doing away with something is killing it off, so the letter named is removed from the word before it.

Killers picking off fifty people from abroad.

'Doing away with' is what murderers do in the surface and what the wordplay does to one letter, so the same three words carry the joke and the instruction.

12 across OPERETTA

Amusingly trivial work is awful to repeat (8)

Definition: Amusingly trivial work · anagram

An operetta is a short light opera with jokes and spoken dialogue, so being an amusingly trivial work is its whole job.

  • to repeat → TOREPEAT Eight letters of fodder, the two words taken exactly as printed.
  • awful — Something awful is a mess, and a mess of letters is not left in the order it was handed over in.

A dreadful bit of music nobody could face hearing a second time.

The definition runs three words and the fodder only two, so the join lands mid-sentence and 'work is awful to repeat' reads as a complaint instead of an instruction.

13 across ALWAYS

Gangster is ahead of customs every time (6)

Definition: every time · charade

Told that somebody wins every time, you could swap the answer straight in: both mean on each occasion without exception.

  • Gangster → AL Al Capone is the crossword's default gangster, and his first name is the piece wanted.
  • customs → WAYS The ways of a people are their customs.
  • ahead of — Ahead of puts the piece just clued in front of the one named next.

A mobster arriving at the border before the customs officers do.

'Customs' is the airport desk in the surface and settled habits in the wordplay, and 'every time' is an adverb, so the answer has to be one as well.

14 across SNOG

After losing heart, boy shot back for a kiss (4)

Definition: a kiss · charade + deletion + reversal

A snog is a long kiss, British and informal, and the definition keeps its article so that it stays a noun like the answer.

  • boy → SN A boy is a son, with the letter at its centre taken out.
  • shot → OG A shot at something is a go, turned round here.
  • losing heart — A word that loses its heart gives up the letter sitting at its exact centre.
  • back — An across answer read back runs right to left, so this piece is written the other way round.

A nervous lad having one more try at getting a kiss.

'Losing heart' is losing your nerve in the surface, which is the whole story the sentence tells and the instruction at the same time.

15 across CROCODILES

Freshly-made cider cools some basking in the sun? (10)

Definition: some basking in the sun? · anagram

Crocodiles haul out on to riverbanks to warm themselves, so the definition points at them by their most familiar habit rather than by species.

  • cider cools → CIDERCOOLS Ten letters of fodder, the two words exactly as printed.
  • Freshly-made — Something freshly made has just been put together anew, so its parts are not in the order they arrived in.

A new batch of cider chilling while everybody stretches out in the heat.

'Cools' looks like the verb the sentence needs and is really the back half of the fodder, which is why the definition seems to start several words too late.

18 across FLAT-PACKED

Gang in uniform head regularly boxed before assembly? (4-6)

Definition: boxed before assembly? · charade + container + alternate letters

Flat-packed furniture is sold boxed flat for the buyer to build, so the definition describes the state the wardrobe is in before the Allen key comes out.

  • uniform → FLAT A flat rate is a uniform one, the same all the way through.
  • Gang → PACK A pack is a gang, whether of wolves or of people.
  • head → ED The second and fourth letters of the word, taken in that order.
  • in — In puts the piece just clued inside the letters that surround it rather than alongside them.
  • regularly — Letters taken regularly come at an even step through the word, so every second one is picked and the rest dropped.

Uniformed heavies being loaded into a van ahead of a rally.

'Gang in uniform' reads as one idea — a mob dressed alike — and the wordplay splits it, with the little word 'in' carrying the only instruction.

20 across LOST

Group imbibing drop of stout will get wasted (4)

Definition: wasted · container + first letter

Time or an opportunity that has been wasted is lost — gone for good, rather than merely mislaid.

  • Group → LOT A lot is a group of people or things, opened up here to take a letter.
  • stout → S The first letter of the word, the smallest drop of it there is.
  • imbibing — Imbibing is drinking down, so the letter named next is taken inside this word.
  • drop of — A drop of something is the smallest part of it, which in letters is the one it begins with.

A crowd sinking a drop of the black stuff and getting thoroughly hammered.

'Wasted' is drunk in the surface and squandered in the definition, so the last two words are pulling in opposite directions at once.

23 across IN TURN

Where one might find desire to perform back to back (2,4)

Definition: Where one might find desire to perform back to back · cryptic definition

The answer means each after the other in sequence, which is what 'back to back' asks for — it names the order people take their go in.

  • Where one might find desire to perform → The surface reading: a question about where an urge to get on stage comes from, with 'perform' tugging loosely at the sense of a turn as a stage act.
  • back to back → The reading the setter means: each party taking a go, one behind the other, in rotation.

A question about where the itch to get up on stage comes from.

Performers take their go one behind the other, which is all the definition needs. The wink is that desire is a rut, and a rut read backwards hides inside the answer's second word.

25 across DERRIERE

Ruddy turned on about pinching one's buttocks (8)

Definition: buttocks · reversal + charade + container

Derriere is French for behind, borrowed into polite English as the word you use when you do not want to say backside.

  • Ruddy → DER Ruddy means red, and the colour is written back to front here.
  • on → RE On a subject is re it, as in the heading of a memo.
  • one's → I One is the numeral I.
  • about → RE About something is re it as well, so the same two letters are wanted twice.
  • turned — Turned about, an across piece is read from its far end back to the letter it started on.
  • pinching — Pinching something is gripping it tight, so the letter named is held inside this piece.

A red-faced somebody getting worked up and grabbing at a backside.

'Ruddy' is a mild British swear word in the surface — a ruddy nuisance — and the plain colour red in the wordplay.

26 across NUMBER ONE

Drink after drug like opium is the best (6,3)

Definition: the best · charade

The number one in a chart or a field is the best of them, so the answer names top rank rather than a count.

  • drug like opium → NUMBER Opium deadens feeling, so a drug like it is a thing that numbs — read as the comparative of numb.
  • Drink → ONE A drink is a one, as in stopping off for a quick one.
  • after — After places the piece just clued behind the one named next, so the drink comes second.

A nightcap taken on top of something a good deal stronger.

The six letters have to be read as numb-er, the comparative of numb, rather than as a figure; opium is the clue's nudge towards deadening.

28 across NINJA

Mercenary judge held in ship going to America? (5)

Definition: Mercenary · container

A ninja was a covert agent hired for stealth work in feudal Japan, so 'mercenary' names the answer by trade: somebody paid to fight.

  • ship going to America? → NINA The Nina was one of the three ships Columbus sailed west with, and the question mark flags one particular vessel.
  • judge → J J is the standard short form for judge in a law report.
  • held in — Held in means kept inside, so this letter is enclosed by the word around it.

A judge locked up below decks on a boat bound for the States.

'Mercenary' is a noun here, not the adjective meaning grasping, which is the reading the surface's judge invites you to take.

29 across SHOOTING

Filming Ghost in Barking, Whoopie's heart stops! (8)

Definition: Filming · anagram + middle letter

Shooting is the film industry's own verb for recording a scene, so it swaps straight in for 'filming'.

  • Ghost in → GHOSTIN Seven letters of fodder, the film's title and the little word after it taken as printed.
  • Whoopie's → O The letter at the exact centre of the seven-letter name.
  • Barking — Barking is British slang for raving mad, and letters called that will not stay in any sensible order.
  • heart stops — The heart of a word is the letter at its dead centre, and stopping it lifts that letter out.

A film crew making Ghost out in Barking, and Whoopi Goldberg's heart giving out.

Whoopi Goldberg was in Ghost and Barking is a district of east London, so every proper noun pulls towards a location shoot while all three are only doing letter work.

30 across BEDSIT

Live debuts from Dre and Snoop one tenor digs? (6)

Definition: digs · charade + first letters

Digs is British slang for rented lodgings, and a bedsit is the cheapest kind: one room that is bedroom and sitting room at once.

  • Live → BE To live is to be.
  • Dre and Snoop → DS The opening letters of the two rappers' names, in the order printed.
  • one → I One is the numeral I.
  • tenor → T T is the standard short form for tenor on a score.
  • debuts from — A debut is a first appearance, so each name that follows gives up the letter it opens on.

Two rappers opening a live show that a singer in the crowd is enjoying.

'Live' is the concert adjective in the surface and the verb to be in the wordplay, and 'digs' is British for rented lodgings rather than anything a spade does.

3 down LIMPET

Clingy type is flaccid and extremely expectant (6)

Definition: Clingy type · charade + outer letters

A limpet grips its rock so hard it cannot be prised off, and the name transfers to a person who will not let go of you.

  • flaccid → LIMP Anything flaccid hangs limp.
  • expectant → ET The far ends of the word: E at the front, T at the back.
  • extremely — Extremely points at the two extremes of a word, its outermost letters, and takes nothing from between them.

Somebody drooping and on tenterhooks at the same time.

'Extremely expectant' reads as an intensifier — very expectant indeed — which is exactly what hides the instruction to take a word's two ends.

5 down AIR

Arabian with touch of irritating trapped wind (3)

Definition: wind · container + first letter

Sailors call a light breeze an air, so the two words meet at moving atmosphere rather than at the stuff we breathe.

  • Arabian → AR Ar is the dictionary short form for Arabian or Arabic, prised apart here to take an insertion.
  • irritating → I The first letter of the word, which is all a touch of it comes to.
  • touch of — A touch of something is the merest bit of it, which in letters is the one it starts on.
  • trapped — Something trapped cannot get out of what surrounds it, so this letter goes inside the other piece.

An Arabian horse, a niggling complaint and a bout of trapped wind.

'Trapped wind' is one idea in the surface — indigestion — and the clue splits it down the middle, keeping 'wind' for the definition and 'trapped' as the instruction.

6 down GENTLED

Bloke on line my boss stroked soothingly (7)

Definition: stroked soothingly · charade

To gentle a horse is to calm it by handling, which is precisely what stroking it soothingly amounts to.

  • Bloke → GENT A gent is a bloke, the clipped form of gentleman.
  • line → L L is the standard short form for line.
  • my boss → ED The setter's boss at the paper is the editor, ed for short.
  • on — In a down clue one thing sits on top of another, so the first piece is written above the second.

A man on the phone, and an editor being talked down from something.

The definition is a past tense and the answer matches it: this is the horseman's verb, what you do to a nervous animal until it settles.

7 down THETA

Greek character hugged by lithe Tasmanian (5)

Definition: Greek character · hidden word

Theta is a letter of the Greek alphabet, the one that spells the th sound, so 'Greek character' names it by its class.

8 down RESISTS

Reservation about South Island street fights (7)

Definition: fights · container + charade

To resist is to fight something off — standing against a force rather than trading blows, the sense in which a body resists infection.

  • Reservation → RES Res is the everyday short form of a reservation, as in a table res, and it opens up to take three more pieces.
  • South → S S is the compass short form for south.
  • Island → I I is the short form for island, as on a chart.
  • street → ST St is the short form used on every address.
  • about — Wrapped about something, a word goes round the outside of it and the rest is packed within.

A booking query about brawls on a street somewhere in the South Island.

'Reservation' is a booking rather than a doubt or a tract of land, and 'South Island street' is three separate one- and two-letter conventions rather than an address.

9 down ESOTERIC

Private parts at last held by naughty coterie (8)

Definition: Private · anagram + last letter

Esoteric knowledge is kept inside a small circle, so 'private' means restricted to insiders here rather than personal.

  • parts → S The letter this word finishes on.
  • coterie → COTERIE Seven letters of fodder, a coterie being a small exclusive set.
  • at last — At last asks for the point a word finishes on, which is its final letter and nothing else.
  • naughty — A naughty child will not stay where it is put, and letters described that way will not either.

A misbehaving little in-crowd and somebody's private parts.

'Private parts' is one phrase in the surface and two jobs in the wordplay: 'Private' is the definition, and 'parts' lends nothing but its last letter.

16 down CLEVERER

Brighter ace bails out of chopper with spinning soldiers (8)

Definition: Brighter · deletion + charade + reversal

Both the definition and the answer are comparatives meaning more intelligent, with 'bright' in its mental sense rather than its light one.

  • ace → A A is the standard short form of ace, and it is the letter that jumps.
  • chopper → CLEVER A butcher's chopper is a cleaver, handed over once that A has gone.
  • soldiers → ER The Royal Engineers, the army's sappers, are RE — turned round here.
  • bails out of — Bailing out of an aircraft is jumping clear of it, so this letter leaves the word it was sitting in.
  • spinning — A spinning piece comes to rest facing the other way, so its letters are read in the opposite order.

A pilot parachuting out of a helicopter above troops marching in circles.

'Chopper' is the helicopter the surface wants and the butcher's blade the wordplay needs, and the ace stays a flying ace in either reading.

17 down LOOSE ENDS

Loose ends are the odd jobs and unresolved details left once the main work is done — exactly the unfinished business you go back to tie up.

  • bogs → LOOS A loo is a British lavatory and bog is the coarser word for the same room.
  • Earl → E E is the standard short form for earl in the peerage.
  • concludes → ENDS To conclude something is to end it.

An earl winding up his business in the gents.

Nothing here is an instruction: three ordinary words are simply laid end to end, and 'Earl' is a rank rather than somebody's name.

27 down OWN

Wife visits Iona naked? That's personal! (3)

Definition: personal · deletion + container

Your own something is the one that belongs to you and nobody else, which is what 'personal' means when it modifies a noun this way.

  • Iona → ON The Scottish island with the letters at either end taken off.
  • Wife → W W is the standard short form for wife.
  • visits — A visitor goes inside the place visited, so this letter drops into the middle of the other piece.
  • naked — A word stripped naked has lost what it wore on the outside, so its first and last letters go.

A wife on a skinny-dipping trip to a small Scottish island.

'That's personal!' reads as an objection to a nosy question, and it is doing the defining: the possessive sense, as in minding your own business.
