Independent Sunday Cryptic Crossword No 1,876

Across

1 across GOBBLEDEGOOK

Jargon I allow, having talked turkey first (12)

Definition: Jargon · charade

Jargon -> GOBBLEDEGOOK: gobbledegook is language so clotted with technical and official terms that it stops communicating, which is jargon at its worst.

  • having talked turkey → GOBBLED a turkey gobbles, so a turkey that has talked has gobbled
  • I → EGO the ego is the self, which is why setters render I as those three letters
  • allow → OK to OK something is to allow it, the shortest permission in English
  • first — Saying a piece comes first puts it at the front of the build, so the noise made by the bird opens things and the two short bits follow.

Talking turkey normally means getting down to blunt business, which is the opposite of what the definition describes. Here it is meant literally: the bird makes a noise.

9 across STRANDED

Cut off in long twisted pieces (8)

Definition: Cut off · double definition

Cut off -> STRANDED, left with no way to move on; and in long twisted pieces -> STRANDED, made up of fibres laid together.

  • Cut off → the marooned sense: left behind somewhere with no way of getting on
  • in long twisted pieces → the rope-maker's sense, describing something built from fibres laid and twisted together

Both senses come from one noun. A strand is a shore, so being left on one is being cut off, and a strand is also a single twisted fibre in a rope.

10 across DOODAH

Thingamy's party none enjoyed, on reflection (6)

Definition: Thingamy · charade + reversal

Thingamy -> DOODAH: both are placeholder nouns for an object whose real name has slipped your mind mid-sentence.

  • party → DO a do is a party or social function in everyday British use
  • none → O the letter is the shape of a zero, so it stands for nothing
  • enjoyed → DAH someone who enjoyed a thing HAD it, and those three letters are then turned round
  • on reflection — A reflection shows you a word the wrong way round, so the letters immediately before this are set down in reverse.

Only the final piece reverses, not the whole assembly. The instruction sits at the end of the clue and reaches back over exactly one word.

11 across ACHE

What about rolls for hunger? (4)

Definition: hunger · reversal

hunger -> ACHE: to hunger for something is to ache for it, the sense of longing for what you lack rather than of physical pain.

  • What → EH EH is the written form of the questioning noise you make when you have missed something
  • about → CA CA is short for circa, the Latin for about, used of approximate dates
  • rolls — A thing that rolls turns over on itself, so the two pieces named before it are read back the other way.

The surface offers you a bread roll and a bakery. Rolls is really the instruction, and the definition is the single word waiting at the end.

12 across MINOTAUR

Child eating seconds of steak made our bull cross (8)

Definition: bull cross · container + second letters

bull cross -> MINOTAUR: in Greek myth the Minotaur is the offspring of a bull and a queen, a crossbreed with a bull's head on a man's body.

  • Child → MINOR a minor is a person under the legal age, the law's word for a child
  • steak made our → TAU the second letter of each of these three words, taken in turn
  • eating — Whatever is eaten ends up inside the eater, so the letters named next are swallowed by the word before them.
  • seconds of — The second of anything is the one after the first, so each word listed here gives up its second letter alone.

Seconds of steak reads as a second helping, which is why there is a meal in the surface at all. It is an instruction about letters, not about dinner.

14 across REPUBLIC

Peril Cuba largely represented as one of USSR's vassals (8)

Definition: one of USSR's vassals · anagram + deletion

one of USSR's vassals -> REPUBLIC: the Soviet Union was formally a union of fifteen republics, each nominally sovereign and in practice run from Moscow.

  • Peril → PERIL five letters going into the mix exactly as written
  • Cuba largely → CUB the island's name shorn of its last letter, leaving three
  • largely — Largely a thing is most of it and not quite all, so the final letter drops off before anything else is done.
  • represented — To re-present is to present again in another form, so the letters come back rearranged rather than as printed.

Cuba was a Soviet ally but never a Soviet member, which is the joke. The clue points you at Havana while the definition is really about Ukraine and Georgia.

15 across REFINE

Perfect decider at Wembley netted close to time (6)

Definition: Perfect · charade + last letter

Perfect -> REFINE: to perfect a technique is to work it over until it is polished, which is what refining does to anything crude.

  • decider at Wembley → REF Wembley is England's national football stadium, and the referee is the official whose calls settle the game
  • netted → IN a ball that has been netted has gone in, which is what a commentator means by the word
  • time → E the last letter of that word
  • close to — The close of a word is its ending, so this takes the very last character of what follows and leaves the rest.

Perfect is a verb here rather than an adjective, and it is the first word rather than the last. Both of those are the setter setting you up.

17 across CAVERN

Deep void right inside Vance spread (6)

Definition: Deep void · anagram

Deep void -> CAVERN: a cavern is a large hollow chamber underground, which is literally a deep empty space in the rock.

  • right → R R is the standard abbreviation for right, as in a map reference or a stage direction
  • Vance → VANCE five letters of surname, joining the R in the mix
  • inside — The letter named just before this is not tacked onto either end but dropped among the others.
  • spread — Things that have been spread are scattered out of their arrangement, so the letters settle in an order of their own.

A surname dropped into a grid is almost always fodder rather than a fact you need to know. Nothing about the man matters here beyond his five letters.

19 across IDLENESS

Rest in Leeds, travelling south (8)

Definition: Rest · anagram

Rest -> IDLENESS: rest in the sense of doing nothing whatever, which is the state idleness names rather than a pause between bouts of work.

  • in Leeds → INLEEDS the preposition counts as fodder too, so seven letters go into the mix
  • south → S S is the compass abbreviation for south
  • travelling — Letters that travel do not stay where they were put, so the ones named here arrive somewhere other than where they set off.

The little word in is doing letter work rather than joining anything, and that is the trap: it reads as ordinary grammar while supplying a quarter of the fodder.

21 across CLEAR OFF

Successfully jump over high bunk (5,3)

Definition: bunk · charade

bunk -> CLEAR OFF: to bunk, or do a bunk, is British slang for making a hurried departure, which is exactly what clearing off is.

  • Successfully jump over → CLEAR a horse or an athlete that clears a bar has got over it without touching
  • high → OFF meat or milk called high has gone off, which is to say turned rotten

High meaning rotten is a British food word, from game birds hung until they are high. It is the only piece of this clue that is not being literal.

23 across FETE

Event with games and refreshments cost hosts time (4)

Definition: Event with games and refreshments · container

Event with games and refreshments -> FETE: a fete is an outdoor fundraising day of stalls, games and tea, run by a British village, church or school.

  • cost → FEE a fee is what a thing costs you
  • time → T T is the standard abbreviation for time
  • hosts — A host takes the guest into the house, so the letter named after this sits inside the word before it.

Five words of definition and three of wordplay is a lopsided clue, and that is why the mechanism feels too small to find until you spot where it starts.

25 across JUST SO

Quite violent equestrian events in which Oscar comes last (4,2)

Definition: Quite · anagram

Quite -> JUST SO: just so is the clipped expression of agreement, carrying the same precisely that a bare quite conveys in British speech.

  • violent equestrian events → JOUSTS a joust is mounted combat with lances, the tournament sport of medieval knights
  • in which Oscar comes last — Oscar is O in the radio alphabet, and saying it comes last shifts that one letter to the very end while everything else closes up behind it.

Only one letter travels, so the fodder and the answer look almost identical on the page. That near-identity is what makes this hard to see rather than easy.

26 across CLAPTRAP

Likely Republican gets overwhelming ovation for silly speech (8)

Definition: silly speech · container

silly speech -> CLAPTRAP: claptrap is empty, pretentious talk, and the word began as a bit of stage rhetoric written purely to trap applause.

  • ovation → CLAP an ovation is applause, and applauding is clapping
  • Likely → APT apt means likely to do something, as in apt to forget
  • Republican → R R is the one-letter tag for a Republican in American political reporting
  • gets overwhelming — To be overwhelmed is to be engulfed and covered over entirely, so the two pieces named before it end up buried within the word that follows.

Republican and ovation together paint an American rally, and only one of them is doing any work. The party abbreviation supplies a letter; the rally supplies nothing.

27 across CASH-STRAPPED

Poor saint behind tree shat trousers (4-8)

Definition: Poor · container

Poor -> CASH-STRAPPED: short of money, the literal sense of poor rather than poor meaning shoddy, feeble or unlucky.

  • shat → CRAPPED to shit is to crap, so shat and crapped are two past tenses of the same act
  • tree → ASH the ash is a common British hardwood
  • saint → ST St is the standard abbreviation for saint, as in St Paul's
  • behind — What stands behind something comes after it, so the saint's two letters follow the tree's three rather than leading them.
  • trousers — To trouser a thing is to pocket it, tucking it out of sight inside, so those letters go into the middle of the word beside them.

Trousers is a verb here, not a garment: to trouser money is to pocket it. The whole crude surface exists to keep you reading it as clothing.

Down

2 down OUTRAGE

Shock from back of generator during power cut (7)

Definition: Shock · container + last letter

Shock -> OUTRAGE: an outrage is a thing so offensive it shocks, and the noun names the affront rather than any physical jolt.

  • power cut → OUTAGE an outage is a break in supply, which is what a power cut is
  • generator → R the final letter of that word
  • back of — The back of a word is its final letter, so only the last character of the word named is taken.
  • during — Something that happens during an event falls in the middle of it, so this letter is planted inside the word beside it.

The surface is an electrical fault and the definition is emotional, which is the whole trick: shock reads as a jolt of current right up until the last moment.

3 down BOATHOUSE

American in Soho, tube heaving, gets home for eight (9)

Definition: home for eight · anagram

home for eight -> BOATHOUSE: an eight is a racing shell rowed by a crew of eight, and the boathouse is the shed by the water where it is kept.

  • American → A A is the standard abbreviation for American
  • Soho, tube → SOHOTUBE eight letters handed over exactly as printed, comma ignored
  • heaving — A heaving crowd is one shoving in every direction at once, so the letters end up somewhere other than where they began.

Soho is a district of central London and the tube its underground railway, so the picture is a packed night out. Neither place contributes anything but letters.

4 down EDDY

Vortex that inspired Dyson hoovers? (4)

Definition: Vortex · hidden word

Vortex -> EDDY: an eddy is a small circular current turning against the main flow, a vortex at modest scale.

  • inspired Dyson → EDDY four consecutive letters straddle the gap between these two words, starting near the end of the first
  • hoovers — In Britain any vacuum cleaner is a hoover and to hoover is to suck up, so the words in front have swallowed something whole.

Dyson's bagless cleaners work by spinning air into a cyclone, so the brand and the definition are the same idea twice over, which is why the surface reads as a fact.

5 down ENDEMIC

Venice exposed with rising sea coming in across the region (7)

Definition: across the region · container + middle letters + reversal

across the region -> ENDEMIC: a disease or trait called endemic is constantly present throughout one particular region and scarce anywhere else.

  • Venice → ENIC the city loses the letter at each end, leaving the four in the middle
  • sea → DEM MED is the Mediterranean, the sea a crossword usually means, and it is set down the other way up
  • exposed — Exposing a thing strips off what covers it, and what covers a word is its first and last letters, leaving the middle bare.
  • rising — In a down entry the letters run downwards, so anything rising is written from the bottom up and reads the other way.
  • coming in — Whatever comes in ends up on the inside, so these letters are set within the other piece rather than beside it.

The definition is the four words at the very end, which read as more of the flooding picture. Nothing in the clue signals where the wordplay stops.

6 down ORDINARILY

Arab's cash I brought through Paris airport as usual (10)

Definition: as usual · container

as usual -> ORDINARILY: ordinarily means in the ordinary run of things, and swaps straight into a sentence where you would say as usual.

  • Paris airport → ORLY Orly is the older of the two big airports serving Paris
  • Arab's cash → DINAR the dinar is the currency of Iraq, Jordan, Kuwait and several other Arab states
  • I → I the letter the clue names outright
  • brought through — Bringing something through a place carries it into the middle of that place, so these letters finish up inside.

Paris has two big airports and the short one is the one worth banking: four letters, against the twelve of Charles de Gaulle.

7 down KNOUT

Russian scourge treating UK to a bit of Novichok (5)

Definition: Russian scourge · anagram + first letter

Russian scourge -> KNOUT: the knout was the heavy plaited whip used for judicial flogging in imperial Russia, a scourge in the most literal sense.

  • UK to → UKTO four letters straight off the page, the little preposition counting as fodder
  • Novichok → N the opening letter of the nerve agent's name
  • treating — To treat a substance is to work on it until it comes out altered, so the letters do not stay in the order given.
  • a bit of — One bit of a longer word is its opening letter, and that single character is all the word hands over.

Novichok is the Russian-made nerve agent of the Salisbury poisonings, which is what lets the whole clue pass as a news headline instead of an instruction.

8 down SATURN

World leaders that shout and argue have become sour (6)

Definition: World · charade + first letters

World -> SATURN: definition by example, since Saturn is one particular planet and any planet counts as a world.

  • shout and argue → SA the opening letters of the two verbs, in the order printed
  • become sour → TURN milk that goes off is said to turn, so turning is becoming sour
  • leaders — A word's leader is the letter at its head, so each word named surrenders its opening character and nothing more.

World leaders reads as heads of state, and that is the whole trap: only the first word is the definition, and leaders is an instruction about letters.

13 down CLANGOROUS

Noisy family? Try ours for a change (10)

Definition: Noisy · charade + anagram

Noisy -> CLANGOROUS: clangorous means full of clanging, the harsh metallic racket of bells or machinery, so it is noisy in one particular key.

  • family → CLAN a clan is a family group, the Scottish sense of the word
  • Try ours → GOROUS a try is a GO, and those two letters plus the four of OURS are the ones being reordered
  • for a change — Doing a thing for a change means doing it differently from usual, so these letters are set down in a different order.

Try meaning a go is worth banking, because setters lean on it constantly. Here it quietly supplies two of the ten letters while looking like plain conversation.

16 down FINGERTIP

Better advice about golf which might fetch you a bogey (9)

Definition: might fetch you a bogey · charade + container

might fetch you a bogey -> FINGERTIP: bogey is British slang for a lump of dried nasal mucus, and a fingertip is what digs one out.

  • Better → FINER finer means better in quality, comparative matching comparative
  • golf → G golf is G in the radio alphabet used by pilots and the forces
  • advice → TIP a tip is a handy piece of advice
  • about — One thing about another is wrapped round it, so the single letter named next is enclosed by the word before it.

Golf supplies a letter and a false trail at once. On a course a bogey is a score of one over par, and that is emphatically not the bogey being promised here.

18 down ALL-OUT

Extremely hard for ten playing cricket (3-3)

Definition: Extremely hard · double definition

Extremely hard -> ALL-OUT: an all-out effort keeps nothing in reserve; and in cricket a side is all out once ten of its eleven batters have been dismissed.

  • Extremely hard → the everyday sense: going at something with everything you have and nothing held back
  • for ten playing cricket → a cricket side of eleven bats until ten are dismissed, since the last one has no partner left, and that state has its own name

There is no wordplay to hunt for: two straight meanings sit side by side. The cricket one is the harder half unless you grew up with the game.

19 down IN FOCUS

Sharp intelligence of copper (2,5)

Definition: Sharp · charade

Sharp -> IN FOCUS: of a picture or a lens, sharp means the edges come out crisp and defined, which is exactly what being in focus produces.

  • intelligence → INFO info is short for information, and intelligence is information gathered on someone
  • copper → CU Cu is the chemical symbol for copper, taken from the Latin cuprum
  • of → S the intelligence OF copper is copper's intelligence, and that possessive s is what this small word hands over

Copper wants badly to be a policeman and the whole clue wants to be about a shrewd detective. It is the metal, and even the word of turns out to be a letter.

20 down SEEPAGE

Leaf on the end of twig oozing (7)

Definition: oozing · charade

oozing -> SEEPAGE: seepage is the slow leaking of liquid through something, so it names the oozing itself rather than the stuff that oozes.

  • twig → SEE to twig, in British informal use, is to catch on or suddenly see what is going on
  • Leaf → PAGE a leaf of a book is a page
  • on the end of — Put on the end of a word means written after it, so the piece named first is the one that finishes the answer.

Oozing is a noun here, not a participle: the clue means the slow leaking itself. A leaf and a twig make a tidy surface out of two words that are doing neither botany nor gardening.

22 down ARTIC

Lorry starts to alarm rider with uncontrolled movement (5)

Definition: Lorry · charade + first letters

Lorry -> ARTIC: British shorthand for an articulated lorry, the kind whose cab and trailer are hinged, so a lorry by example.

  • alarm rider → AR the opening letters of the two words, in the order written
  • uncontrolled movement → TIC a tic is an involuntary twitch that nobody can stop happening
  • starts to — The start of a word is its opening letter, and each of the two words after this gives up just that one character.

The surface is a lorry frightening a cyclist, which is exactly the picture wanted. The answer turns out to be the lorry itself and not the fright.

24 down FAIR

Pretty remote island is splitting (4)

Definition: Pretty · container

Pretty -> FAIR: fair in the older sense of good-looking, as in a fair maiden, rather than fair meaning just or merely adequate.

  • remote → FAR far means remote, a long way off
  • island → I I is the map-maker's abbreviation for island
  • splitting — To split a thing is to go into the middle and part it, so the letter named lands between the two halves.

Pretty reads as the qualifier in pretty remote, meaning fairly far away. It is the definition standing alone, and it means good-looking.
