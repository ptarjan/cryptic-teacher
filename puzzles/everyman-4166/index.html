Across

1 across MERSEYSIDE

Where I see my Reds playing? (10)

Definition: Where I see my Reds playing? · &lit + anagram

  • I see my Reds → MERSEYSIDE the supporter's boast supplies exactly ten letters, and they are the ten needed

The Reds are Liverpool FC. The whole sentence works twice over: ask it as a question and it is also the material that answers it, which is why nothing is left over.

6 across OGRE

Man-eater correspondingly retires (4)

Definition: Man-eater · reversal

  • correspondingly → OGRE 'correspondingly' is ergo, the logician's therefore, and those four letters are then read the other way round

Latin ergo is worth stocking up on: crosswords use it for 'therefore', 'so' and 'accordingly'. The surface, meanwhile, is a monster quietly taking his pension.

9 across SOUR GRAPES

Unedified attitude of our gross primate on board (4,6)

Definition: Unedified attitude · container + charade

  • on board → SS SS is the crossword's steamship, and being aboard puts the rest between its two letters
  • our → OUR the clue's own word, taken exactly as it stands
  • gross → GR gr. is the standard abbreviation for a gross, the twelve dozen of the wholesale trade
  • primate → APE an ape is the primate a crossword reaches for first

The surface paints a coarse monkey aboard a ship, which is the wrong picture entirely. The attitude is Aesop's: the fox decides the fruit he cannot reach was never worth having.

10 across PAIR

Item that sounds like prune - or another fruit (4)

Definition: Item · homophone

  • prune → PAIR to prune a shrub is to pare it back, and pare is said this way
  • another fruit → PAIR the pear is the second fruit, and it hides in the same single sound

One sound, three spellings: the clue hands you two of them and you write in the third. 'Item' is the couple sense, two people spoken of as one.

11 across GROUNDHOG DAY

Occasion celebrating pork mince - yet again? (9,3)

Definition: yet again · charade

  • mince → GROUND minced meat is ground meat, the same operation under two names
  • pork → HOG pork comes off a hog, so the animal stands in for its meat
  • Occasion → DAY a big day is an occasion, as in 'the big day'
  • celebrating → surface only: it makes the clue read as a food festival and carries no letters

A pork festival is a fine disguise for a February morning in Pennsylvania, where a rodent forecasts the spring and a film sent the same twenty-four hours round for ever.

15 across EXTREME

Kiss in cemetery, naked, dancing: it's a bit much (7)

Definition: a bit much · anagram + middle letters

  • cemetery, naked → EMETER CEMETERY with its outer C and Y taken off leaves six letters
  • Kiss → X X is the kiss written at the foot of a letter

The graveyard and the dancing belong to the surface's ghost story. The definition is the small phrase at the end, which reads like part of that story and is not.

16 across NAIROBI

Flipping interminable, the writer wearies Scotsman somewhere in Kenya (7)

Definition: somewhere in Kenya · charade + deletion + reversal

  • the writer → I the person writing the clue refers to themselves as I
  • wearies → BOR to weary somebody is to bore them, and the last letter of that verb is dropped
  • Scotsman → IAN Ian is the Scottish forename crosswords keep permanently on hand

Built in clue order the wordplay spells out a tedious Scot, and only turning the whole assembly round produces an African capital. 'Flipping' is doing duty as mild swearing too.

17 across CHERVIL

Butcher, vilified, hides leaves (7)

Definition: leaves · hidden word

  • Butcher, vilified → CHERVIL seven consecutive letters begin at the fourth letter of the first word and run on into the second

A butcher being vilified is a strong enough picture to hide a herb behind. The herb is the salad kind, fine feathery fronds used much as parsley is.

19 across BRIGAND

Highway robber's sturdy as well as round: right! (7)

Definition: Highway robber · container + charade

  • sturdy → BIG a big man is a sturdy one, the same solid build
  • right → R r is the standard abbreviation for right, as in stage directions
  • as well as → AND 'as well as' joins two things exactly as the conjunction does

The exclamation mark makes 'right!' sound like a cry of agreement, which is why it reads as surface rather than as the single letter it is.

20 across I'M SORRY TO SAY

Or is my story a shambles, sadly? (2,5,2,3)

Definition: sadly · anagram

  • Or is my story a → ORISMYSTORYA twelve letters, the whole opening of the clue including its small 'a'

The clue is a writer's anxious question about their own manuscript, and every word of it except the last is material. Only 'sadly' is doing the defining.

23 across TIED

Like shoelaces needing extra time (4)

Definition: Like shoelaces · double definition

  • Like shoelaces → laces are knotted in a bow at the top of the shoe, which is the everyday sense
  • needing extra time → a cup match still level after ninety minutes goes to extra time, which is the sporting sense

One definition is a bow on a shoe and the other a scoreline at the final whistle. Four letters cover both, and not one of them moves.

24 across BLANK SLATE

Fresh start; military dummies behind schedule (5,5)

Definition: Fresh start · charade

  • military dummies → BLANKS blanks are the cartridges fired on exercise, charged but with no bullet in them
  • behind schedule → LATE running behind schedule is simply being late

The surface is an army exercise that has overrun. The answer reaches back to the schoolroom: a writing slate wiped clean, with nothing yet chalked on it.

25 across ROTA

Spoil a list of duties (4)

Definition: list of duties · charade

  • Spoil → ROT food left out will spoil, which is to rot
  • a → A the clue's own word, carried straight across

In British workplaces this is the sheet pinned to the wall saying whose turn it is next: the washing-up, the school run, the night shift.

26 across CYBERNETIC

Britain restrained by unwise reticency regarding futuristic tech (10)

Definition: regarding futuristic tech · anagram

  • Britain → B B is the standard abbreviation for Britain, as on the old oval car plates
  • reticency → RETICENCY nine letters from a rare form of reticence, holding your tongue

'Reticency' is a real if unusual word, and its oddity is the tell: a word that sits awkwardly in the surface is generally there for its letters alone.

Down

1 down MESH

Work together well, as Everyman quiet (4)

Definition: Work together well · charade

  • Everyman → ME the setter's own name in this paper, so it stands for the first person
  • quiet → SH sh is the noise you make when asking for quiet

A setter writing themselves into their own clue is a standing Everyman trick, and 'as' is doing nothing but holding the sentence upright.

2 down ROUX

Piglet's friend, we hear, is basis for various sauces (4)

Definition: basis for various sauces · homophone

  • Piglet's friend → ROUX Roo is the small kangaroo of the Hundred Acre Wood, and his name is said exactly like the French cookery term

A A Milne supplies the friend; the kitchen supplies a French spelling that the sound gives no hint of. Everything turns on hearing the name rather than reading it.

3 down EAGER BEAVER

Beer, average (but free): I'm keen (5,6)

Definition: I'm keen · anagram

  • Beer, average → BEERAVERAGE eleven letters of material; the comma is the setter's punctuation and counts for nothing

The bracket makes 'but free' read as a note on the bar prices, when it is the instruction that unsettles the eleven letters standing just before it.

4 down STATUTE

Output of lawmaker investing time in public art (7)

Definition: Output of lawmaker · container

  • public art → STATUE a statue is the commonest piece of art standing in a public square
  • time → T t is the standard abbreviation for time, as on a stopwatch

The surface has a politician spending time on the arts budget, which is a good disguise. 'Output of lawmaker' means the law itself, not the person who drafts it.

5 down DRESDEN

Compadres denied bottles somewhere on the Elbe (7)

Definition: somewhere on the Elbe · hidden word

  • Compadres denied → DRESDEN seven consecutive letters start in the middle of the first word and finish inside the second

'Compadres' pulls hard towards Spanish and the answer is German, which is the whole disguise: a run of perfectly ordinary letters hides behind a word from the wrong country.

7 down GRAND TOTAL

Sum that gives you a G? (5,5)

Definition: Sum that gives you a G? · cryptic definition

  • Sum → the surface offers plain arithmetic, a column of figures waiting to be added up
  • gives you a G → the italic letter is slang for a thousand, so the figure at the bottom of the column is money and comes to exactly that

No letters move anywhere; the clue is one pun held together by the italics. Read the G as a musical note or a school grade and the whole thing stays shut.

8 down EARLY BIRDS

Archaeopteryx etc: their diet includes worms (5,5)

Definition: Archaeopteryx etc · double definition

  • Archaeopteryx etc → the Jurassic fossil usually called the first of the birds, with 'etc' widening it to the whole dawn group
  • their diet includes worms → the proverb hands the worm to whoever is up first, so the menu identifies the riser

One half is palaeontology and the other is a proverb about getting out of bed, with a colon the only thing between them. Not a single letter shifts.

12 down ORIGINAL SIN

First mistake: milling grain in silo (8,3)

Definition: First mistake · anagram

  • grain in silo → GRAININSILO eleven letters, the whole phrase going into the mill

Farm machinery on the surface, theology underneath. 'Milling' sits so comfortably beside grain that it barely reads as an instruction at all, which is the trap.

13 down DESCRIPTOR

Predictor's remiss: that tells you something (10)

Definition: that tells you something · anagram

  • Predictor's → PREDICTORS ten letters, and the possessive S at the end is part of the material rather than grammar

'Predictor's remiss' reads as a grumble about a forecaster who got it wrong, and that grumble is where every letter comes from.

14 down STRESS TEST

Emphasise cricket match is trial demanding endurance (6,4)

Definition: trial demanding endurance · charade

  • Emphasise → STRESS to stress a word is to emphasise it
  • cricket match → TEST a Test is an international cricket match between two countries, played over five days

Banks and hearts get put through these; the surface offers five days in the sun instead. Test cricket is worth knowing, because setters use it for any kind of trial.

18 down LOYALTY

Monarch and family switching sides to gain allegiance (7)

Definition: allegiance · substitution

  • Monarch and family → ROYALTY royalty is the reigning house, the monarch together with their relatives
  • switching sides → L the R that opens that word is right, and left puts an L in its place

Switching sides normally means the opposite of what is being defined, which is the joke: change one letter of the royal household and out comes the very virtue a turncoat lacks.

19 down BROWNIE

Helpful youngster baked something that's chewy (7)

Definition: Helpful youngster · double definition

  • Helpful youngster → the junior branch of the Guides, girls of about seven to ten, known for doing a good turn daily
  • baked something that's chewy → the dense chocolate square cut from a tray, deliberately underdone so it stays soft

A British uniformed movement and an American tray-bake, sharing one spelling and nothing else. No letters move; you simply have to hear the word twice.

21 down DAFT

Yes, Russian getting UK newspaper is ridiculous (4)

Definition: ridiculous · charade

  • Yes, Russian → DA da is the Russian for yes
  • UK newspaper → FT the FT is the Financial Times, the pink-papered London business daily

Two abbreviations from very different worlds laid end to end, with a Russian buying a British paper as the cover story that keeps the join out of sight.

22 down MERC

Moneyed European's resplendent carrier, primarily? (4)

Definition: Moneyed European's resplendent carrier, primarily? · &lit + first letters

  • Moneyed European's resplendent carrier → MERC four words standing in a row, each handing over its first letter

Read once, the sentence is an instruction about initials; read again, it describes the car those initials spell — a German marque, expensive and gleaming.
