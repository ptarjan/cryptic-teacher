9 across SCRAMMED

Having conned rook inside, made oneself scarce (8)

Definition: made oneself scarce · container

The word means to clear off in a hurry, and its past tense answers 'made oneself scarce' with the tense already matching.

  • Having conned → SCAMMED to scam is to con, and the -ed answers 'having'
  • rook → R R is the chess rook
  • inside — 'Inside' puts the letter named just before it within the previous word, and it doubles as where a con man ends up.

A swindler who has taken somebody in and vanished before anyone notices.

Rook is a bird, a chess piece and a verb meaning to cheat. The clue leans on the chess sense for its letter and on the cheating sense for its story, and the bird is left out entirely.

10 across BOLEYN

Why ‘noble’ misrepresented royal (6)

Definition: royal · anagram

Henry VIII's second queen, so the definition works by example: she was a royal rather than a word meaning royal.

  • Why → Y 'why' is the name of the letter Y, written down
  • ‘noble’ → NOBLE the quotation marks flag the word as five letters rather than a meaning
  • misrepresented — To misrepresent something is to set it out other than as it stands, which is what happens to these six letters.

Somebody asking why a person of good character has been described unfairly.

Quotation marks round a word are a setter's nudge that it is being handled as raw material. The answer is a surname, which is why 'royal' can only point at a person by example.

12 across NO MORE MISTER NICE GUY

Taking over in charge, monster emergin’ – you bastard! (2,4,6,4,3)

Definition: Taking over in charge, monster emergin’ – you bastard! · anagram + &lit

The whole sentence reads straight as the picture: somebody has seized control, turned monstrous, and earned the shout that ends the clue.

  • in charge → IC i/c is the standing shorthand for in charge
  • monster emergin’ → MONSTEREMERGIN fourteen letters exactly as printed, the dropped g included
  • you → YOU 'you' throws its own three letters onto the pile
  • bastard — A bastard version of anything is a mongrel, irregular form of it, so the letters come out mongrelised rather than in the order given.

A takeover, a monster surfacing and a shout of abuse at whoever is behind it.

The sentence describes the answer and supplies it at the same time, which is what makes it an all-in-one. The dropped g of 'emergin' is not just cockney colour: with it the letters would not fit.

15 across ELEVEN-PLUS

Method of selection for school team an asset (6-4)

Definition: Method of selection for school · charade

The exam English children sat at eleven, and passing it decided which secondary school they were sent to.

  • team → ELEVEN a football or cricket side is eleven players, so a team is a number
  • an asset → PLUS an asset is a plus — something on the credit side

Picking a school side, with one candidate having something extra going for them.

Selection is the hinge of the joke: the surface is picking a team and the answer is picking a school, and 'school' belongs to the definition rather than to the wordplay.

17 across OIL

See about including a little incentive – it should help things go smoothly (3)

Definition: it should help things go smoothly · reversal + container + first letter

What you put on a hinge or a bearing so it moves without friction, and by extension what smooths any awkward piece of business.

  • See → LO lo! is the old word for see or behold
  • incentive → I the first letter of the word, and nothing else of it
  • about — Turned about is turned round: the piece just named is written back to front.
  • including — To include something is to have it inside you, so the letter that follows goes within.
  • a little — 'A little' of a word is a small piece of it, and one letter — the one it starts with — is as little as a piece gets.

Somebody slipping in a small sweetener to keep a deal running.

'Lo' is the archaic word for see, met mainly in 'lo and behold' and in carols, and it is one of the handful of two-letter words a setter can build a three-letter entry from.

19 across BAH HUMBUG

With no time for a good wash, smell an irritation? Rubbish! (3,6)

Definition: Rubbish! · deletion + charade

Scrooge's snort dismisses whatever has just been said as nonsense, and 'Rubbish!' is the same exclamation in modern words.

  • time → T T is the standard letter for time, and it is the letter that goes missing
  • a good wash → BAH a good wash is a bath, three of whose letters survive
  • smell → HUM to hum is to smell, and never pleasantly — British and informal
  • an irritation → BUG a bug is something that keeps on irritating you
  • With no — 'With no' says that something is missing from the word that follows, and what it names is what has to go.

Someone skipping a wash and being told, unkindly, how they smell.

The exclamation mark on the definition is the fairest signal in the clue: it says the answer is something a person shouts rather than a noun meaning junk.

20 across TRESPASSES

Right about sixth sense? Fools – they’re wrong! (10)

Definition: they’re wrong · charade + reversal

In the Lord's Prayer these are the wrongs we ask forgiveness for — offences committed, not the people who commit them, and the plural matches 'they'.

  • Right → TR Rt is the abbreviation in Rt Hon, and it is entered the other way about
  • sixth sense → ESP ESP is extra-sensory perception, the sixth sense of ghost stories
  • Fools → ASSES an ass is a fool, and the plural follows the clue's own
  • about — 'About' is the turn-round instruction rather than 'concerning': the letters before it go in the other way.

A sceptic dismissing the people who believe in second sight.

'Right' at the front of a clue almost always looks like the definition, and here the definition is at the far end. The word is only two letters of a title.

22 across ALL-IMPORTANT

Vital mail-order left inside by dock worker (3-9)

Definition: Vital · anagram + container + charade

Vital says nothing matters more, and the answer is the emphatic form that says exactly the same about a thing's importance.

  • mail → ALIM the four letters of mail, put into a new order
  • left → L left is L, and it drops into the middle of them
  • dock → PORT a port is where ships dock
  • worker → ANT the crossword's worker is nearly always the ant
  • order — An order is what letters are put into, and 'mail-order' hides the instruction to put these ones into a different one.
  • inside — 'Inside' drops the letter named just before it into the middle of those letters rather than beside them.

A catalogue parcel left on the quay for a docker to deal with.

'Mail-order' is one word in the surface and two separate instructions in the wordplay — setters call that lift and separate, and a hyphen is the usual place to look for it.

26 across KENYAN

African’s principal relative a bit mixed up (6)

Definition: African · anagram

The answer names the people of one African country, so the definition works by example: true of them, and true of many others too.

  • principal → KEY the principal reason for something is the key one
  • relative → NAN nan is a British child's word for a grandmother
  • a bit mixed up — 'A bit' keeps the shuffle small, and it is small: only the middle pair of letters actually change places.

Somebody's chief relation, in a bit of a muddle.

'Principal' looks like the head teacher the school clues elsewhere have primed you for; it is the adjective, and the answer is a nationality rather than a person's name.

27 across TATTOOER

He designs rubbish as well, I think (8)

Definition: He designs · charade

The man who draws the design and then inks it into the skin, so the definition names him by his trade.

  • rubbish → TAT tat is British for cheap worthless stuff — a stall full of tat
  • as well → TOO as well = too
  • I think → ER er is the noise of somebody hesitating over what they think

Somebody grumbling that a designer's work is junk, and hedging while they say it.

The definition is two words long and sits right at the front, which is the hardest place to spot it when the rest of the sentence runs on as naturally as this one does.

28 across TETHER

Offensive female’s on moor (6)

Definition: moor · charade

Mooring a boat is tying it up, and the answer is the verb for doing the same to an animal — the same rope, a different owner.

  • Offensive → TET the Tet Offensive was the North Vietnamese campaign of 1968, named after the lunar new year
  • female → HER the female pronoun, straight from the clue
  • on — 'On' butts one piece straight up against the other, the female half laid against the offensive one.

An unpleasant woman out on the moors.

'Moor' looks like the bleak upland the surface is standing on and is a verb here — what you do to a boat. 'Offensive' is a military noun, not an adjective about manners.

1 down BASE

Bored due to frequent exposure, ignoring large bottom (4)

Definition: bottom · deletion

The bottom of a thing is what it stands on — the same word does for the foot of a pillar or a lamp.

  • Bored due to frequent exposure → BLASE someone blasé is unimpressed because they have seen the thing far too often
  • large → L large is L on any clothing label, and that L is the letter passed over
  • ignoring — To ignore something is to take no notice of it, so one letter of the word just built is passed over and never reaches the grid.

Someone so used to the sight that even a large backside no longer registers.

Blasé keeps its accent in the dictionary and loses it in the grid, which is how a French import can hide inside four plain English squares.

2 down MARE

Mount Everest’s peak undergoes damage (4)

Definition: Mount · charade + first letter

A female horse is something you ride, and any animal you ride is a mount.

  • damage → MAR to mar something is to damage it
  • Everest’s → E the first letter of the mountain's name, taken from its top
  • peak — A mountain's peak is its very top, and the top of a written name is the letter it starts with.
  • undergoes — Read it as two words: the letter goes under, which in a down entry means it is written below what came first.

The summit of Everest being battered and worn away.

'Mount' wants to belong to Everest and is standing alone as the definition — a mare is a mount, and the mountain is only there for its first letter.

3 down VAMOOSES

Victory when animal locked up runs off (8)

Definition: runs off · charade + container

The word means to clear off in a hurry — American slang picked up from Spanish 'vamos' — and the -s matches 'runs'.

  • Victory → V V for victory, Churchill's two fingers
  • when → AS 'as' does the work of 'when' in a sentence like 'as I arrived'
  • animal → MOOSE the animal is a moose, and it is the piece that gets shut in
  • locked up — Something locked up is shut inside something else, so the beast named goes inside the letters already laid down.

A caged animal bolting the moment somebody declares a victory.

An animal in a clue is usually anagram fodder or a hidden word; this one walks in whole and is simply surrounded by the two letters of a very short word.

4 down DIEGO

Spanish guy departs (5)

Definition: Spanish guy · charade

One of the commonest Spanish men's names, so the definition points at a name rather than at a word meaning 'man'.

  • departs → DIE one word for departing this life
  • departs → GO and the everyday one — the clue's single verb is cashed a second time

A man from Spain walking out.

One word of the clue has to be spent twice, once for each half of the name. Setters do this often and never signal it; the enumeration is what tells you a second helping is needed.

6 down UTOPIA

Lovely place Surinam, occasionally Trump admitted (6)

Definition: Lovely place · alternate letters + container

The name for an imagined perfect society, which makes 'lovely place' a deliberate understatement rather than a straight synonym.

  • Surinam → UIA every second letter of the country, starting from the second
  • Trump → TOP to trump a card is to top it
  • occasionally — Something done occasionally happens at intervals, and letters taken at intervals means every second one of the name.
  • admitted — To admit a person is to let them in, so the piece just named is let inside the letters picked out.

A South American country letting a certain president in through the door now and then.

'Trump' wants to be the president the surface is grinning about; it is the card-table verb. Surinam sits on the north-east shoulder of South America and is here purely for its spelling.

7 down BRENT GOOSE

One paying flying visit from Oregon best getting busy (5,5)

Definition: One paying flying visit · anagram

This small dark goose breeds in the Arctic and only drops in on British coasts for the winter — a visitor that arrives on the wing.

  • Oregon best → OREGONBEST the state and the adjective hand over five letters each, and all ten are shaken
  • getting busy — Getting busy here is bustle rather than work: the letters mill about and none of them stays where it was printed.

Someone dropping in briefly, flying over from Oregon while everything gets hectic.

'Flying visit' does double duty in the definition: it is both the shortness of the stay and the literal way the bird turns up, which is what makes the definition fair rather than vague.

8 down GENERALIST

He’s not particularly concerned about upsetting celebs with intelligence (10)

Definition: He’s not particularly concerned · charade + reversal

Someone whose interest runs across the board rather than into particulars, so the definition turns on the two senses of 'particular'.

  • intelligence → GEN gen is British informal for information — the gen on something
  • about → ER 'about' is re, and this one arrives the other way up
  • celebs → ALIST the A-list is where the celebrated names are kept
  • upsetting — To upset a thing is to tip it over, and in a down entry the piece it tips is then written upwards.

A man shrugging at gossip about famous people while claiming to be well informed.

'Particularly' is the hinge. Taken as an adverb it says he is not very bothered; taken as 'in particular' it says what kind of mind he has, and only the second reading is the definition.

11 down REBUTS

Attempts to prove wrong tense used in puzzle (6)

Definition: Attempts to prove wrong · container

The verb means to argue that a claim is false; it stays an attempt because the argument can fail, and the -s matches a singular subject.

  • puzzle → REBUS a rebus is the sort of puzzle that spells a word out in pictures
  • tense → T T is the grammarian's tense
  • used in — 'Used in' sets the letter to work inside the other word rather than alongside it.

A grammar question about which tense some puzzle is written in.

A rebus is the picture puzzle — an eye, a heart and a ewe for a valentine — and it is one of very few crossword words that names a rival kind of puzzle.

13 down WET BLANKET

Angry tweet about empty fire extinguisher? (3,7)

Definition: fire extinguisher · anagram + container

A soaked blanket really is thrown over flames to smother them; the question mark admits that the everyday sense of the phrase is the killjoy one.

  • tweet → WETET the five letters of the message, reordered and then parted to make room
  • empty → BLANK an empty form is a blank one
  • Angry — An angry message is one that has lost its composure, and letters that lose their composure lose their printed order too.
  • about — 'About' means round here rather than concerning: one piece ends up wrapped round the other.

A furious post online about nothing whatsoever.

The definition is the literal reading of a phrase almost nobody uses literally, and the question mark is the setter apologising for it. A tweet is short, which is a hint at how little fodder there is.

14 down WE SHALL NOT BE MOVED

Expression of defiance suggested by nobles’ wealth (2,5,3,2,5)

Definition: Expression of defiance · anagram

The old union and civil-rights song is sung by people announcing that they will not budge, so the phrase is defiance itself.

  • nobles’ wealth → WESHALLNOTBE twelve letters that rearrange into the first four words of the entry
  • suggested by → MOVED the clue runs backwards: the entry is an instruction, and this is the word in it that does the shuffling

A protest slogan brought on by the sight of how much the aristocracy owns.

The shuffling instruction is not in the clue at all — it is the last word of the entry. Read the answer as an order rather than a statement and it tells you to move the first four words, which produces the clue's own phrase.

16 down PERIOD

Time for a party, I reflected (6)

Definition: Time · charade + reversal

A stretch of time, the plainest of synonyms — the sense a history lesson uses when it talks about the Georgian one.

  • for → PER per means for each — twice per week
  • I → I the pronoun hands over its own letter
  • a party → OD a do is a party, and this one is turned round
  • reflected — What is reflected comes back at you the other way round, so those two letters are written in reverse.

Somebody deciding it is time to celebrate, then thinking better of it.

A 'do' for a party is British and informal — a works do, a leaving do — and it is the only piece here that the mirror acts on.

18 down MAGNETIC

Commend school over accepting small number good at drawing (8)

Definition: good at drawing · charade + reversal + container

Something that attracts pulls things towards it, so the definition uses 'draw' in its dragging sense rather than its pencil one.

  • school → MAG a gam is a school of whales, written here the other way about
  • small number → N n is the mathematician's stand-in for a number nobody has fixed yet
  • Commend → ETIC to cite someone is to commend them, and it too goes in backwards
  • over — Turned over is turned round, and here it acts on both pieces at once rather than on the nearer one only.
  • accepting — To accept a thing is to take it in, so the letter named is taken into the middle of what has just been turned.

A school being praised, with a modest roll of pupils who are good at art.

Both halves of the wordplay are laid down and then turned as one lump, which is why neither word can be reversed on its own and made to fit. A gam is a school of whales, a word that survives mainly in whaling books and crosswords.

21 down IMPALE

Stick around, mate – Vlad’s close to finish (6)

Definition: Stick · charade + container + last letter

The verb means to stick something on a point, which is 'Stick' in its transitive sense — stick a pin through it.

  • Vlad’s → IM Vlad set this puzzle, so 'Vlad's' is the setter's own I'm
  • mate → PAL a mate is a pal, and this is the piece that gets enclosed
  • close → E the last letter of the word 'close' itself
  • around — What is around a thing sits on both sides of it, which is where the outer pieces end up.
  • to finish — 'To finish' asks for the letter a word finishes on rather than for the word itself.

Vlad telling a friend to hang about until the end.

Vlad the Impaler is the joke the setter is making at his own expense. 'Close' is there to be spelled rather than read, which is the one step that has to be seen before the rest falls out.

23 down TRAIN

Coach drops team leader for a start (5)

Definition: Coach · charade + first letter

Coaching somebody is drilling them into shape, and the answer is the everyday verb for doing precisely that.

  • team → T the letter at the front of 'team'
  • drops → RAIN what falls in drops, taken as a noun
  • leader — The leader of a word is the letter out in front of the rest of it.
  • for a start — 'For a start' says where that letter belongs — at the beginning, before anything else arrives.

A manager leaving the captain out of the side.

'Drops' looks like an instruction to delete something, which is the reading the surface is built to encourage. It is a noun, and what comes in drops is rain.

24 down GONG

Leaving, I wanted knighthood possibly? (4)

Definition: knighthood possibly · deletion

British informal for a medal or decoration, and a knighthood is one instance of an honour — 'possibly' is what marks it as an example.

  • Leaving → GOING leaving is going
  • I → I the letter the word is lacking
  • wanted — 'Wanted' is the older sense of lacking, as in found wanting, so the letter it names is not there.

Somebody on their way out who had been hoping for a title.

Gong for a medal is the word a British paper reaches for at honours-list time, and 'possibly' is the setter conceding that a knighthood is only one kind of them.

25 down ARMY

Pub finally sacked crazy host (4)

Definition: host · deletion

A host in the older sense is a great armed multitude — the heavenly host — not the person who throws the party.

  • Pub → B the last letter of 'Pub'
  • crazy → BARMY barmy means crazy, and it is the word losing a letter
  • finally — 'Finally' picks out the letter a word ends on, and nothing more of it.
  • sacked — Sacked is dismissed, so the letter just picked out is dismissed from the word that follows.

A pub throwing out somebody who has been behaving oddly.

Barmy is British for crazy and comes from barm, the froth on fermenting beer, so the pub is still in the picture even after it has handed over its letter.
