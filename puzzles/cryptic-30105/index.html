Across

1 across TREASURE

Love endless free entertainment? Indeed (8)

Definition: Love · charade + deletion

To treasure something is to hold it dear, which is 'love' in the verb sense of prizing rather than romance.

  • free entertainment → TREA a treat is an entertainment laid on at somebody else's expense, and it arrives here one letter short
  • Indeed → SURE 'sure' is how you agree with someone, the same shrug of assent as 'indeed'
  • endless — The word means having no end, so the piece beside it turns up with its final letter taken off.

Someone is asked whether they love unlimited free entertainment, and agrees emphatically.

'Love' is the verb here — to treasure a thing rather than to feel love for one — and a 'treat' is entertainment somebody else is paying for.

5 across ISLAND

Is not sea, but is in it (6)

Definition: Is not sea, but is in it · charade + &lit

An island is land entirely surrounded by water, which is precisely something that is not sea and yet sits in it.

  • Is → IS the clue's opening word taken as two letters, exactly as written
  • not sea → LAND dry ground, defined here by what it is not — the two of them cover the whole surface of the planet

The whole clue is the definition and its first three words are also the wordplay, which is what &lit means: read one sentence twice, once for sense and once as instructions.

9 across RAG TRADE

It’s the fashion to purchase from this business (3,5)

Definition: this business · charade

The rag trade is the British name for the clothing business, so 'this business' points at the industry the clue has been describing.

  • It’s the fashion → RAG clothes are rags in the slang of the people who sell them — glad rags — and the fashion is what you wear
  • to purchase → TRADE to buy and sell is to trade, so purchasing is half of what the word covers

Everybody is buying from this shop because that is the fashionable thing to do.

'It's the fashion' reads as a passing craze and means clothing here — the industry has always called what it sells rags, cheerfully enough to put the word in its own name.

10 across MEDIUM

Person entranced that is often called happy (6)

Definition: Person entranced · double definition

A medium is the person who goes into a trance to reach the dead, and also the middle course between extremes that idiom calls happy.

  • Person entranced → the spiritualist who goes into a trance to carry messages from the dead
  • that is often called happy → the middle course between two extremes, which English pairs with 'happy' so often that the adjective points straight at the noun

Somebody deep in a trance is described as blissfully content.

The séance and the middle way are the two senses, and the idiom 'a happy medium' is what the second half is pointing at — the phrase does the defining.

11 across EVENTIDE

Flow that’s regular late in the day (8)

Definition: late in the day · charade

Eventide is an old word for evening, so the definition names the stretch of time it covers rather than an event.

  • regular → EVEN a regular pulse is an even one, steady and unvarying
  • Flow → TIDE the sea's twice-daily flow is the tide

A steady current keeps running as the day gets late.

'Flow' is clued first and lands second in the answer, so the build runs against the reading. The word itself is poetic — hymns and Victorian verse rather than weather forecasts.

12 across FINALS

Late stages of World Cup seen in Netherlands, Belarus and Wales (6)

Definition: Late stages of World Cup · charade + last letters

The finals are the closing knockout rounds of a tournament, which is exactly the late stages of a World Cup.

  • seen in → FINAL not which name the letter is in but where in each name it is seen — the position, spelled out as a word
  • Netherlands, Belarus and Wales → S the one letter all three of these names close with

Three countries are named as places where the closing rounds of the World Cup were watched.

Read the answer as two words and the clue explains itself: the letter S is what is final in Netherlands, Belarus and Wales. The World Cup half is a straight definition.

14 across PICCALILLI

Relish saying ‘Choose a flower’ (10)

Definition: Relish · homophone

Piccalilli is a pickled vegetable relish, so 'relish' names it by the class of condiment it belongs to.

  • Choose a flower → PICCALILLI to choose is to pick, and the flower is the lily; said at speed the three words run into one
  • saying — Something said is heard rather than read, so the quoted words count for their sound and not for their spelling.

A speaker takes pleasure in telling someone to go and pick a bloom.

Say the quoted words at speed and they turn into the name of a mustard-yellow pickle. 'Relish' is both the definition and, in its other sense, what the speaker is doing.

18 across REANIMATED

With new energy, admire neat dancing (10)

Definition: With new energy · anagram

Something reanimated has been brought back to life, so it carries on with new energy where it had stopped.

  • admire neat → ADMIRENEAT ten letters exactly, which is the enumeration, waiting to be reordered
  • dancing — Dancers keep swapping places, and the letters in front of the word take new positions in the same way.

Watching some tidy dancing with fresh enthusiasm.

'With new energy' looks like a lead-in — 'new' is a favourite anagram signal — and is in fact the definition, with the shuffle instruction saved for the last word.

22 across CONKER

Reportedly overcome hard nut (6)

Definition: hard nut · homophone

A conker is the hard shiny seed of the horse chestnut, so 'hard nut' describes it exactly.

  • overcome → CONKER to overcome an enemy is to conquer them, and in British speech that word and the six letters wanted are one sound
  • Reportedly — A word reported is one you hear said, so what is wanted is the sound and not the spelling.

A tough character is said to have been beaten.

British children play conkers by threading horse chestnuts on string and swinging them at each other's, so the hard nut is a literal one. Say 'conquer' aloud and you have the spelling.

23 across SERAGLIO

Gaoler is to reorganise women’s quarters (8)

Definition: women’s quarters · anagram

A seraglio is the shut-off part of a Turkish palace where the women lived, which is a set of women's quarters.

  • Gaoler is → GAOLERIS eight letters, matching the enumeration, ready to be rearranged
  • to reorganise — Reorganising a thing keeps every part of it and changes only the arrangement, which is what the letters before it undergo.

A prison officer is told to sort out the women's wing.

'Gaoler' is the British spelling of jailer, which is where the O and the A come from. A seraglio is the sealed women's part of an Ottoman palace, the harem.

24 across IN TOTO

Completely at home with Dorothy’s pet (2,4)

Definition: Completely · charade

In toto is Latin for 'in total', used in English to mean wholly or entirely — 'completely' with a gown on.

  • at home → IN somebody at home is in, the standing crossword sense of the word
  • Dorothy’s pet → TOTO the little dog Dorothy carries through the Land of Oz

Somebody is entirely comfortable in the company of Dorothy's dog.

The Latin tag means 'in total' and survives in legal English — a claim rejected in toto. 'Completely at home' reads as one phrase, which is where the split hides.

25 across CARTHAGE

Ancient city prison: pictures initially hung in it? (8)

Definition: Ancient city · container + first letter

Carthage was the great Phoenician city on the North African coast, ancient by any reckoning and long gone.

  • prison → CAGE a cage holds a prisoner, and this one is about to be opened after its first letter
  • pictures → ART pictures hung on a wall are art
  • initially hung → H the opening letter of the word the instruction sits on
  • initially — It calls for the opening letter of the word beside it and nothing more of it.
  • in it — The pictures go inside the prison, so the letters just gathered are placed within the word for it.

An old city's prison, with pictures hung on the cell walls.

The question mark covers the cheek of hanging an art collection in a cell. Carthage was the Phoenician power on the North African coast that Rome fought three wars to destroy.

26 across ESCAPE

Key exercise, to trap a fly (6)

Definition: fly · charade + container

To fly is to make off in a hurry — the suspect flew — which is what escaping is, verb for verb.

  • Key → ESC the key at the top left of a keyboard, the one that gets you out of a dialogue box
  • a → A the clue's own indefinite article, caught between the two other pieces
  • exercise → PE PE is the standing abbreviation for exercise, physical education at school
  • to trap — Trapping a thing shuts it in, so the letter it points at is enclosed by the pieces on either side.

An important drill designed to catch a fly.

'Key' is the button on a keyboard rather than a door key or a musical one, and 'fly' is the verb: birds do it, and so do prisoners.

27 across VELOCITY

Love to break down large town fastness? (8)

Definition: fastness · anagram + charade

Velocity is speed in a given direction, and 'fastness' is the noun from fast, the quality of being quick.

  • Love → VELO the four letters of the clue's first word, set down in a new order
  • large town → CITY a town becomes a city once it is grand enough
  • to break down — Breaking a thing down leaves its parts loose, free to be built up again in a different order.

Someone would love to knock down the stronghold of a large town.

'Fastness' is a stronghold in the surface and speed in the answer — fast as in quick, not fast as in held firm — which is what the question mark is marking.

1 down TARGET

Given time, smash great goal (6)

Definition: goal · charade + anagram

A target is the mark you aim at and a goal is the objective you aim for — the same idea in two vocabularies.

  • Given time → T time hands over its one-letter abbreviation, the T of a timetable
  • great → ARGET the five letters of the word resettled in a new order
  • smash — Smash a thing and the bits can be put back in any order, which is the licence the letters beside it are given.

Given enough time, a player will hammer in a great goal.

'Goal' is the target you aim at, not the one you score, which is what the football surface wants you to hear.

2 down EAGLET

Young bird caught by beagle terrified (6)

Definition: Young bird · hidden word

An eaglet is an eagle's chick, so 'young bird' names it by its general kind rather than its species.

  • beagle terrified → EAGLET run the two words together and the six letters you need are consecutive, starting one letter in
  • caught by — Anything caught by something is held fast inside it, so the letters wanted sit unbroken within the words that follow.

A terrified beagle has caught a chick.

'Terrified' looks like it should be shaking the letters up; it is simply the second half of the hiding place. The bird is a specific one, the chick of an eagle.

3 down STRATA

Working on skills, turning up in more than one bed (6)

Definition: more than one bed · charade + reversal

Strata are the layers rock settles in, and geologists call each layer a bed, so a plural definition meets a plural answer.

  • Working on → AT somebody working on a job is at it — a loose equivalence the setter leans on
  • skills → ARTS an art is a practised skill, so the plural answers the plural
  • turning up — In a down entry, up runs back towards the top of the grid, so the pieces just built are read the other way.

Somebody practising their craft keeps appearing in more than one bed.

Beds here are geological, the layers a cliff face shows you, not anything in a bedroom or a garden. 'Working on' is the loose piece: to be at something is to be working on it.

4 down RED ADMIRAL

Senior Soviet officer, one previously safely cocooned (3,7)

Definition: one previously safely cocooned · charade

A red admiral is a butterfly, so it did spend its pupal stage safely wrapped in a cocoon before it flew.

  • Soviet → RED the Soviet side were the Reds, after the colour of the flag
  • officer → ADMIRAL at sea the flag rank above captain, the one with a fleet under him
  • Senior → no letters of its own: it fixes which officer is meant, the one at the top of the navy rather than a lieutenant

A high-ranking Russian officer who spent his early years wrapped up safely.

'Senior Soviet officer' wants to be the definition and is the wordplay; the butterfly is what the rest describes, having spent its pupal stage wrapped up safely.

6 down SPECIAL K

No ordinary king’s breakfast? (7,1)

Definition: breakfast · charade

Special K is a breakfast cereal, so 'breakfast' names it by the meal it belongs to — one brand for a whole class.

  • No ordinary → SPECIAL whatever is not ordinary is special
  • king → K K is the standing abbreviation for king, as on a chess scoresheet

A king sits down to a breakfast rather grander than anyone else's.

The question mark is the setter admitting this is one brand of cereal standing in for a whole meal. The apostrophe-s is joinery rather than a possessive to solve.

7 down AMICABLE

Is the crossword setter to appear on television as friendly? (8)

Definition: friendly · charade

Amicable describes dealings conducted in a friendly spirit, so the adjective is a plain synonym in its everyday sense.

  • Is → AM the verb changes person: 'is' becomes 'am' once the speaker is the subject
  • the crossword setter → I the setter writing the clue speaks of himself in the first person
  • to appear on television → CABLE cable is how television reaches many homes, so appearing on one is appearing on the other

The person who wrote this puzzle wonders whether he would come across as friendly on television.

'The crossword setter' means I or ME in a Guardian clue, a convention worth owning since it comes round most weeks. Cable is television named by the route it arrives on.

8 down DOMESTIC

Maid accustomed to home (8)

Definition: Maid · double definition

A domestic is a household servant, and as an adjective the word covers anything settled about the house, animals included.

  • Maid → the noun sense: the servant employed to keep a household running
  • accustomed to home → the adjective sense, used of a creature that has settled to living indoors alongside people

A servant who has grown thoroughly used to the house she works in.

'Maid' hands you the noun and 'accustomed to home' the adjective, with nothing but a space between them. No letters are shuffled, hidden or spelled out anywhere.

13 down ACCELERATE

A cold salad plant not quite consumed: hurry up! (10)

Definition: hurry up · charade + deletion

To accelerate is to go faster, which is what 'hurry up' asks of anybody it is said to.

  • A → A the clue's own first word, lifted as a single letter
  • cold → C C is the standing abbreviation for cold, as on a bathroom tap
  • salad plant → CELER celery is the stringy green stalk in the salad bowl, arriving one letter short
  • consumed → ATE consumed in the past tense — what you did to your dinner
  • not quite — Stopping not quite at the end of a word means letting go of its last letter and keeping the rest.

A chilled salad left half-eaten, and somebody being told to get a move on.

The colon does the work of an equals sign, splitting the recipe from the definition. 'Hurry up!' is an exclamation in the surface and a plain synonym in the parse.

15 down PRACTICE

Cost to hold part of play rehearsal (8)

Definition: rehearsal · container

A practice is the session where a team or a cast runs through it all before the real thing — a rehearsal.

  • Cost → PRICE the cost of a thing is its price, and this word is about to be opened up
  • part of play → ACT a play comes divided into acts, so a part of one is an act
  • to hold — To hold a thing is to have it inside you, so one piece here is wrapped around another rather than laid beside it.

The ticket price covers a single act of the play.

'Part of play' looks like an instruction to take some letters out of the word 'play'; it is an act, the division a drama comes in. 'Cost' is a noun, the price on the ticket.

16 down MAGNETIC

Showing a natural ability to draw? (8)

Definition: Showing a natural ability to draw? · cryptic definition

Something magnetic draws things to itself — iron to a magnet, people to a person — and does so by its nature.

  • Showing a natural ability → the surface offers innate talent, the child who could always sketch; 'natural' also means belonging to nature, and this is one of its forces
  • to draw → not drawing on paper: to draw is to pull towards you, which is what iron filings feel

Someone is praised as a born artist.

'Draw' moves from pencils to attraction and 'natural' from talent to physics: the pull a lodestone exerts on iron is entirely a property of nature.

17 down SIDESTEP

Avoid son, despite staggering (8)

Definition: Avoid · anagram

To sidestep something is to avoid it by moving aside and letting it pass, rather than by refusing it.

  • son → S S is the standing abbreviation for son, as on a family tree
  • despite → DESPITE the seven letters of the preposition, thrown in with the S as fodder
  • staggering — Somebody staggering cannot hold a straight line, and the letters beside the word do not hold their order either.

Even while lurching about, somebody manages to keep out of their son's way.

'Despite' reads as a conjunction arguing with the first half of the sentence, and it is the fodder; the extra letter it needs is the son standing next to it.

19 down TATTOO

Pageant shows tasteless stuff as well (6)

Definition: Pageant · charade

A tattoo is a military pageant of drill, bands and drums put on for an audience, so 'pageant' names its kind.

  • tasteless stuff → TAT tat is British for cheap and nasty goods, the junk at the back of a market stall
  • as well → TOO both mean in addition

A pageant turns out to be full of cheap junk as well.

A tattoo here is the military display of massed pipes and marching bands, the Edinburgh sort, rather than the ink on somebody's arm that 'tasteless' nudges you towards.

20 down ALKALI

Walk a little, holding lime or soda perhaps (6)

Definition: lime or soda perhaps · hidden word

Lime and soda are both alkalis, so the clue defines the class by naming two members, with 'perhaps' flagging the leap.

  • Walk a little → ALKALI run the three words together and six consecutive letters spell what you need, starting one letter into the first
  • holding — Whatever is held sits inside its holder, so the letters wanted run unbroken through the words just before.

Somebody strolls a short way holding a lime or a soda.

'Lime or soda perhaps' is a definition by example — both are bases in the chemical sense, which is what the 'perhaps' is apologising for. The drink belongs to the surface only.

21 down COMEDY

Will’s play full of errors? Dante’s was divine (6)

Definition: Will’s play full of errors · double definition

Shakespeare's Comedy of Errors and Dante's Divine Comedy both complete their titles with the word, which names a work with a happy ending.

  • Will’s play full of errors → Will is Shakespeare, and one of his titles is completed by the word wanted plus 'of Errors'
  • Dante’s was divine → the Florentine poet's great work is his, and it is described by the adjective sitting here

Shakespeare wrote a play full of mistakes, while Dante's was heavenly.

Shakespeare's is The Comedy of Errors and Dante's is The Divine Comedy, so each half of the clue hands you a famous title with the same word lifted out.
