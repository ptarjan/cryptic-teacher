Across

1 across SUBJECT

Take flight, carrying Charlie, following sea transportation issue (7)

Definition: issue · charade + container

'Issue' in the sense of the matter under discussion: the issue before a meeting is the topic on the table, not a problem or a magazine.

  • sea transportation → SUB a sub is a submarine, the usual short form
  • Take flight → JET to jet off somewhere is to take a flight
  • Charlie → C Charlie is C in the NATO alphabet
  • carrying — What you carry you hold inside you, so the flight word closes around the letter handed to it rather than sitting beside it.
  • following — To follow is to come along behind, so the flight half is written after the vessel instead of in front of it.

'Take flight' wants to be fleeing and 'issue' wants to be a magazine or a problem. Neither reading survives, and the letter goes inside the verb rather than after it.

5 across HONOURS

Recognises love shared by you and me? (7)

Definition: Recognises · charade

To honour somebody is to recognise them formally — the verb behind an honours list, where public recognition is the whole point.

  • love → HON hon is the affectionate address, short for honey
  • shared by you and me → OURS what belongs to the two of us together is ours

'Love' is the term of endearment, not the tennis nought a solver reaches for first, and the question mark is the setter admitting the short form.

9 across HEADS

Oversees advancement of ecstasy in hell (5)

Definition: Oversees · anagram

To head a department is to be in charge of it, so the -s form answers 'oversees' as a verb rather than naming any body part.

  • hell → HADES hell in Greek myth is Hades, the underworld itself
  • advancement of ecstasy — Ecstasy is E by the usual drug convention, and advancing it moves that letter one place forward through the others.

Nothing is scrambled at random here. One letter steps forward and everything else keeps its place, which is why the indicator names the letter it is moving.

10 across TIMETABLE

Schedule board to pursue Master embroiled in relationship (9)

Definition: Schedule · charade + container

A schedule set out by the clock is a timetable — the British word for the printed plan of a school day or a train service.

  • relationship → TIE a tie is a bond between two people
  • Master → M M is the standard abbreviation for Master
  • board → TABLE board and table are the same piece of furniture, as in 'board and lodging'
  • to pursue — To pursue is to chase along behind, so the furniture word is written after everything the clue has already built.
  • embroiled in — Someone embroiled in an affair is caught up inside it, so the abbreviation sits within the bond rather than beside it.

The surface hints at gossip about a schoolmaster; the M is only an abbreviation. Note that 2 down makes the same board-for-table swap in the opposite direction.

11 across ORDINARILY

Normally, on the other hand, start off cautiously after loud noise (10)

Definition: Normally · charade + deletion

Ordinarily means in the ordinary run of things, so it does the job 'normally' does — a statement of habit rather than of what is normal.

  • on the other hand → OR the word that offers the alternative is or
  • loud noise → DIN a din is a continuous loud noise
  • cautiously → ARILY the adverb wanted is warily, and its first letter has been started off
  • start off — To start something off is to begin it, and beginning this adverb means taking its opening letter away.
  • after — 'after' says where the trimmed adverb goes: behind the noise rather than in front of it.

'Start off cautiously' reads as advice about how to begin something, which is the trap: it is an instruction to take the first letter off an adverb.

12 across FACT

Reality show tailing female (4)

Definition: Reality · charade

A fact is what is genuinely the case, and 'reality' names the same thing as a noun: the truth of things, not a TV format.

  • female → F F is the standard abbreviation for female
  • show → ACT an act is a turn shown on stage
  • tailing — To tail somebody is to follow along behind them, so the performance is written after the letter it trails.

'Reality show' is the trap — it reads as the television genre. The definition stops after the first word, and the show belongs to the wordplay.

14 across DISPLACEMENT

Being out of order, criticise and put down setter, heartless nut! (12)

Definition: Being out of order · charade + deletion

Displacement is the state of having been shifted out of position, which is what 'out of order' means when order is arrangement rather than repair.

  • criticise → DIS to dis someone is to criticise them, in the slang spelling
  • put down → PLACE to put something down is to place it
  • setter → ME the setter is the person writing the clue, so 'setter' is ME
  • nut → NT the U at its centre is the heart that goes
  • heartless — The heart of a three-letter word is its middle letter, and a heartless word has had that letter taken out.

'Out of order' means out of its proper place here, not broken or badly behaved. The setter-is-ME convention is worth owning: it turns up in every grid.

18 across INTELLIGENCE

Another order for gilt Nile scene excluding special information (12)

Definition: information · anagram + deletion

Information gathered about an enemy or a rival is intelligence — the spy's sense of the word, which is why the surface talks about scenes and exclusions.

  • gilt Nile scene → GILTNILESCENE the three words are here for their thirteen letters, not their picture
  • special → S S is the standard abbreviation for special, and it is the letter left out
  • Another order — Order here means arrangement rather than command, and another arrangement of the same letters is what the clue wants.
  • excluding — What is excluded is kept out, so the letter named next never joins the letters being shuffled.

The deletion happens before the shuffle, so count the fodder first: thirteen letters offered, one taken away, twelve to be rearranged into the enumeration.

21 across GRID

Giganet’s first free network (4)

Definition: network · charade + first letter

The grid is the network of cables distributing electricity around a country — Britain calls its own the National Grid.

  • Giganet’s first → G the opening letter of that made-up company's name
  • free → RID to be rid of something is to be free of it
  • first — 'first' asks only for the opening letter of the invented company standing in front of it.

Giganet is invented for the surface and only its initial matters. The definition is electrical, which is what makes the technology surface sound plausible.

22 across BATTALIONS

Flying creature with claws circling international groups of soldiers (10)

Definition: groups of soldiers · charade + container

A battalion is an army unit of several hundred troops, so the plural names groups of soldiers by their formal size.

  • Flying creature → BAT the flying creature wanted is the only mammal that does it properly
  • claws → TALONS the claws of a bird of prey are its talons
  • international → I I is the standard abbreviation for international
  • circling — Something circling goes right round the thing it circles, so the claws close on both sides of the letter between them.

The surface shows one bird — flying, clawed and circling — while the parse needs three separate pieces out of it, and the circling belongs to the letters, not the bird.

25 across EDUCATION

Teaching can die out if it’s disordered (9)

Definition: Teaching · anagram

'Teaching' as a noun names the activity, and education is that activity taken as a whole — schooling rather than a single lesson.

  • can die out → CANDIEOUT three ordinary words in a row, offered for their letters
  • disordered — Something disordered has been shaken out of its order, which is the instruction to find a new one for these nine letters.

The fodder hides by being a sentence with an opinion in it. Reading 'can die out' as a gloomy prediction is exactly what stops you counting its letters.

26 across ANGLE

Swing headless fish (5)

Definition: fish · deletion

To angle is to fish with rod and line — the verb behind 'angler', and the sense that keeps the word alive outside geometry.

  • Swing → DANGLE something swinging loosely from a hook dangles
  • headless → D the head of that swinging word is its D, and that is what goes
  • headless — The head of a word is the letter it begins with, so a headless word has lost its first letter.

Three words and none of them does the job you expect. 'Fish' is the definition, in the sense of what an angler does, and 'Swing' is the word to behead.

27 across TREBLES

Wood surrounding the outskirts of Blackpool gets three times bigger (7)

Definition: gets three times bigger · container + outer letters

To treble is to multiply by three, so a sum that trebles has grown to three times what it was before.

  • Wood → TREES a wood is a stand of trees
  • Blackpool → BL the outer letters of the resort's name, its first and its last
  • surrounding — What surrounds something wraps all the way round it, so the woodland word opens up and closes again on both sides.
  • outskirts of — The outskirts of a town are its outer edges, so the edges of the word are the two letters wanted.

Blackpool is an English seaside resort with a tower and a pier, but the clue wants only the letters at its two ends — everything in the middle is scenery.

28 across ELEMENT

Part company in English hamlet, half-cut (7)

Definition: Part · container + deletion

An element is one part of a whole: the elements of a plan are its components, which is the sense the definition uses.

  • English → E E is the usual abbreviation for English
  • hamlet → LET a hamlet is a village too small for a church, and only its back half is used
  • company → MEN a company is a body of men, in the army sense of the word
  • in — 'in' is the plainest way of saying something goes inside, and the company is what goes in.
  • half-cut — Cut in half, the village word keeps only one of its halves, and it is the back one that survives.

'Part company' is the phrase the surface wants and the parse cuts in two: 'Part' is the definition, 'company' the wordplay. Half-cut is British slang for tipsy.

Down

1 down SCHOOL

Principally, social categorisation has one overwhelming limitation – it’s divided by class (6)

Definition: divided by class · first letters

Pupils are split into classes, so the phrase describes the institution by how it organises its day rather than by naming it outright.

  • social categorisation has one overwhelming limitation → SCHOOL six words standing in a row, each giving up the letter it begins with
  • Principally — The principal part of a word is where it starts, so this asks for the opening letter of each word that follows.

The surface is sociology, where class means social rank; the definition means the classroom sort. A principal runs one of these, which is the setter winking at his own indicator.

2 down BOARDS

Chief briefly interrupted by a doctor turning tables (6)

Definition: tables · container + deletion + reversal

A board is a plank and so the table made from one — 'board and lodging' still means the meals taken at somebody's table.

  • Chief → BOS the chief of a firm is the boss, docked here to three letters
  • a → A the article is taken at face value, as a single letter
  • doctor → RD a doctor is DR in the standard abbreviation, and it arrives the other way round
  • briefly — Said briefly, a word is cut short, and here that means losing the last of its four letters.
  • interrupted by — An interruption arrives in the middle of what is being said, so the new letters land inside the word rather than after it.
  • turning — Something turning ends up facing the other way, so the abbreviation is written from its last letter to its first.

'Turning tables' reads as one phrase and does two separate jobs: the doctor is what turns, and the tables are the definition sitting quietly at the end.

3 down ESSENTIALS

French milk served up in German city’s just what we need (10)

Definition: just what we need · container + reversal

Essentials are the things you cannot do without, so 'just what we need' names them by the test they have to pass.

  • German city → ESSEN Essen is a city in the Ruhr, a setter's favourite for its vowels
  • French milk → TIAL milk in French is lait, and this entry runs down the grid, so it is set out from the bottom
  • ’s → S the possessive tacks its own letter onto the city
  • served up — Serving something up presents it the other way about, and 'up' is the direction a down entry reverses in.
  • in — 'in' is the plainest containment instruction there is: the reversed word goes inside the city, not next to it.

The apostrophe-s does two jobs at once: it is the final letter of the assembly and the 'is' joining wordplay to definition. Setters allow themselves that double duty.

4 down TUTOR

Instructor’s express disapproval with soldiers (5)

Definition: Instructor · charade

A tutor instructs one pupil or a small group — the private or university teacher, rather than anyone standing in front of a class.

  • express disapproval → TUT the noise you make to show you disapprove is written tut
  • soldiers → OR OR is 'other ranks', the British Army's term for soldiers who are not officers

Two of the plainest conventions there are, laid end to end. The soldiers one is worth owning: OR turns up whenever a clue mentions men or ranks.

5 down HUMILIATE

Smell I left before I ate is cause to feel bad (9)

Definition: cause to feel bad · charade

To humiliate is to make somebody feel small in front of others: 'cause to feel bad' with the particular sting of wounded pride.

  • Smell → HUM in British usage something that hums smells, and not pleasantly
  • I left → IL the setter's I taken at face value, then L, the standard abbreviation for left
  • I ate → IATE the I comes round a second time and 'ate' contributes its own three letters
  • before — 'before' fixes the order of the pieces: what is named first is written first, and the eating comes last.

Five short pieces in a row, and the same letter I is clued twice — setters count each appearance separately, so spotting one does not mean you have both.

6 down NOTE

N American, African, Asian, Oceanian or even from the Antarctic! (4)

Definition: N American, African, Asian, Oceanian or even from the Antarctic! · cryptic definition

The clue lists where paper money is issued, and the last item is the joke: the one continent without a country of its own.

  • N American, African, Asian, Oceanian → read as nationalities this is a list of people, which is the reading the surface is pushing
  • or even from the Antarctic! → the continent with no nation of its own still has collectors' paper money, and the exclamation mark is the setter enjoying that

There is no separable wordplay here — the whole sentence is the definition. Read the adjectives as currencies rather than as peoples and the answer is paper money.

7 down UNBIASED

Open-minded nude American Bishop is sacked (8)

Definition: Open-minded · anagram

Somebody unbiased leans neither way, which is what open-minded describes: willing to weigh a case without arriving with the verdict.

  • nude → NUDE the word is here for its four letters
  • American → A A is the standard abbreviation for American
  • Bishop → B B is the bishop in chess notation
  • is → IS the little verb is fodder too, contributing two more letters
  • sacked — A sacked city has been ransacked and left in disarray, and letters in disarray are letters to be put in a new order.

The word 'is' looks like joinery and is really material. Setters count every letter offered, and a two-letter word is the easiest one to leave out of the count.

8 down SPECTATE

Watch Spooner’s electronics series? (8)

Definition: Watch · spoonerism

To spectate is to watch from the side rather than take part — the verb built backwards out of 'spectator'.

  • electronics → SPEC electronics is tech, and it gives its opening sound away in exchange for an SP
  • series → TATE a spate of things is a run of them one after another, and it collects the T that tech gave up
  • Spooner’s — The Reverend Spooner traded the opening sounds of two words, so naming him is the instruction to swap them over.

The question mark admits the looseness: tech for electronics and a spate for a series are both everyday shorthand rather than dictionary equivalents.

13 down PENCIL-CASE

Nice places being arranged – somewhere to accommodate writers (6-4)

Definition: somewhere to accommodate writers · anagram

The thing accommodates pens and pencils, and 'writers' is the setter's word for the implements that do the writing.

  • Nice places → NICEPLACES the two words are here for their ten letters
  • being arranged — Things being arranged are being put into a new order, which is exactly what happens to these ten letters.

'Nice places' wants to be the French Riviera, and the capital letter is fair because the phrase opens the clue. The writers are implements, not people.

15 down PRIMARIES

1Ds for young, strait-laced group of stars (9)

Definition: 1Ds for young · charade

1 down's answer is SCHOOL, and in Britain primaries are the schools that take children up to about eleven.

  • strait-laced → PRIM prim means stiffly proper and easily shocked
  • group of stars → ARIES Aries the ram is a constellation, the first sign of the zodiac

The definition points at another clue: read '1Ds' as the answer to 1 down, in the plural. Cross-references like this are always to the solved word, never to the clue.

16 down DINGIEST

Gin diet’s crazy – and most dull! (8)

Definition: most dull · anagram

Dingy means grimy and gloomy, so the dingiest room is the drabbest — dull in the sense of dim and colourless, not boring.

  • Gin diet’s → GINDIETS the letters of both words, apostrophe and all, are the material
  • crazy — What is crazy has gone out of its right order, which is the instruction to put these letters into a different one.

The dash marks the join: 'crazy' finishes the wordplay and 'most dull' begins the definition. Guardian setters use it where a link word would otherwise go.

17 down ATTITUDE

Mood elevation when drinking Tango, putting away litres! (8)

Definition: Mood · container + deletion

An attitude is the stance somebody takes towards things, so 'mood' fits as the settled frame of mind rather than a passing feeling.

  • elevation → ALTITUDE elevation is height above sea level
  • Tango → T Tango is T in the NATO alphabet
  • litres → L l is the symbol for litre, and this is the letter taken off
  • when drinking — To drink something is to take it inside you, so the letter goes within the word that swallows it.
  • putting away — Putting something away is stowing it out of sight, so the letter it names leaves the word altogether.

Tango is a British fizzy orange drink as well as the NATO alphabet's T, which is what lets the surface read as a night out rather than a spelling lesson.

19 down DONGLE

It may be carrying files from the French supporting Eastern currency (6)

Definition: It may be carrying files · charade

A dongle is the small stick that plugs into a computer to carry files or unlock software, so the whole phrase describes one by what it does.

  • Eastern currency → DONG the dong is the currency of Vietnam
  • the French → LE 'le' is one of the French words for 'the'
  • supporting — A support sits underneath what it holds up, and underneath in a down entry means later, so this piece goes at the bottom.

Foreign-word clues like this one lean on schoolroom French, and the direction word is doing real work: in a down entry, 'supporting' means below and so later.

20 down ASCENT

State agreement for rise (6)

Definition: rise · homophone

An ascent is a climb upwards and 'rise' names that same movement as a noun: the going up, rather than the thing that goes up.

  • agreement → ASCENT agreement given is assent, spelt with a double S and sounding identical to the climb
  • State — To state something is to say it out loud, so what gets written down is whatever the spoken word sounds like.

Two spellings, one sound. Only the crossing letters tell you which one the grid wants, which is why homophone clues are worth leaving until a few crossers are in.

23 down TENSE

Disorganised teens stressed (5)

Definition: stressed · anagram

Somebody tense is strung tight and on edge, which is what 'stressed' describes — the adjective for the person, not the verb for emphasis.

  • teens → TEENS the five letters offered are the whole of the material
  • Disorganised — What is disorganised has lost the arrangement it came in, so the word beside it is to be put into another.

Three words, and the only work is deciding which two belong together. 'Disorganised teens' is the wordplay; the last word alone is the definition.

24 down FAIL

Antifa illegally concealing collapse (4)

Definition: collapse · hidden word

Collapse here means coming to nothing — a scheme that collapses has not fallen over, it has simply not worked.

  • Antifa illegally → FAIL the letters run consecutively across the join between the two words
  • concealing — To conceal something is to hide it inside something else, so the letters are already there in order, not waiting to be shuffled.

Hidden words hide because the surface is doing all the shouting: two political words in a row, with the answer lying flat across the gap between them.
