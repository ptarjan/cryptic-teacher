Across

1 across PHARMACY

Drug supplier quick to hide damage (8)

Definition: Drug supplier · container

'Drug supplier' → PHARMACY: the shop licensed to dispense medicines, so it supplies drugs in the legal, medical sense rather than the criminal one.

  • quick → PACY 'pacy' is the adjective for something that moves at a fast pace
  • damage → HARM plain synonym: the damage done to a person is the harm done to them
  • to hide — Hiding a thing means putting it out of sight inside something bigger, and that is where these letters are meant to go.

A dealer moving fast to cover up the injury they have caused.

The surface sells you a criminal dealer concealing an injury, while the supplier wanted is the legitimate shop on the high street. 'Quick' has to become the less obvious synonym before there is room for anything inside it.

5 across LUSHES

Boozers: quite attractive, some say (6)

Definition: Boozers · homophone

'Boozers' → LUSHES: a lush is a habitual heavy drinker, and the plural noun answers the plural noun in the clue.

  • quite attractive → LUSHES 'luscious' means richly attractive, and spoken at speed its ending flattens into a plural noise written quite differently
  • some say — 'Say' points at speech, and the phrase tells you to trust how a word sounds in some mouths rather than how it is spelt.

A verdict passed on the pub regulars: fairly good-looking, if you ask around.

Say 'luscious' quickly and the whole join appears — the compliment in the surface and the insult in the definition are the same sound.

9 across CHECKS IN

Registers offence after attack on king? (6,2)

Definition: Registers · charade

'Registers' → CHECKS IN: arriving at a hotel desk or airport counter and being entered on the list is exactly registering your arrival.

  • attack on king? → CHECK in chess, an attack that puts the king in danger is announced with this one word
  • offence → SIN an offence against a moral or religious code
  • after — 'After' places the thing named before it at the back, so the offence follows the other piece instead of leading.

Someone's crime is recorded in the wake of an attack on the king.

'King' pushes you towards royalty when the answer wants chess, and the question mark is the setter admitting that the attack named is only one particular kind.

10 across SPACER

Fight about church – it will create division (6)

Definition: it will create division · container

'it will create division' → SPACER: the washer or block put between two parts to keep a gap open, so it makes a division.

  • Fight → SPAR to spar is to fight, especially the trial kind boxers do in training
  • church → CE the Church of England, shortened the way a setter always shortens it
  • about — 'About' means round the outside of, so whatever it introduces is wrapped up inside the other piece.

A row over the church that ends up splitting the parish.

'Division' is the trap: the surface offers a congregation breaking into factions, and the answer is a small piece of hardware that holds two parts apart.

12 across LOSES

Finishes down a hundred and suffers defeat (5)

Definition: suffers defeat · deletion

'suffers defeat' → LOSES: to lose is precisely to be beaten, and both are third-person singular verbs, so one swaps straight in for the other.

  • Finishes → CLOSES to close a thing is to finish it, and the six letters arrive whole before anything happens to them
  • a hundred → C the Roman numeral for a hundred, and it is the letter that goes
  • down — 'Down' is the till-counting sense, short by a stated amount, so the amount named next is missing from what you started with.

A trader ends the day a hundred short and takes a beating.

Roman numerals are the setter's standing shorthand for numbers — C for a hundred, M for a thousand — and 'down' is the only word telling you this one leaves rather than joins.

13 across LASER BEAM

Something that might cut amber seal apart (5,4)

Definition: Something that might cut · anagram

'Something that might cut' → LASER BEAM: focused laser light is used industrially to slice metal and cloth, so it is a genuine cutter.

  • amber seal → AMBERSEAL anagram fodder — nine letters to be re-ordered
  • apart — 'Apart' means in separate pieces, and once letters are in pieces the order they were handed over in no longer holds.

A tool for prising open a seal made of amber.

'Apart' sits at the end of the sentence where a solver expects the definition, and the vague 'Something that might cut' sits at the front where the fodder usually starts.

14 across FIT AS A FIDDLE

Excited if data field’s in excellent condition (3,2,1,6)

Definition: in excellent condition · anagram

'in excellent condition' → FIT AS A FIDDLE: the old simile takes a well-tuned violin as its picture of perfect health and repair.

  • if data field’s → IFDATAFIELDS anagram fodder — twelve letters, counting the S of the apostrophe-s
  • Excited — 'Excited' describes something stirred up and jumping about, and letters in that state will not stay in the order they were given.

Someone delighted that a database field is working perfectly.

The apostrophe-s belongs to the fodder rather than to the grammar, and the letters only reach twelve once you count it — the sort of counting a long anagram lives on.

18 across MOUNTAINEERS

They hope to conquer extremely attritional places for example (12)

Definition: They hope to conquer extremely attritional places for example · cryptic definition + outer letters

The clue is one long definition: people who hope to conquer the Alps, and by extension any mountains, are MOUNTAINEERS.

  • attritional places → the outer pair of each of these two words, read in order, spells ALPS
  • They hope to conquer → the surface offers an army taking ground; the conquest meant is a climb to a summit
  • for example → a warning that what the letters spell is one instance of the terrain, not the definition itself
  • extremely — 'Extremely' points at the extremes of a word — the letters standing at its two far ends — rather than at anything in between.

A bleak assessment of who is trying to win some brutally wearing ground.

ALPS is an extra rather than the whole: the letters name one range, and 'for example' asks you to widen that to mountains in general before the definition lands.

21 across SIGHTSEER

Tourist puts forecaster on spot (9)

Definition: Tourist · charade

'Tourist' → SIGHTSEER: a sightseer is a visitor going round the sights, which is what most tourists have come to do.

  • spot → SIGHT to spot something is to sight it — to catch it with the eye
  • forecaster → SEER a seer foretells what is coming, the old word for a prophet
  • on — In an across entry, X 'on' Y is read as Y then X, the way the pieces would stack if the row were stood upright.

A tourist putting the weather forecaster on the spot with an awkward question.

'Put on the spot' is a single idiom in the surface, and the clue needs it prised into separate pieces, one of them the ordinary verb 'spot'.

23 across V AND A

Institution displaying objects from both ends of Venezuela (1,3,1)

Definition: Institution displaying objects · charade + outer letters

'Institution displaying objects' → the V AND A: the Victoria and Albert Museum in London, the national collection of art, design and decorative objects.

  • Venezuela → V the opening letter of the country's name
  • both → AND 'both' is one plus the other, and the enumeration shows that the joining word is spelt out in the middle
  • Venezuela → A the closing letter of the same name
  • ends — 'Ends' asks for the letters standing at the two edges of a word, the first and the last, with nothing from between them.

A gallery showing exhibits gathered from every corner of Venezuela.

The enumeration (1,3,1) is doing the teaching here: it tells you the ampersand in the museum's usual shorthand has to be written out as a word.

24 across I DID IT

Confess to creating two sets of papers with information technology (1,3,2)

Definition: Confess · charade

'Confess' → I DID IT: the phrase is the confession itself, the three words a guilty party says when admitting responsibility.

  • two sets of papers → IDID papers here are identity documents, and 'two sets' asks for that pair of letters written out twice
  • information technology → IT the standard short form of information technology, as in an IT department

An admission of having produced two sets of paperwork with the help of computers.

'Papers' means identity documents rather than newspapers, and the enumeration cuts the six letters in a completely different place from the wordplay, which is what hides the join.

25 across VIN ROUGE

Cook our veg in French wine (3,5)

Definition: French wine · anagram

'French wine' → VIN ROUGE: literally 'red wine' in French, the phrase an English speaker borrows for an everyday bottle of red.

  • our veg in → OURVEGIN anagram fodder — eight letters to be re-ordered
  • Cook — Cooking works ingredients into something with a new shape, which is why a setter uses it for letters that come out arranged differently.

A recipe instruction: cook our vegetables in wine from France.

'in' is fodder rather than a preposition, and that is the hardest letter-pair in the clue to see, because the surface needs the word as ordinary grammar.

26 across GUESTS

Speculate about time invitees turn up (6)

Definition: invitees · container

'invitees' → GUESTS: guests are exactly the people who have been invited, and both are plural nouns for the same set.

  • Speculate → GUESS to speculate about something is to guess at it
  • time → T the standard single-letter shorthand for time, as in a physics formula
  • turn up → surface only — it paints the invited crowd arriving and hands over no letters
  • about — 'About' means encircling, so the letter it introduces is planted inside the other word rather than tacked on the end.

Wondering aloud what time the invited crowd will arrive.

'Speculate about' reads as one phrase in the surface and has to be split, with 'about' switching from a preposition into an instruction.

27 across ET CETERA

Early telegraph cables initially offering short version of this (2,6)

Definition: this · first letters + deletion

'this' → ET CETERA: the clue points at its own answer, saying ETC abbreviates it, and ET CETERA is Latin for 'and the rest'.

  • Early telegraph cables → ETC the opening letter of each of the three words, taken in the order they come
  • initially — 'Initially' asks for what a word starts with, so each of the words before it gives up only its opening letter.
  • short version of — 'Short version of' runs an abbreviation backwards: the letters just made are the clipped form, and the phrase behind them at full length is what goes in the grid.

Early telegraph wires could at first send only a shortened version of a message.

The wordplay builds a short form and leaves you to supply the phrase behind it — the Latin that ETC has always stood for, spelt out in full.

Down

1 down PICKLE

Papa goes over child’s little difficulty (6)

Definition: difficulty · charade

'difficulty' → PICKLE: to be in a pickle is idiomatic English for being in an awkward fix, so the noun means a predicament.

  • Papa → P Papa is the radio alphabet's word for this letter
  • child’s little → ICKLE 'ickle' is nursery talk — how a small child says the word little
  • goes over — In a down entry, being over something means sitting higher up the column, so the piece named first ends up on top.

A father bending over his small child's minor trouble.

Radio alphabets are a setter's standby for single letters — Papa, Oscar, Tango — and 'ickle' comes from the nursery, so both halves ask you to hear a register rather than a meaning.

2 down AGEISM

Some language is magnificent, showing discrimination (6)

Definition: discrimination · hidden word

'discrimination' is the wide category and AGEISM one variety of it, so the definition is deliberately broader than the answer.

  • language is magnificent → AGEISM six consecutive letters, running from inside the first word, across the whole of the second and into the third
  • Some — 'Some' asks for a part of what follows rather than all of it, which is how a setter points at letters lying inside the words.

Someone praising a particular tongue as a splendid thing.

'Some' is doing the work: it asks for a portion of what follows instead of describing it, and the praise in the surface keeps your eye on the meaning rather than on where one word ends and the next begins.

3 down MAKESHIFT

Force to move- it’s only temporary (9)

Definition: only temporary · charade

'only temporary' → MAKESHIFT: a makeshift arrangement is improvised to serve until the real thing arrives, so it is temporary by design.

  • Force → MAKE to force someone into doing a thing is to make them do it
  • to move → SHIFT shift is the everyday verb for moving something out of the way

An order to get something out of the way, just for now.

'Force' reads as a noun in the surface — a police force, a physical force — and the wordplay needs the verb instead.

4 down CHILL CABINET

Relaxed government unit will keep things fresh (5,7)

Definition: will keep things fresh · charade

'will keep things fresh' → CHILL CABINET: the refrigerated open display case in a British supermarket, whose whole purpose is keeping food cold.

  • Relaxed → CHILL 'chill' is the informal adjective for laid-back and unbothered
  • government unit → CABINET the committee of senior ministers that runs a British government

A laid-back government department that promises to bring in new ideas.

Both halves hide in plain sight as politics, and they only turn into shop fittings once you stop reading 'fresh' as 'new'.

6 down UPPER

Superior but essentially stupid experts (5)

Definition: Superior · charade + middle letters

'Superior' → UPPER: higher in position or rank — an upper floor, the upper hand — rather than better in quality.

  • stupid → UP the two letters sitting at the dead centre of this six-letter word
  • experts → PER the three letters at the centre of this seven-letter word, in the order they stand
  • essentially — 'Essentially' means at the essence, the heart of a thing, so it asks for the letters in the centre of a word and not those at its edges.

A dismissal of some specialists as basically dim, however grand they look.

'Essentially' is doing positional work rather than describing anybody: the setter's sense is 'at the very heart of', which points into words instead of at them.

7 down HACIENDA

Criminal had a nice big property in Mexico (8)

Definition: big property in Mexico · anagram

'big property in Mexico' → HACIENDA: the Spanish word for a large estate and its main house, used throughout Mexico and Latin America.

  • had a nice → HADANICE anagram fodder — the eight letters to be re-ordered
  • Criminal — 'Criminal' describes something crooked and out of order, and letters that are out of order are letters you may rearrange.

A crook turns out to own a handsome large house in Mexico.

'Criminal' reads as the noun the sentence is about, which is exactly what stops it looking like an instruction; the fodder is sitting in plain sight right after it.

8 down STRIMMER

Son, in better shape, that’s useful around the garden (8)

Definition: that’s useful around the garden · charade

'that’s useful around the garden' → STRIMMER: the British name for a string trimmer, the whirling-nylon-line tool that cuts grass along edges.

  • Son → S the standard abbreviation for son, as used in family trees and name lists
  • in better shape → TRIMMER someone trim is fit and neat, so being in better shape makes them trimmer

A son who has got fitter and now earns his keep outdoors.

The comma after 'Son' makes it look like someone being addressed, which hides the fact that the word is worth a single letter.

11 down ESCAPE ARTIST

One struggling to become free professionally? (6,6)

Definition: One struggling to become free professionally? · cryptic definition

The whole clue is the definition: an ESCAPE ARTIST is the performer whose act is getting out of chains and locked boxes for a living.

  • One struggling to become free → read straight, this is a prisoner or a debtor fighting their way out of a bind
  • professionally → the word that turns the struggle into a paid act: getting loose is the performance, not a misfortune

A worker fighting to break out of some professional entanglement — a contract, perhaps, or a job.

Struggling to get free is a predicament for most people and a living for a very few, and the question mark warns that the whole sentence is the definition, with no wordplay hiding inside it.

15 down IN REVERSE

Home defeat means we’re going backwards (2,7)

Definition: going backwards · charade

'going backwards' → IN REVERSE: a car in reverse travels backwards, and the phrase covers any motion run the wrong way.

  • Home → IN someone at home is in — the plainest sense of the word
  • defeat → REVERSE a defeat is a turn of fortune against you, the noun sporting and military reports prefer

A home loss in the league, and a season sliding the wrong way.

'Home defeat' is one idea to a football fan and two separate words to the wordplay, which is where the clue gets its grip.

16 down SMASHING

Tremendous destruction (8)

Definition: Tremendous · double definition

'Tremendous' is the informal British 'excellent'; 'destruction' is the literal breaking of things. SMASHING covers both, one as praise and one as a plain verbal noun.

  • Tremendous → informal British praise: first-rate, wonderful, a word for good weather and good news
  • destruction → the act of breaking things up, taken as a noun

A report of wreckage on an impressive scale.

'Tremendous' is the British informal use — smashing weather, a smashing time — which sits a long way from the wreckage the other half of the clue means.

17 down QUAGMIRE

Swamp with lots of paper about company meeting (8)

Definition: Swamp · container

'Swamp' → QUAGMIRE: soft waterlogged ground you sink into, which is one kind of swamp, and the word doubles for any hopeless mess.

  • lots of paper → QUIRE a quire is twenty-four sheets of paper, a stationer's measure
  • company meeting → AGM the annual general meeting a company holds for its shareholders, always written as three letters
  • about — 'About' means round the outside, so the piece it introduces is wrapped in the other rather than added on the end.

An office drowning in paperwork ahead of the shareholders' annual meeting.

'Swamp' reads as a verb in the surface — to be buried under paperwork — and the definition needs the noun instead, a patch of boggy ground.

19 down ENDURE

Stick, broken, reunited without it (6)

Definition: Stick · anagram + deletion

'Stick' → ENDURE: to stick something is to put up with it — 'I can't stick him' — so the verb means to bear.

  • reunited → REUNITED the word is handed over whole first, all eight letters of it
  • without it → REUNED 'it' names an I and a T, and both come out, leaving six letters to shuffle
  • broken — Something broken has come apart into pieces, and letters in pieces can be put back in a different order.
  • without — 'Without' here means lacking rather than outside — the letters it names are absent from what gets shuffled.

A snapped stick, glued back together with one bit missing.

'Stick' looks like the noun the surface is built on and is really the definition, in the sense of sticking something out. The deletion has to happen before the shuffle.

20 down CAMERA

Motor includes compiler and a recording device (6)

Definition: recording device · container + charade

'recording device' → CAMERA: a camera records whatever is in front of it onto film or a sensor, which is recording in the plainest sense.

  • Motor → CAR a motor is a car in everyday British speech
  • compiler → ME the compiler is the person who built the crossword, so the word wanted is what they call themself
  • a → A the letter is taken straight off the page
  • includes — 'Includes' says one thing contains another, so the piece named next goes inside rather than alongside.

A car with a compiler and a recording device inside it.

'A recording device' looks like one phrase and has to be cut in two, with the 'a' doing wordplay and only the last two words defining. 'Compiler' is the setter's standard way of pointing at themself.

22 down TAINT

Disgrace adding AI to explosive material (5)

Definition: Disgrace · container

'Disgrace' → TAINT: a taint is the mark of disgrace that clings to a name, both being nouns for a stain on a reputation.

  • explosive material → TNT the standard shorthand for trinitrotoluene, the explosive
  • AI → AI the two letters are lifted straight off the page
  • adding — Adding one thing to another normally just joins them, and the setter is stretching it here to mean putting one inside the other.

A scandal about putting artificial intelligence into weapons.

Setters use 'AI' both ways — as the initials of artificial intelligence and as two bare letters — and here nothing at all is translated.
