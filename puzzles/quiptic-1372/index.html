Across

1 across SHAVER

What leaves one exhibiting bare-faced cheek? (6)

Definition: What leaves one exhibiting bare-faced cheek? · cryptic definition

A shaver is a razor, and the thing a razor leaves is a smooth face, so the whole clue is a description of what one does.

  • What leaves one exhibiting → The surface asks what would give a person the nerve to behave like that, so 'leaves' reads as causes and 'one' as a human being.
  • bare-faced cheek → The idiom for brazen impudence has been pulled back into its literal parts: a face with nothing growing on it, and the side of that face.

What on earth would give somebody the nerve to be quite that brazen?

There are no letters to find here. A shaver is a razor — the sense kept alive by 'electric shaver' — and what a razor leaves behind is a cheek with no stubble on it.

4 across GAFFES

Blunders made by bosses having run away (6)

Definition: Blunders · deletion

Blunders → GAFFES: a gaffe is the social sort of blunder, the remark everybody hears and politely ignores.

  • bosses → GAFFERS A gaffer is a boss in British working slang — the foreman on a site, or the chief electrician on a film crew
  • run → R R is the cricket scorebook's abbreviation for a run, and it is the letter that goes
  • away — What has gone away is no longer there, so the letter named beside it never makes it into the word.

The bosses have fled, and their mistakes are all that is left of them.

'Having run away' does double duty: it paints the bosses making a getaway and it issues an instruction. Cricket's R is one of the letters a British setter can drop in unannounced.

9 across GAME SET AND MATCH

Summing-up after court battle (4,3,3,5)

Definition: Summing-up after court battle · cryptic definition

The umpire's call totals up a completed tennis match, so the phrase really is a summing-up delivered after a battle fought on a court.

  • Summing-up → In the surface this is counsel's closing address to the jury, the speech that gathers a whole trial together.
  • after court battle → The court the setter has in mind has a net across the middle of it, and the battle is a contest between two players.

A barrister's closing address at the end of a long and bitter trial.

Nothing here comes apart into letters. 'Game, set and match' is the umpire's formula, called when the last point of a tennis match has been taken, and 'court' is the only word that has to switch meaning.

10 across RUSSIA

Country, reportedly one that moves swiftly (6)

Definition: Country · homophone

Country → RUSSIA: the plainest relation there is, a category naming one of its members, which is why the whole difficulty of the clue sits in the sound.

  • one that moves swiftly → RUSSIA Somebody who rushes is a rusher, and spoken aloud that is what you write in the grid
  • reportedly — Something reported has been said out loud, so the clue is handing you a sound and leaving the spelling to you.

A nation gets a mention in the same breath as somebody in a tearing hurry.

The match works in a non-rhotic British accent, where the final -er of 'rusher' flattens to the same unstressed vowel that ends RUSSIA. Homophone clues in these papers are calibrated to that accent.

11 across THE ASHES

Seethe as Hesketh secures trophy (3,5)

Definition: trophy · hidden word

trophy → the urn two national cricket teams compete for, so the definition names the class and the answer supplies one very famous member.

  • Seethe as Hesketh → THEASHES The letters run consecutively through these three words, starting three letters into the first
  • secures — To secure is to hold something fast, and what these two names are holding fast is a run of consecutive letters.

Hesketh wins a cup while somebody else quietly boils with rage.

The Ashes is the tiny urn England and Australia have played Test cricket for since an 1882 newspaper printed a mock obituary of English cricket. Hesketh is there only to supply letters.

12 across REAPPEAR

To be published again, most of papers are amended (8)

Definition: To be published again · anagram + deletion

To be published again → REAPPEAR: for a book or an issue, to appear is to come out in print, so appearing again is being published again.

  • most of papers → PAPER PAPERS gives up its last letter, so five of its six letters go into the fodder
  • are → ARE The word itself joins the fodder, three more letters taken as written
  • amended → anagram indicator
  • most of — Most is not all, so the word hands over everything but its final letter.
  • amended — To amend is to alter, and altering a set of letters means writing them down in a different order.

A newspaper's correction notice: most of the papers are being amended.

'Papers are' reads as the subject of an ordinary sentence and is really a heap of eight letters. The only sign that they are fodder is 'amended' sitting immediately after them.

14 across SWANEE

River bird with wings of eagle (6)

Definition: River · charade + outer letters

River → SWANEE: a definition by example, the general word standing in for one particular waterway that happens to fit.

  • bird → SWAN The bird is a swan, taken whole with nothing done to it
  • wings of eagle → EE EAGLE gives up the letter at each of its wingtips
  • wings of — Wings are what stick out at either side, so the word surrenders its first and last letters and nothing in between.

A river bird that has somehow borrowed an eagle's wings.

The Swanee is the Florida river of Stephen Foster's 'Old Folks at Home', which spells it a syllable shorter than the map does — and that song spelling is the one crosswords use.

15 across SPRING

Economical, refusing a rise (6)

Definition: rise · deletion

rise → SPRING: to spring is to rise suddenly, the sense in 'spring to one's feet', and both are plain verbs in the same form.

  • Economical → SPARING Sparing means economical, careful with however much there is to use
  • a → A The single letter A is what gets refused, lifted out of the middle
  • refusing — To refuse a thing is to decline to take it, so the letter offered next is left out of the word.

Somebody thrifty is turning down the offer of a pay rise.

'A rise' reads as one thing, the increase you ask your boss for, and the clue needs it in two: the A is what gets refused and the rise is the definition.

18 across COALFACE

Site of mining company with a large clock (8)

Definition: Site of mining · charade

Site of mining → COALFACE: the exposed seam a miner actually cuts at, which is why 'at the coalface' now means wherever the real work happens.

  • company → CO Co is the abbreviation printed on a firm's letterhead
  • a → A The clue's own 'a' is taken as a single letter
  • large → L L is the size label's abbreviation for large
  • clock → FACE The face is the part of a clock you read, and in British slang a person's clock is their face

A mining company's premises, complete with a big clock over the gate.

Reading 'a large clock' as one object is what hides three separate pieces of wordplay inside it. The clue never signals a join, so the only way in is to try the words singly.

21 across HONOLULU

Hard old Scottish singer nets working capital (8)

Definition: capital · charade + container

capital → HONOLULU: the seat of government of a US state, Hawaii's, so the definition names the class and the answer supplies the city.

  • Hard → H H is the grade stamped on a hard pencil
  • working → ON A machine that is working is on
  • old → O O is the standard abbreviation for old
  • Scottish singer → LULU Lulu is the Glasgow-born singer of 'Shout' and 'Boom Bang-a-Bang', a household name in Britain since the 1960s
  • nets — To net a thing is to catch it inside your net, so those letters end up trapped within the pieces already assembled.

A tough old Scottish singer is bringing in working capital.

'Working capital' is the accountant's phrase the surface leans on, and the clue cuts it down the middle: the first word is wordplay and the second is the definition.

22 across TANDEM

Bicycle stand emptied, partly (6)

Definition: Bicycle · hidden word

Bicycle → TANDEM: a bicycle built for two, so the definition is the general word and the answer one particular kind of it.

  • stand emptied → TANDEM The six letters lie consecutively across the join between these two words
  • partly — 'Partly' says you want only some of what is written, and the part wanted is an unbroken run of letters.

A bike rack has been cleared out, or most of it has.

'Emptied' is the trap. It looks like an instruction to hollow a word out, and it is fodder — one of the two words the letters run through — with 'partly' the only instruction here.

24 across OF NO CONSEQUENCE

From midday, series involving Charlie Petty? (2,2,11)

Definition: Petty · charade + container

Petty → OF NO CONSEQUENCE: calling a matter petty says it is trivial and not worth attention, which is what having no consequence amounts to.

  • From → OF From and of both mark origin — made from oak, made of oak
  • midday → NOON Midday is noon, four letters that are about to be split open
  • Charlie → C Charlie is C in the NATO phonetic alphabet
  • series → SEQUENCE A sequence is a series, one thing following another
  • involving — To involve something is to wrap it up in what you are doing, so the letter it introduces is taken inside the word before it.

From noon onwards, a television series featuring somebody called Charlie Petty.

'Charlie Petty' reads as a person's name, and the two words sit on opposite sides of the clue: Charlie is a single letter of wordplay, Petty the definition.

25 across RASHER

More reckless swine, to a tiny extent (6)

Definition: More reckless · double definition

More reckless → the comparative of rash; swine to a tiny extent → a rasher, the thin slice of bacon that is about as little pig as you can have.

  • More reckless → The comparative of rash: one degree further into heedlessness than the last person.
  • swine, to a tiny extent → A pig reduced to a single thin slice, the sort you lay in a frying pan.

A pig behaving rather more recklessly than it usually does.

Both readings hang on the same -ER ending, and only one of them is grammar: the reckless sense really is 'more rash', while the breakfast sense is a plain noun that happens to end the same way.

26 across BEDSIT

Took effect outside editor’s living room? (6)

Definition: living room? · container

living room? → BEDSIT: a single rented room that is bedroom and sitting room at once, so it is literally a room lived in.

  • Took effect → BIT Bit is the past tense of bite, and a thing that bites has taken effect — the frost bit, the medicine bit
  • editor’s → EDS Ed is the abbreviation for editor, and the possessive supplies the S
  • outside — Standing outside something means going round it, so the biting word opens up and the editor's letters go in.

Something began to bite just outside the editor's front room.

'Living room' is the trap: a bedsit is a room lived in rather than the room a house calls its lounge, and the question mark is the setter conceding the stretch.

Down

1 down STATURE

Eminence of Queen possessed by Venus de Milo? (7)

Definition: Eminence · container

Eminence → STATURE: both name the standing a person has come to hold in others' eyes, not physical height.

  • Venus de Milo? → STATUE The Venus de Milo is a famous marble statue, and the question mark admits that it is only one example of one
  • Queen → R R is for Regina, the Latin a British queen signs after her name
  • possessed by — What one thing possesses is held within it, so the letter belonging to the queen sits inside her owner.

The armless marble goddess is credited with a queen's grandeur.

R for Regina is worth owning outright: it follows the monarch's signature, and it is all any clue ever wants from a queen. The surface offers a whole goddess to distract from one letter.

2 down ABETS

Helps offender since being impaled with stake (5)

Definition: Helps offender · container

Helps offender → ABETS: to abet is not merely to help but to help somebody do wrong, which is why the definition carries the criminal with it.

  • since → AS 'Since' and 'as' both mean because, so one swaps straight in for the other
  • stake → BET A stake is money laid on an outcome, which is a bet
  • being impaled with — Impaling drives something through the middle of a thing, so the word is split open and the piercing letters lodge inside.

A criminal is being helped after somebody ran a wooden post through him.

The stake is money on a wager rather than a vampire-hunter's post, and 'since' is the causal one you could replace with 'as'. Both words have quietly changed sense between surface and wordplay.

3 down ELEVATE

Raise tax in European shelter (7)

Definition: Raise · charade + container

Raise → ELEVATE: both mean to lift a thing to a higher position or rank, one plain and one Latin-rooted.

  • European → E E is the standard abbreviation for European
  • shelter → LEE The lee of anything is its sheltered side, the one the wind does not reach
  • tax → VAT VAT is value added tax, the sales tax charged in Britain
  • in — Putting the tax 'in' the shelter means exactly that: those three letters go bodily inside, splitting the host word apart.

Taxes are being put up inside some European refuge.

'Raise' sits at the front of a down clue, which is exactly where a reversal instruction usually goes, and here it is the definition instead. The shelter is the nautical kind.

5 down ADDRESS

Speak to a groom about daughter (7)

Definition: Speak to · charade + container

Speak to → ADDRESS: to address someone is to direct words straight at them, the verb of addressing a meeting.

  • a → A The clue's own 'a' is taken as a single letter
  • daughter → D D is the genealogist's abbreviation for daughter
  • groom → DRESS To groom is to dress or smarten up, so the verb swaps for its synonym
  • about — 'About' asks for one piece to be wrapped round another, though the wrapping here is all on one side: the daughter lands at the front edge.

Somebody is having a quiet word with a groom on the subject of his daughter.

'Groom' is the verb, to dress or smarten, and not the man at the wedding or the one in the stable — both of whom the surface is happy for you to picture instead.

6 down FLAGSTAFF

One used to keep up standards? (9)

Definition: One used to keep up standards? · cryptic definition

One used to keep up standards? → FLAGSTAFF: a staff exists to hold a flag up, and a standard is a flag.

  • One used to keep up → In the surface this is a person who maintains quality; read literally it is an object whose job is to hold something aloft.
  • standards → A standard is a banner or ensign — the sense in the Royal Standard — so the word swings from levels of quality to pieces of cloth.

Somebody relied on to stop the rest of us letting things slide.

No letters come apart here. A flagstaff is the pole a flag flies from, and a standard is a flag, so the thing that keeps standards up is the pole holding them in the air.

7 down SECRETE

Hide case of sleaze on Mediterranean island (7)

Definition: Hide · charade + outer letters

Hide → SECRETE: the verb behind 'secret', meaning to stow something away where it will not be found, rather than the glandular sense.

  • case of sleaze → SE SLEAZE keeps only its shell, the first letter and the last
  • Mediterranean island → CRETE Crete is the largest of the Greek islands, well out in the eastern Mediterranean
  • case of — A case is what encloses a thing, so the word gives up its casing — the outside letters — and nothing from within.
  • on — In a down clue, one piece sitting on another rests on top of it, which fixes these letters above the island rather than beside it.

A dossier of scandal is being buried somewhere on a Greek island.

'On' carries real information in a down clue: the piece before it goes above the piece after it, and that is the order the letters take. Across, the same word would only mean 'next to'.

8 down MATTER

Mother embraces tense affair (6)

Definition: affair · container

affair → MATTER: an affair in the sense of a business or concern, as in 'a serious matter', not the romantic sense the surface offers.

  • Mother → MATER Mater is Latin for mother, the word British public schools kept in circulation
  • tense → T T is the grammarian's abbreviation for tense
  • embraces — To embrace is to close your arms round something, so the mother's word shuts over the letter and holds it inside.

A mother wraps her arms around a fraught love affair.

'Tense' is grammar rather than nerves, and MATER is a crossword regular worth learning — Latin for mother, and the reason so many clues about mothers are really about three or five letters.

13 down POISONOUS

Toxic boy, devout externally, pinches ring (9)

Definition: Toxic · container

Toxic → POISONOUS: the everyday word for something that will harm you if swallowed, where 'toxic' is its technical twin.

  • devout → PIOUS Pious means devout, and it is the word that goes round the outside
  • boy → SON A boy of the family is a son, three letters dropped into the middle
  • ring → O A ring is a circle, and O is the crossword's circle
  • externally — 'Externally' puts the devout word on the outside, wrapping the boy rather than being laid end to end with him.
  • pinches — To pinch is to nick, and what the assembly nicks it keeps inside itself.

A wicked boy who looks holy from the outside has stolen some jewellery.

'Toxic boy' reads as a single insult, and the two words belong to opposite halves of the clue: the first is the definition, the second is three letters of wordplay.

16 down PROFFER

Tender academic intensely passionate? Not half (7)

Definition: Tender · charade + deletion

Tender → PROFFER: to tender is to hold something out for acceptance — a resignation, a bid — which is what proffering is.

  • academic → PROF A prof is an academic, the everyday short form of professor
  • intensely passionate → FER FERVID means intensely passionate, and three of its six letters survive the halving
  • Not half — Refusing half of a word leaves the other half standing, and here it is the front half that survives.

An academic is being asked just how deeply passionate they really are.

'Tender' is the verb, to put something forward, and not the adjective about softness that the passionate surface points you at. Which half 'not half' keeps is the setter's choice.

17 down GLUCOSE

Compound clues go astray (7)

Definition: Compound · anagram

Compound → GLUCOSE: a compound is a substance made of more than one element, and this is sugar under its chemical name rather than its kitchen one.

  • clues go → CLUESGO anagram fodder, seven letters
  • astray → anagram indicator
  • astray — Anything that goes astray has wandered off the path it was set on, and these letters wander out of the order they were given in.

A puzzle in which the clues themselves have wandered off course.

'Compound' looks like an instruction to put pieces together, which is what a charade would want from it, and it is the definition — the chemist's noun, not the verb.

18 down COURSE

Announced vulgar race (6)

Definition: race · homophone

race → COURSE: a course is a single run or chase, the sense kept alive in coursing, where each pursuit after a hare is one course.

  • vulgar → COURSE Coarse means vulgar, and spoken aloud it is indistinguishable from the word the grid wants
  • Announced — Something announced has been said out loud, so what the clue describes is a sound and you choose which spelling of it fits.

Someone has publicly called a race a crude affair.

The two spellings are a perfect pair of homophones, which is why the clue needs only three words. 'Vulgar' is doing all the work and 'race' is the definition.

19 down ANTIQUE

Ancient social worker that is covering up question (7)

Definition: Ancient · charade + container

Ancient → ANTIQUE: the adjective sense, belonging to the far past, which the noun for a collectable object tends to eclipse.

  • social worker → ANT The crossword's worker is an ant, and ants are the social insects above all others
  • that is → IE i.e. is the Latin id est, which translates as 'that is'
  • question → QU Qu. is the standard written abbreviation for question
  • covering up — Covering something up hides it under what lies over it, so the two letters that follow disappear inside the pair before them.

An elderly social worker is hushing up an awkward question.

'Social worker' earns its ant twice over — the insect is a worker and it is social — and 'that is' is always the Latin i.e. Neither has anything to do with the caseworker the surface draws.

20 down CREW CUT

Rather brief form of shock? (4,3)

Definition: Rather brief form of shock? · cryptic definition

Rather brief form of shock? → CREW CUT: a shock is a mass of hair, and a crew cut is that hair worn very short.

  • Rather brief → Quite short — and the shortness is literal, a measurement of length rather than of time.
  • form of shock → A shock is a thick unruly mass of hair, as in a shock of red hair, so a form of shock is a way of wearing it.

Something startling, but only mildly and not for long.

Nothing here comes apart into letters. 'Shock' has to stop meaning a jolt and start meaning a head of hair; the briefest way to wear one is clipped to a crew cut.

23 down NEEDS

The more of these you have, the more you want (5)

Definition: The more of these you have, the more you want · cryptic definition

The more of these you have, the more you want → NEEDS: a need is something wanted and not yet had, so more needs is more wanting.

  • The more of these you have → 'These' stands in for the answer itself, which is what makes the sentence a definition rather than a statement about anything.
  • the more you want → 'Want' is doing both jobs at once: to desire, and — in the older sense of 'for want of' — to lack.

The familiar complaint that getting things never leaves you satisfied.

There are no letters to find. The clue is a small paradox that only resolves once you notice a need is a thing you want and a thing you lack at the same time, so collecting them cannot possibly reduce them.
