Across

1 across EAST

Earl and flipping emperor finally lost direction (4)

Definition: direction · charade + deletion + reversal

East is one of the four compass points, so 'direction' names it the way it would name north or west.

  • Earl → E E is the peerage abbreviation for earl, the rank between marquess and viscount.
  • emperor → TSA The emperor of Russia was a TSAR, and the clue takes his closing letter away before turning what is left.
  • flipping — To flip a thing over is to turn it so the far end comes first, which is how the letters beside it are read.
  • finally lost — 'Finally' picks out the last letter and 'lost' says it goes, so the emperor arrives one letter short.

An earl and a deposed emperor who no longer know which way they are going.

'lost direction' reads as one phrase about a wanderer going astray, and only 'lost' belongs to the wordplay. Emperors in crosswords are Russian nine times out of ten.

3 across HANDSPRING

Round of applause: son parking with hoop as cartwheel (10)

Definition: cartwheel · charade

A handspring is an acrobatic flip powered off the hands, and cartwheel is the everyday word for that family of hands-down revolutions.

  • Round of applause → HAND Giving someone a hand is applauding them, and a big hand is a round of it.
  • son → S S is the family-tree abbreviation for son.
  • parking → P P is the letter on every parking sign, and the standard wordplay shorthand.
  • hoop → RING A hoop is a ring, whether a gymnast's or a circus performer's.

A circus turn drawing applause: a boy parks a hoop and rolls it along like a wheel.

'Cartwheel' is the gymnastic move, not the wooden thing on a cart, and the surface spends its whole length pushing you towards the cart.

9 across EMUS

They're grounded and somewhat bemused (4)

Definition: They're grounded · hidden word

Emus are flightless birds, permanently on the ground, so 'They're grounded' defines them by the one thing they cannot do.

  • bemused → EMUS Four consecutive letters sit inside this word, and picking them out is the whole move.
  • somewhat — Somewhat means only partly, so only part of the word beside it is wanted, and the part wanted is a run of letters as they stand.

Somebody confined to the house and mildly baffled about it.

'Grounded' does double duty in the surface: a teenager kept in for the weekend, and a bird that cannot get off the floor. Only the second reading defines anything.

10 across SANDBAGGED

According to Spooner, group fell under attack (10)

Definition: under attack · spoonerism

Somebody sandbagged has been ambushed, originally with a sand-filled sock, so they are very much under attack and taken by surprise.

  • group → SAND A group of musicians is a band; it gives its B away and takes an S in exchange.
  • fell → BAGGED Something that fell in the middle sagged; it hands over its S, takes the B, and the noise that comes back is spelt this way.
  • According to Spooner — The Reverend Spooner was famous for swapping the opening sounds of two words, so the two things named here trade their fronts.

A group of musicians collapsing, as the Reverend might have reported it.

Say 'band sagged' and move the noises at the front across. To sandbag someone is to ambush them with a cosh — a sock filled with sand — which is where the definition comes from.

12 across DESMOND TUTU

Archbishop's mutt sounded rabid (7,4)

Definition: Archbishop · anagram

'Archbishop' defines by example: Desmond Tutu held the office in Cape Town, so the title names him as one holder of it.

  • mutt sounded → DESMONDTUTU The eleven letters of MUTT SOUNDED rearranged.
  • rabid — A rabid animal is out of its own control, and letters out of control will not stay in the order they were handed over in.

An archbishop's stray dog howling as though it had rabies.

'sounded' looks like a marker for a word said out loud, and it is fodder — its letters go into the mix. Desmond Tutu was Archbishop of Cape Town and chaired South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission.

15 across THICKEN

Plot may do this:catch something from character with lisp (7)

Definition: Plot may do this · substitution

A plot thickens when it grows more tangled, so 'Plot may do this' points straight at the verb that phrase made famous.

  • catch something → SICKEN To catch something is to sicken — the phrase people use for coming down with an illness.
  • character with lisp → TH A lisp turns the S sound into TH, so the opening letter is the one that steps aside.
  • character with lisp — A lisp is the speech habit that turns an S sound into TH, so the character named here changes one sound of the word for another.

Catching a bug off someone who cannot say their esses.

'The plot thickens' is the stock phrase the definition leans on, and a lisp is the setter's licence to trade an S sound for a TH.

16 across ST BARTS

Small, tense composer of musical is somewhere in Caribbean (2,5)

Definition: somewhere in Caribbean · charade

St Barts is the everyday English name for Saint Barthélemy, a small French island in the Caribbean, so the definition places it rather than describes it.

  • Small → S S is the size on the clothing label.
  • tense → T T is the grammarian's abbreviation for tense, as in past T or future T.
  • composer of musical → BART Lionel Bart wrote Oliver!, the London stage musical made from Oliver Twist.
  • is → S 'Is' contracts to 's, as in 'it's', so the word lends one letter.

A small, nervous songwriter somewhere in the Caribbean.

Lionel Bart is the composer setters reach for at four letters. The island is Saint Barthélemy, a French territory in the Leeward Islands, and the short name is what everyone actually says.

17 across OBSCENE

Appalling church buried in broken bones (7)

Definition: Appalling · anagram + container

Appalling and OBSCENE both mean shocking enough to offend, obscene carrying the extra charge of indecency the surface is playing with.

  • broken bones → OBSNE Bones supplies five letters and 'broken' says they need not stay in the order given.
  • church → CE CE is the Church of England, the church setters mean nine times out of ten.
  • buried in — To bury a thing in something is to put it inside and close the rest over it, so one piece ends up within the other.
  • broken — Something broken has come apart and gone back together differently, which is what happens to the letters it points at.

A church sunk under a heap of shattered bones.

'buried in' reads as a grave in the surface and as an insertion in the wordplay, and the two readings sit on top of each other for the whole length of the clue.

19 across LATRINE

Dead enthralling, largely, skating venue where you go (7)

Definition: where you go · container + deletion

'Where you go' is the polite euphemism for a lavatory, and a latrine is the rough communal version of one.

  • Dead → LATE The late Mr Smith is the dead one, and that is the sense wanted here.
  • skating venue → RIN A skating venue is a rink, and 'largely' keeps all of it but the final letter.
  • enthralling — To enthral is to hold someone in thrall, and a thing holding another keeps it inside itself.
  • largely — Largely means for the most part and not all of it, so the piece it points at arrives one letter short.

A skating rink that is absolutely gripping.

'Dead enthralling' is British for utterly gripping, and the two words are separate instructions rather than one phrase. A latrine is the trench toilet of a camp or barracks.

20 across UNTRAVERSED

Adventurer's ground that hasn't been crossed (11)

Definition: that hasn't been crossed · anagram

To traverse is to cross, so the un- prefix gives land nobody has crossed: the clue defines by unpacking the word's own parts.

  • Adventurer's → UNTRAVERSED The word with its 's holds eleven letters, and they are rearranged.
  • ground — Ground is the past tense of grind, and anything put through a mill comes out in pieces that no longer sit where they did.

An explorer's patch of country that nobody has walked over.

'Adventurer's ground' reads as a noun phrase — the explorer's own territory — and 'ground' is the past tense of grind, hiding an instruction inside a piece of landscape.

23 across NINETY-NINE

Ice cream, or, non-standardly, IC (6-4)

Definition: Ice cream · double definition

A 99 is the British cone with a Flake stuck in it, and IC is the irregular Roman numeral for the same number.

  • Ice cream → NINETYNINE The British seaside cone, soft whipped ice cream with a chocolate Flake pushed into it, is known by a number.
  • IC → NINETYNINE Read as Roman numerals, I before C means one taken off a hundred.
  • non-standardly — Roman numerals do not normally take a unit off a hundred, so the flag warns that the two letters are an irregular way of writing the number.

A menu shortening 'ice cream' to two letters, which nobody actually does.

The surface offers IC as sloppy shorthand for the words in front of it; the wordplay wants Roman numerals, one before a hundred. XCIX is the form the purists accept.

24 across FLAN

Perhaps quiche getting cool around lake (4)

Definition: Perhaps quiche · container

A flan is an open pastry case with a filling, and a quiche is one of those — the egg-and-cream version — so 'perhaps' marks a definition by example.

  • cool → FAN A fan is the thing you switch on to cool a room.
  • lake → L L is the map abbreviation for lake.
  • around — To go around something is to close on it from both sides, so the longer piece ends up wrapped about the single letter.

A quiche left to cool at the side of a lake.

'Perhaps' is the setter admitting the definition is an example rather than a synonym: a quiche is one kind of flan and not the only one.

25 across AMPERSANDS

Characters seen outside branches of H&M (10)

Definition: Characters seen outside branches of H&M · cryptic definition

Characters are typographic symbols, and the ampersand is one — the & standing between the H and the M on every shopfront.

  • Characters seen outside branches → The surface reading is people loitering on the pavement in front of the shops.
  • of H&M → The setter's reading is typographic: the retailer's name carries a symbol between its two letters, and it is up on the sign over every door.

People hanging about on the pavement outside the clothes shops.

'Characters' means printed symbols rather than people, and 'outside' is literal: the sign carrying the symbol is on the front of the building, not in the aisles.

26 across EDDY

Topless undergarment that may have you in a spin (4)

Definition: that may have you in a spin · deletion

An eddy is a circling current in water or air, so anything caught in one is literally sent spinning round.

  • undergarment → TEDDY A teddy is a one-piece item of women's underwear, and its first letter is the one that goes.
  • Topless — The top of a word is its first letter and topless takes it off — a loose licence, since this entry runs across rather than down, but one setters use freely.

A stripped-down piece of underwear that might leave your head whirling.

The clue's own suggestiveness is the misdirection — 'topless' reads as a description of the garment before it reads as an instruction about letters. An eddy is a small whirl of water or air.

Down

1 down EMENDATION

Denominate reforms for improvement (10)

Definition: improvement · anagram

An emendation is a correction made to a text, so 'improvement' is the editorial sense of the word rather than a general betterment.

  • Denominate → EMENDATION The ten letters of DENOMINATE rearranged.
  • reforms — To re-form something is to form it a second time in a different shape, and nothing obliges the pieces to go back where they were.

Renaming things, as though a new label were itself the way to make them better.

Read as a noun phrase, 'Denominate reforms' sounds like a piece of policy; the second word is a verb and the first is the material it works on.

2 down SQUASHIEST

Most like a pumpkin that's as soft as can be? (10)

Definition: Most like a pumpkin · double definition

Most like a pumpkin works because a pumpkin is a squash; as soft as can be is the everyday sense of something that yields when pressed.

  • Most like a pumpkin → SQUASHIEST A pumpkin belongs to the squash family, so of all vegetables it is the one that most resembles one.
  • as soft as can be → SQUASHIEST Anything you can press a thumb straight into is soft in this way, and 'as ... as can be' asks for the superlative.

A pumpkin gone so far over that it gives under the lightest touch.

Both halves are superlatives, which is what makes the join invisible: one is about resembling a vegetable, the other about how yielding a thing feels.

4 down ABANDON

Spontaneity in desert (7)

Definition: Spontaneity · double definition

Spontaneity is the noun sense, throwing yourself into something unrestrained; desert is the verb, leaving something or someone behind.

  • Spontaneity → ABANDON Dancing with this quality means dancing without restraint or self-consciousness.
  • desert → ABANDON To desert a post is to walk away from it and leave it to its fate.

A burst of impulsiveness turning up somewhere out in the sand.

'in' looks like a container instruction and is only holding the sentence up. One sense is a noun for reckless freedom, the other a verb for walking out.

5 down DIDDUMS

Executed swashbuckling author, article expressed: 'Oh dear!' (7)

Definition: 'Oh dear!' · charade + deletion

'Oh dear!' fits DIDDUMS, the British baby-talk noise of exaggerated sympathy, offered to a crying child or sarcastically to a sulking adult.

  • Executed → DID To execute a task is simply to do it, and the clue's past tense sets the form.
  • swashbuckling author → DUMAS Alexandre Dumas wrote The Three Musketeers, the swashbuckling novel above all others.
  • article → A A is the indefinite article, and it is the letter being squeezed out of the novelist.
  • expressed — To express a liquid is to squeeze it out, so the small word named beside it is pressed out of the name it sits in.

A newspaper piece reacting with mock pity to the execution of an adventure novelist.

'expressed' wants to be a marker for something said out loud; it is the older sense, squeezed out like juice from a lemon. DIDDUMS is what you coo at a grazed knee.

6 down PEANUT BUTTER

Regrettably, punter ate tub - of this? (6,6)

Definition: Regrettably, punter ate tub - of this? · anagram + &lit

The whole sentence reads straight: peanut butter is sold in tubs, and getting through one at a sitting is exactly the sort of thing you would regret.

  • punter ate tub → PEANUTBUTTER The twelve letters of PUNTER ATE TUB rearranged.
  • Regrettably — Something done regrettably has gone wrong, and letters that have gone wrong are letters no longer standing in their proper order.

A gambler regretting the tub he got through in one sitting.

The clue is its own definition: read straight it describes a man eating a tub of the stuff, and the very same words hold the letters. That double duty is what &lit means.

7 down IAGO

Tragic figure, Everyman has a go (4)

Definition: Tragic figure · charade

Iago is the schemer of Shakespeare's Othello, so he is a figure from a tragedy rather than a man who is himself tragic.

  • Everyman → I Everyman is the name this setter signs, so when he refers to himself the letter is I.
  • a go → AGO The last two words are lifted from the clue exactly as they stand.

The setter having an attempt at something, alongside a doomed character.

'Tragic figure' sends you hunting for the hero, and the man here is the villain — the tragedy belongs to Othello. A setter naming himself in his own clue means I.

8 down GIDE

Soldier had intro to erotic French author (4)

Definition: French author · charade + first letter

'French author' defines by example: André Gide, the novelist of The Immoralist, is one such author rather than the only one.

  • Soldier → GI GI is the standard crossword soldier, from the US Army's 'government issue'.
  • had → D 'Had' contracts to 'd in speech — he had, he'd — so the word lends a single letter.
  • erotic → E The opening letter of the word, taken as the intro asks.
  • intro to — An intro is the opening of a piece, so the clue wants the letter that opens the word standing after it.

A soldier being introduced to some racy French writing.

André Gide won the Nobel Prize in 1947 and wrote openly about desire, which is why 'erotic' sits so comfortably next to his nationality in the surface.

11 down COOKIE CUTTER

I'll take the biscuit - regularly! (6,6)

Definition: I'll take the biscuit - regularly! · cryptic definition

The whole clue describes the tool: it takes biscuits out of dough, does it the same way each time, and lends its name to anything mass-produced.

  • I'll take the biscuit → The surface reading is a boast: to take the biscuit is to be the most outrageous thing going.
  • regularly! → The setter's reading is literal and repeated — the same shape pressed out of the dough over and over, which is where the phrase for anything formulaic comes from.

Someone boasting that they will outdo everyone else, and keep on doing it.

A cookie cutter takes the biscuit in the plainest possible sense — it cuts one out of the dough — and it does so identically every time, which is why the phrase came to mean formulaic.

13 down FRUIT SALAD

Sweet tomatoes maybe given to a bloke (5,5)

Definition: Sweet · charade

'Sweet' is the British name for the pudding course, and a fruit salad is a standard one — the noun sense of sweet, not the adjective.

  • tomatoes maybe → FRUITS A tomato is botanically a fruit, and 'maybe' flags that it is one example of the wider class.
  • a → A The article is taken straight off the page as a single letter.
  • bloke → LAD Bloke and lad are both British informal words for a male, so one stands in for the other.

Sweet tomatoes being handed over to some chap.

In British usage 'sweet' is the dessert course rather than a sugary morsel, and that is the sense the definition needs. Tomatoes count as fruit botanically, however they are eaten.

14 down ASCENDANCY

Predominance of crooked candy canes (10)

Definition: Predominance · anagram

Ascendancy is the state of being on top of others, so 'predominance' is a plain synonym — dominance held rather than dominance exercised.

  • candy canes → ASCENDANCY The ten letters of CANDY CANES rearranged.
  • crooked — Crooked means bent out of true, and letters bent out of true are letters no longer in the order they came in.

Christmas sweets with a bend in them.

'Crooked' describes the shape of a candy cane, the hook at the top that everyone pictures, which is exactly why it reads as scenery rather than as an instruction.

18 down ESTONIA

Planes to Niagara? Not entirely getting you here! (7)

Definition: here · hidden word

'Here' points at the answer as a place you might fly to, and Estonia is a Baltic country four thousand miles from the falls the surface offers.

  • Planes to Niagara → ESTONIA Seven consecutive letters run out of the end of the first word, across the second and into the third.
  • Not entirely — 'Not entirely' says you want part of what came before rather than all of it, and the part wanted is a run of letters standing side by side.

A grumble that flights to the falls do not take you all the way.

'Planes to Niagara' looks like scenery for a travel joke and is the hiding place, and the italics on 'entirely' are the setter nudging you to read 'Not entirely' as an instruction.

19 down LEARNED

King Edward among friends is erudite (7)

Definition: erudite · charade

Learned, said as two syllables, means deeply read and scholarly, which is precisely what erudite means.

  • King → LEAR Shakespeare's King Lear is the king setters reach for when four letters are wanted.
  • Edward → NED Ned is the old familiar shortening of Edward, sitting alongside Ted and Ed.
  • among friends — Among friends a man goes by his familiar name rather than his formal one, so the clue wants the nickname and not the christening.

King Edward showing off his reading when he is among friends.

'King Edward' looks like one man — a monarch, or the potato named after him — and it is two separate names. Barristers address each other as 'my learned friend', which is where the surface gets its polish.

21 down ANNA

Ms Karenina going back and forth (4)

Definition: Ms Karenina · palindrome

Ms Karenina is Tolstoy's Anna: the clue hands over the title and the surname and leaves the first name as the thing to find.

  • Ms Karenina → The definition end of the clue: Tolstoy's heroine, given by title and surname only.
  • going back and forth → The wordplay end: four letters that read identically in either direction, which is all the help the clue offers.
  • going back and forth — A word that goes back and forth reads the same whichever end you start from, so there is nothing to turn round — only a symmetry to notice.

Tolstoy's heroine pacing to and fro, unable to settle.

ANNA reads the same from either end, and that symmetry is the whole of the wordplay — 'going back and forth' catches her restlessness as well as the shape of her name.

22 down SNIP

Trim legs raised (4)

Definition: Trim · reversal

To snip is to cut with one quick closing of the scissors, which is what trimming a fringe or a hedge amounts to.

  • legs → PINS Pins is British slang for legs, as in being steady on your pins.
  • raised — This is a down entry, so something raised is read from the bottom of the grid upwards and its letters arrive in the other order.

A shapely pair of legs lifted into the air.

'Trim' reads as an adjective in the surface, describing neat legs, and it is the verb that defines the entry — to take a little off with the scissors.
