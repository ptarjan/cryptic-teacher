Across

1 across OTTO

Serving of risotto’s for German lad (4)

Definition: German lad · hidden word

Otto is a standard German boy's name — Bismarck's first name — so the definition points at a forename rather than at anything to do with rice.

  • risotto’s → OTTO four consecutive letters sit inside this one word, starting at its third letter
  • Serving of — A serving is a portion dished out of something bigger, so only a stretch of the word that follows is wanted.

A plate of rice being dished up for a boy from Germany.

'Serving of' belongs to the restaurant picture and is also the instruction; the letters are already lying in order inside the dish, needing nothing done to them.

4 across PRAT

Nitwit in protective cover taken aback (4)

Definition: Nitwit · reversal

A prat is a fool in ordinary British usage — the mild insult you throw at someone who has just done something silly.

  • protective cover → PRAT a tarp is the waterproof sheet thrown over whatever has to stay dry, and those four letters are then read from the far end
  • taken aback — To be taken aback is to be knocked backwards, and in an across entry backwards means read from the right-hand end.

An idiot startled while sheltering under a tarpaulin.

'Taken aback' is surprise in the surface and a direction of travel in the wordplay, which is the only thing hiding a very ordinary four-letter word.

8 across REFRESHER COURSE

Rescuers here for nonsensical update (9,6)

Definition: update · anagram

A refresher course is training taken to bring worn knowledge back up to date, so 'update' names it by what it does to the person on it.

  • Rescuers here for → REFRESHERCOURSE fifteen letters of raw material across three words, coming out in a new order
  • nonsensical — Something nonsensical makes no sense as written, so the words in front of it are not to be read in the order they stand.

A caption saying where the rescue party is and what they have come about.

'Rescuers here for' reads like the start of a news bulletin, and every letter of it is fodder — including the 'for' that looks like nothing but joinery.

11 across OWN GOAL

In which defence alas is best form of attack? (3,4)

Definition: In which defence alas is best form of attack? · cryptic definition

The whole clue describes the football mishap in which a defending player puts the ball into his own net, so his defending is what produces the other side's score.

  • In which defence alas → the surface offers a maxim about tactics; 'defence' here means the players at the back, the ones furthest from the net they are supposed to be shooting at, and 'alas' is the setter wincing
  • is best form of attack? → the saying runs the other way round — attack is the best form of defence — and turned over it describes a defender putting the ball where only the opposition should

Somebody offering a fresh version of the old sporting maxim about attack and defence.

The proverb is printed back to front, and read that way it describes the moment a defender beats his own keeper. 'Alas' is the one word admitting whose misfortune this is.

12 across PANACEA

Loudly criticise a prophet’s cure-all (7)

Definition: cure-all · charade + homophone

A panacea is a remedy claimed to cure everything, which is what 'cure-all' says — the word is used mostly of solutions that turn out not to work.

  • criticise → PAN to pan a film is to review it savagely
  • a prophet → ACEA a prophet is a seer, and in most British speech that final R is not sounded, so what you hear is four more letters
  • Loudly — Something said loudly is said out loud, so what matters is the noise the words make rather than the way they are spelt.

Shouting down the miracle remedy some prophet is peddling.

Non-rhotic British speech is what makes this work: the R at the end goes unsounded, so a two-word phrase comes out as the tail of a word about medicine.

13 across FLIP CHART

Facetious chat overcoming resistance; it helps the presentation (4,5)

Definition: it helps the presentation · charade + container

A flip chart is the pad of big paper on an easel that a speaker turns over sheet by sheet, so it genuinely helps a presentation along.

  • Facetious → FLIP a flip remark is a glib, not-quite-serious one
  • chat → CHAT the clue's own word, used exactly as it stands
  • resistance → R R is the physicist's symbol for resistance, as in Ohm's law
  • overcoming — To come over something is to go over the top of it, so the word beside this closes across the letter named next.

Jokey small talk winning round a reluctant audience.

'Overcoming' reads as winning an argument and works as a physical instruction, and the definition is a whole clause rather than a synonym — the object a speaker stands beside.

14 across BREAK

You can hear part of car stop working (5)

Definition: stop working · homophone

A machine that breaks has stopped working, so the definition is the intransitive verb — what the car does, not what anyone does to it.

  • part of car → BREAK the pedal that slows a car down, written the other way once only the sound survives
  • You can hear — What you can hear is the sound of a word, so the spelling wanted is the other one that makes the same noise.

A noise from somewhere in the car, just before the thing packs up.

'Part of car' looks as though it wants a hidden word, since parts of words hide inside phrases all the time. The whole component is meant, and only the noise of its name.

15 across SAGAS

Epic tales whichever way you look at it (5)

Definition: Epic tales · palindrome

A saga is a long heroic narrative, the Icelandic ones above all, and the plural matches 'tales' while 'epic' names their scale.

  • Epic tales → the definition end of the clue: long heroic stories, the Norse ones first of all
  • whichever way you look at it → the wordplay end: five letters that read the same from either direction, which is the whole of the help on offer
  • whichever way you look at it — Reading from either end is the only sense in which a word can be looked at two ways, so the letters have to be symmetrical.

A shrug of a remark: these long dramatic stories are what they are, from any angle.

Norse literature gives us the word, and it is the plural that makes the symmetry work — the singular would not read the same in both directions.

16 across JET STREAM

Time invested in joke by paper that affects current events (3,6)

Definition: that affects current events · charade + container

The jet stream is the fast ribbon of high-altitude air current that steers weather systems, so it does affect events, and 'current' is the joke.

  • joke → JEST a jest is a joke
  • Time → T T is the standard abbreviation for time
  • paper → REAM a ream is five hundred sheets of paper
  • invested in — Money invested in a business goes into it, so the letter named before this ends up inside the word named after.

Somebody putting time into a gag for a newspaper that shapes the news.

'Paper' looks like a publication and is the physical stuff, sold five hundred sheets at a time, while 'current events' hides a second sense: currents of air, not news.

19 across ICELAND

Some chic élan demonstrated in Northern location (7)

Definition: Northern location · hidden word

Iceland is the island republic out in the North Atlantic, further north than anywhere else in Europe, so the definition names it by where it sits.

  • chic élan demonstrated → ICELAND seven consecutive letters run from partway through the first word into the start of the third
  • Some — Some of a thing is a part of it and never the whole, so only a stretch of the words that follow is wanted.

Stylish flair being shown off somewhere well to the north.

'Some' at the front reads as a quantity of style and is the extraction order, while the italicised French word looks like a definition by example and is only letters.

21 across YULE LOG

Christmas list – piece of cake (4,3)

Definition: piece of cake · charade

A yule log is the chocolate-and-cream sponge rolled and iced to resemble a log, eaten at Christmas, so the definition is meant literally.

  • Christmas → YULE Yule is the old word for Christmas, still heard in yuletide
  • list → LOG a ship's log is the running list of everything that happens aboard

Working down the Christmas to-do list and finding it no trouble at all.

'Piece of cake' is an idiom for something easy in the surface and is meant literally in the definition — the chocolate roll iced to look like firewood.

22 across FLOGS A DEAD HORSE

Pursues lost cause – seeks money at proverbial glue factory? (5,1,4,5)

Definition: Pursues lost cause · charade

To flog a dead horse is to keep pushing at something that cannot go anywhere, which is exactly what pursuing a lost cause amounts to.

  • seeks money → FLOGS to flog something is to sell it, British slang for shifting goods for cash
  • proverbial glue factory → ADEADHORSE the glue factory is where worn-out horses were proverbially sent, so this names what you would find waiting there

A trader trying to get paid at the place where old horses end up.

Horses past working age were once sent for rendering into glue, which is why the idiom reaches for a dead one: nothing you do to it will get it moving again.

23 across ENVY

Begrudge diplomat wanting nothing (4)

Definition: Begrudge · deletion

To envy someone is to begrudge them what they have — resenting their good fortune, not merely wishing for the same thing yourself.

  • diplomat → ENVOY an envoy is a diplomat sent out to represent a government
  • nothing → O nothing is written as a nought, and that is the letter which goes
  • wanting — Something wanting is missing what it ought to have, so the word beside it turns up short of the letter named next.

Resenting a diplomat who appears to lack for nothing.

'Wanting nothing' reads as a comfortable life and is really an instruction, because wanting here is the older sense of lacking rather than of wishing for.

24 across DAME

Woman starts to drowse alongside Everyman (4)

Definition: Woman · charade + first letters

Dame is an old general word for a woman, and in Britain the formal title given to a woman honoured at the rank of a knight.

  • drowse alongside → DA the opening letter of each of these two words
  • Everyman → ME Everyman is the pen name this puzzle's setter signs, so the word means the setter himself
  • starts to — A start is a first letter, and the plural of it covers both of the words that follow rather than just the nearer one.

Nodding off in the seat next to the setter.

Setters slip themselves into their own clues constantly, and this one's pen name is printed above the grid — so the last word is worth two letters rather than none.

2 down THE FORCES

Combatants of Chester mustered (3,6)

Definition: Combatants · anagram

The forces are the army, navy and air force taken together — the people whose job is fighting, which is what 'combatants' names.

  • of Chester → THEFORCES nine letters of raw material, the little joining word included, coming out in another order
  • mustered — Troops are mustered when they are called together and drawn up in a fresh formation, which is what happens to these letters.

Soldiers from a Cheshire city being called out on parade.

The city name does no geographical work at all; it is nine letters of fodder, and the 'of' that looks like grammar goes into the shuffle with it.

3 down OPHELIA

Love Prince? He will get love interest afflicted principally: her (7)

Definition: her · charade + first letters

'her' works only with the clue's prince and madness around it, narrowing the pronoun to Hamlet's love, who loses her reason and drowns.

  • Love → O a score of love in tennis is nothing, and nothing is written as a nought
  • Prince? → P P is the standard abbreviation for prince, and the question mark admits it is a squeeze
  • He → HE the clue's own pronoun, used exactly as it stands
  • love interest afflicted → LIA the opening letter of each of these three words, in the order printed
  • principally — Principally means chiefly or at the head, so the leading letter of each word it governs is all that is taken.

A prince's romance going badly wrong for the woman caught up in it.

Shakespeare is never named: a prince and a love interest driven out of her wits are all the pointing the clue does, and both of those words are wordplay as well.

4 down PARAPET

Elite soldier having hissy fit in defensive structure (7)

Definition: defensive structure · charade

A parapet is the low wall running along the top of a rampart or roof that soldiers shelter behind, so it is both defensive and a structure.

  • Elite soldier → PARA a para is a paratrooper, one of the airborne troops
  • hissy fit → PET to be in a pet is to be in a sulk — an old British word for a fit of temper, nothing to do with animals

A crack soldier throwing a tantrum up on the battlements.

'Pet' meaning a sulk has almost left speech and survives in the phrase 'in a pet', and nothing in the clue warns you that the family cat is not wanted.

5 down ACORN

Something unpleasant afoot? No, a treat for Piglet (5)

Definition: treat for Piglet · charade

Piglet, in Winnie-the-Pooh, is forever after acorns — he calls them haycorns — so the definition names the answer by who eats it.

  • No, a → A the setter withdraws the foot reading with 'No' and hands over the little word beside it exactly as printed
  • Something unpleasant afoot → CORN the hard painful patch a walker gets on a toe, which is what 'afoot' pins the fragment to

A foot complaint being ruled out in favour of something a small pig would enjoy.

'Afoot' sets up the toe complaint and the comma then throws it away, because the same letters read as one word rather than two are a thing to eat.

6 down CRY OFF

Decide against show of emotion: that’s rotten (3,3)

Definition: Decide against · charade

To cry off is to back out of something you had already agreed to do, which is deciding against it after the fact.

  • show of emotion → CRY tears are the most obvious show of emotion there is
  • rotten → OFF milk that has gone off has gone bad, which is the British way of saying food is rotten

Ruling out a display of tears on the grounds that it is a rotten thing to do.

The colon does the work of an equals sign, so the sentence reads as a moral judgement while it is quietly a sum, and 'rotten' points at food rather than at behaviour.

7 down REMARK

Give another grade to statement (6)

Definition: Give another grade to · double definition

To re-mark a paper is to grade it again, while a remark is an aside — the same six letters covering an examiner's task and a scrap of speech.

  • Give another grade to → an examiner going over a script a second time does exactly this, and the hyphen the sense needs has been left off on purpose
  • statement → the everyday noun for something said in passing

A teacher being asked to grade a written statement all over again.

One printed word covers two unrelated ones, kept apart only by a hyphen that is missing: with it an examiner's job, without it a thing somebody said.

9 down FINDING NEMO

OK? Not quite: demon gin drunk in children’s film (7,4)

Definition: children’s film · charade + deletion + anagram

Finding Nemo is the Pixar animation about a clownfish father hunting for his lost son, made for and watched by children.

  • OK? → FIN OK is FINE, and this stops short of that word's last letter
  • demon gin → DINGNEMO eight letters of raw material across the two words, re-sorted
  • Not quite — Not quite means falling just short of the whole thing, so the word before it arrives one letter light.
  • drunk — A drunk cannot walk a straight line, and letters in that state do not stay in the order they were handed over in.

Checking whether somebody is all right after a bottle of fearsome gin.

'Demon' describes the gin in the surface and is plain fodder in the wordplay, while the question mark after the first word makes an instruction look like concern.

10 down ROCKEFELLER

Wealthy businessman to surprise, finally, Claude and Hewer (11)

Definition: Wealthy businessman · charade + last letter

John D. Rockefeller made an oil fortune vast enough that the name became shorthand for a rich businessman, so the definition works by example.

  • to surprise → ROCK news that rocks you is news that shakes you badly
  • Claude → E the last letter of the name, and nothing else from it
  • Hewer → FELLER a feller brings trees down, which is one way of hewing wood
  • finally — Finally means at the very end, so only the closing letter of the name beside it is taken.

A tycoon springing something on two men, Claude and Hewer.

The capital H makes the last word read as a surname standing beside Claude, which is the whole disguise on a six-letter chunk that is really a job.

14 down BETHLEHEM

Liz refurbished helmet endlessly in ancient town (9)

Definition: ancient town · charade + anagram + deletion

Bethlehem is the West Bank town thousands of years old, known as the birthplace of King David and of Jesus.

  • Liz → BETH Liz and Beth are both short for Elizabeth, so either can stand in for the other
  • helmet → LEHEM six letters with the last one struck off, and the five left over re-sorted
  • refurbished — Something refurbished has been stripped down and put back together looking different, which is what happens to the letters beside it.
  • endlessly — Endlessly means without an end, so the final letter of the word it follows is left off.

Liz doing up a helmet, on and on, somewhere very old indeed.

'Refurbished' sits between the two halves and only works on the second. The first is a swap between two pet forms of the same name, which no word in the clue signals at all.

15 down SNIFFY

Disdainful son humming (6)

Definition: Disdainful · charade

To be sniffy about something is to look down your nose at it — disdain expressed through the nose, which is also where the smell came in.

  • son → S S is the standard abbreviation for son, as written on a family tree
  • humming → NIFFY in British slang a thing that is humming smells bad, and niffy says the same — a niff is a nasty smell

A superior young man who badly needs a wash.

'Humming' has nothing to do with music here, and both it and its replacement are informal British words for a bad smell, which is what makes the swap fair.

16 down JADEDLY

Justice terribly delayed – energy dropping – without enthusiasm (7)

Definition: without enthusiasm · charade + anagram + deletion

To do something jadedly is to do it wearily, with the appetite worn out of you, which is doing it without enthusiasm.

  • Justice → J J is the standard abbreviation for a judge or justice, printed after the name in law reports
  • delayed → ADEDLY seven letters of raw material with one struck out, and the six left over re-sorted
  • energy → energy is E to a physicist, and that is the letter taken out of the material
  • terribly — Anything done terribly is done badly, and letters handled badly do not stay in the order they were set down in.
  • dropping — What drops out of something is no longer part of it, so the letter named just before this leaves the material.

A court case dragging on while everyone involved runs out of energy.

Justice delayed is justice denied — the phrase the surface leans on — and the pair of dashes make the middle clause read as a weary aside rather than as an order.

17 down TOYLAND

Told any cobblers in realm of children’s tales? (7)

Definition: realm of children’s tales · anagram

Toyland is the setting of Enid Blyton's Noddy books, the make-believe country those children's stories take place in.

  • Told any → TOYLAND seven letters of raw material across the two words, coming out in a new order
  • cobblers — Cobblers is British slang for rubbish or nonsense, and letters called nonsense are not sitting in any sensible order.

Accusing somebody of talking rubbish about a storybook world.

'Cobblers' comes from rhyming slang — cobbler's awls — and so means nonsense, which is what lets it order a shuffle while reading as part of the accusation.

18 down MAGNET

10am, rising, embracing German that’s attractive (6)

Definition: that’s attractive · container + reversal

A magnet is the thing that literally attracts, pulling iron towards it, so 'that's attractive' is the plain physical sense and not the flattering one.

  • 10am, rising → MANET ten in the morning spelled out gives five letters, then read from the bottom of the column upwards
  • German → G G is the standard dictionary abbreviation for German
  • rising — A down entry read upwards runs from its foot to its head, and anything that rises travels in exactly that direction.
  • embracing — To embrace someone is to close your arms round them, so whatever is named next finishes up inside.

Someone getting up at ten and hugging a good-looking German.

The digits have to be spelled out before anything can be done to them, and the clue never says so. 'Attractive' is meant physically rather than as a compliment.

20 down ARSON

Arouse radiant smouldering – only, naughtily, primarily? (5)

Definition: Arouse radiant smouldering – only, naughtily, primarily? · &lit + first letters

The whole clue reads straight as a description of the crime: rousing a radiant smouldering, and doing it naughtily, is deliberate fire-raising.

  • Arouse radiant smouldering → ARS the letter each of these three words begins with, in the order printed
  • only, naughtily → ON the opening letter of each of these two words, keeping the run going after the dash
  • primarily — Primarily means in the first place, so only the opening letter of each word it governs is taken.

Someone stirring up a warm glow, and being wicked about it.

The sentence describes the crime and, taken a letter at a time off the front of each word, spells it. 'Radiant smouldering' is fire being admired rather than reported.
