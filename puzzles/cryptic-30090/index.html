Guardian Cryptic Crossword No 30,090

Across

1 across ANAGRAM

Crazy MAGA ran America as a repeated crime, for instance (7)

Definition: America as a repeated crime, for instance · anagram

AMERICA rearranges into A CRIME with the A used twice, so the phrase does not describe the answer, it is an example of one.

  • MAGA ran → ANAGRAM the seven letters of MAGA RAN, re-sorted
  • Crazy — Crazy means out of its right mind, and letters out of their right mind will not stay in the order the clue prints them.

Brendan's theme is the crossword itself, and this clue is where it starts. Watch the definition: it hands you a specimen rather than a synonym, which is what 'for instance' is quietly warning you about.

5 across IN SHOCK

Traumatized son held by uncle outside (2,5)

Definition: Traumatized · container

Medicine's shock is the body's collapse after injury, so somebody in shock is somebody the trauma has floored.

  • held by uncle → IN HOCK 'uncle' is old slang for a pawnbroker, so whatever he is holding is in hock
  • son → S S is the family-tree abbreviation for son
  • outside — 'Outside' is a position, not a place: the pawnbroker's phrase goes round the other piece rather than beside it.

The uncle is the giveaway: an ordinary relative in the surface, a pawnbroker in the parse. That sense of the word survives almost nowhere outside crosswords and Victorian novels.

9 across SET IN

Compose crosswords at home to become established (3,2)

Definition: become established · charade

Of weather, rot or a habit, to set in is to arrive and stay: 'the rain has set in' means it has taken hold for good.

  • Compose crosswords → SET the person who composes them is a setter, and what a setter does is set
  • at home → IN IN is the standard crossword reading of 'at home': you are in when you are there

Brendan is describing his own job in the first three letters, which is the sort of joke this puzzle keeps making.

10 across RAMPANTLY

River plant may spread in profusion (9)

Definition: in profusion · anagram

A weed growing rampantly is growing unchecked and everywhere at once, which is what growing in profusion describes.

  • River → R R is the map's abbreviation for river, and it joins the fodder as a single letter
  • plant may → PLANTMAY eight more letters, taken exactly as they stand
  • spread — Something spread has been scattered loose over an area, and letters scattered like that do not come back in the order they left.

'River plant' looks like one thing growing in water, and the setter has cut it in half: the river is a letter, the plant is fodder.

11 across EYEBALLING

Closely observing vote in favour, with subsequent outcry heard (10)

Definition: Closely observing · charade + homophone

To eyeball something is to look it over hard and at close range, the informal verb for staring at a thing to size it up.

  • vote in favour → EYE a vote in favour is an aye, and an aye said out loud is the organ you see with
  • outcry → BALLING an outcry is bawling, and bawling said out loud spells the rest
  • subsequent — 'Subsequent' means coming afterwards, so this piece is added on the end rather than wrapped round anything.
  • heard — 'Heard' hands the clue to the ear: write down what the words sound like, not how a dictionary spells them.

One 'heard' at the very end licenses both soundalikes at once, which is why nothing in the middle of the clue looks like an instruction.

12 across ERGO

… is 21 reversed? So it is! (4)

Definition: So · reversal

Ergo is the Latin 'therefore' that English kept for mock-formal argument, doing the same job as a conversational 'so'.

  • 21 → ERGO 21 across is the monster; turn its four letters round and this is what stands there
  • reversed — The clue wants the other entry written the other way along the row, its last letter first.

The row of dots runs the sentence on from the clue before, so the number is not a number: it stands for whatever 21 turned out to be. The exclamation then cheers the discovery and defines the word.

14 across REDISSOLVED

Kind of setter one’s figured out? Produced solution again (11)

Definition: Produced solution again · charade

A chemist's solution is a solid taken into liquid, so producing one again is dissolving the stuff a second time.

  • Kind of setter → RED a red setter is a breed of dog, so a kind of setter is a colour
  • one’s → IS one is I in crosswords, and the 's comes along as S
  • figured out → SOLVED to figure something out is to solve it

Every word of the surface belongs to crosswords and none of it means what it seems: the setter is a dog and the solution is chemistry.

18 across CROSS SWORDS

Angry outburst about small fight (5,6)

Definition: fight · charade + container

From duelling, where the blades literally cross, the phrase has come to mean clashing with somebody in argument.

  • Angry → CROSS cross is the everyday British word for angry
  • small → S S is the label sewn into the small size
  • outburst → WORDS to have words with somebody is to have a row, so an outburst is words
  • about — 'About' puts one piece round another, so the size letter is swallowed rather than tacked on the end.

This is where the theme surfaces: an angry exchange is cross words, and one extra letter turns the puzzle you are solving into a duel.

21 across OGRE

Turn back on monster (4)

Definition: monster · charade + reversal

The man-eating giant of fairy tale, and by extension anybody brutish enough to frighten children.

  • Turn → OG a turn is a GO, as in whose turn is it, and this one is taken backwards
  • on → RE RE means 'on the subject of', the 'on' at the head of a memo
  • back — Going back along a row means reading it right to left, so the piece in front of the word is written the other way.

'Turn back on' reads as one idiom about rejection. The parse ignores the idiom completely and takes each of the three words on its own, which is the lesson worth keeping.

22 across SUBMERSION

Nothing in numbers is changed as result of moving 15 (10)

Definition: result of moving 15 · anagram

15 down is DOWN UNDER, and to move down under water is to be submerged, which is exactly what this noun names.

  • Nothing → O nothing is a nought, and a nought is written O
  • numbers is → NUMBERSIS nine more letters, taken exactly as they stand
  • changed — Letters told they are changed have to come out different from the way they went in, and their order is the only thing about them that can differ.

'Nothing in numbers' is a single idea on the surface and two separate lots of letters underneath. The cross-reference is the second joke: 15 is a place, and this is what happens when you go there.

25 across TWO-HANDED

Like the most one can do, in terms of manual employment (3-6)

Definition: Like the most one can do, in terms of manual employment · cryptic definition

Working with both hands is the most manual employment anybody can manage, so the phrase for using both is also the phrase for the maximum.

  • the most one can do → The surface pushes 'the best effort somebody can manage', a phrase about trying hard.
  • in terms of manual employment → The setter means it as arithmetic. 'Manual' is Latin for of-the-hand, so what is being counted is how many of those a person has to put to work, and the maximum is not large.

There is nothing to take apart here, no fodder and no letters to move. The whole clue is one definition told slant, and the joke is that the limit it describes is a limit everybody already has.

26 across CODER

Program’s producer, person able to handle Morse, say (5)

Definition: Program’s producer · double definition

Both halves rest on the noun 'code': software is written in one, Morse is another, and whoever handles either is a coder.

  • Program’s producer → CODER software is written in code, so this names whoever writes it
  • person able to handle Morse, say → CODER Morse is a code, and 'say' flags it as one example of the class

Morse arrives capitalised and looks like the Oxford detective, which is the whole misdirection: the other reading is the inventor and the alphabet named after him.

27 across CREATOR

Rector having letter forwarded? It’s about one name for God (7)

Definition: name for God · anagram + container

Genesis has him make the world, so 'the Creator' is the standard reverent title in English for whoever did the making.

  • Rector → CRETOR send the C of RECTOR to the front and this is what is left standing
  • one → A A is the indefinite article, crossword shorthand for one
  • having letter forwarded — A letter that is forwarded is sent on ahead, so one character leaves its place in the churchman's title and goes to the front of the queue.
  • about — 'About' is positional here rather than approximate: the six letters close round the single letter that follows.

Ecclesiastical from top to bottom, a rector and his post and the being he works for, and only the middle of it is doing any wordplay.

28 across MASONED

Solidly constructed – one’s mad to change it (7)

Definition: Solidly constructed · anagram

A mason works in stone, and stonework is the byword for building that will not budge, so masoned walls are solidly built.

  • one’s mad → MASONED the seven letters of ONE'S MAD, rearranged
  • to change — To change something is to leave it other than you found it, and the only thing that can differ about these seven letters is their order.

The dash does the work of an equals sign, and the surface, that you would be mad to change it, is itself the instruction to change it.

Down

1 down ANSWER

Something solvers may lack – and so were not finished (6)

Definition: Something solvers may lack · charade + deletion

What a stuck solver is short of is exactly this, the word that belongs in the grid, so the definition names it by the gap it leaves.

  • and → AN 'and' with its tail lopped off
  • so → S 'so' unfinished is down to a single letter
  • were → WER 'were' gives up its final E the same way
  • not finished — A word left not finished stops short of its last letter, and all three little words in front of this one stop short.

The instruction sits at the end and reaches back over three words at once, which is easy to miss: 'and so were not finished' reads as ordinary grammar rather than as three separate amputations.

2 down ANTHER

Part of bloomer in Guardian? There you have it (6)

Definition: Part of bloomer · hidden word

A bloomer is a plant that blooms, and the anther is the pollen-bearing head of its stamen, so the definition picks out one organ of a flower.

  • Guardian? There → ANTHER the answer runs unbroken across the question mark, straddling the end of the first word and the start of the second
  • in — Read this one literally rather than as joinery: it says the word sits inside the letters that follow.

'Bloomer' is doing two jobs at once: a howler in the surface, at the paper's own expense, and a flower in the definition. Only one of them is true.

3 down RUNS ACROSS

Happens to meet – as any intersecting answer here does (4,6)

Definition: Happens to meet · double definition

Running across someone is the idiom for a chance encounter, and an entry crossing this one goes left to right, which is what running across a grid means.

  • Happens to meet → RUNS ACROSS the everyday idiom for bumping into somebody you were not looking for
  • as any intersecting answer here does → RUNS ACROSS this is a down entry, so everything that cuts through it is an across one

The dash is the split, and the second half is a set of directions: look at the grid and check what the entries cutting through this one are called.

4 down MORAL

Good lesson as example of double definition (5)

Definition: Good · double definition

'Good' is the adjective of upright conduct, and a fable's 'lesson' is the tag printed after it, one word wearing two grammatical hats.

  • Good → MORAL the adjective: upright, on the side of right conduct
  • lesson → MORAL the noun tagged on to the end of a fable
  • as example of double definition → Not wordplay at all: the setter labelling his own device, the way 1 across does.

Two senses of one word sit side by side, and the rest of the sentence is Brendan naming the trick out loud. The surface reads as praise for a sermon.

5 down IMMUNISER

About negative introduction, admission of meanie who delivers jabs? (9)

Definition: who delivers jabs? · charade + container

A jab is British informal for a vaccination rather than a punch, so the person handing them out is the one making you immune.

  • admission → IM an admission opens with I am, shortened, and the apostrophe is dropped on the way in
  • meanie → MISER a meanie is a miser, the one who will not part with a penny
  • negative introduction → UN UN- is the syllable English puts on the front of a word to negate it, and it lands just after the M of MISER
  • About — 'About' is placing here, not topic: the piece named next ends up wrapped inside what the rest of the clue builds.

'Jabs' is the trap: boxing on the surface, vaccination underneath, and the meanie owning up is doing wordplay rather than character work.

6 down SPAN

Get across what’s central to crossword? Separate out valuable bits (4)

Definition: Get across · charade + middle letter

A bridge spans a gap, reaching from one bank to the other, which is the same job as getting across it.

  • crossword → S nine letters, and the fifth of them is the one in the middle
  • Separate out valuable bits → PAN to pan is to wash river gravel until only the gold is left
  • what’s central to — It asks for the letter standing at the exact midpoint of the word that follows, not for anything the word means.

A four-letter answer with the theme word supplying just one letter of it: 'crossword' is here to be measured, not to be read.

7 down OUTDRIVE

Excel on course in skill, not in approach (8)

Definition: Excel on course in skill · charade

Golf's verb for sending the ball further off the tee than your opponent, beating them at driving, the one skill a tee shot tests.

  • not in → OUT whatever is not in is out, the plainest opposition English has
  • approach → DRIVE the drive is a house's approach, the gravel sweep from the gate to the door

Both halves of the clue sound like golf, which is what makes the seam hard to see: the definition is the golfing sense and the wordplay is a suburban front garden.

8 down KEY WORDS

In part of Florida, wife or daughter located crucial elements (3,5)

Definition: crucial elements · charade + container

A keyword is the one that unlocks a search or a text, 'key' carrying its adjectival sense of crucial rather than naming a piece of metal.

  • part of Florida → KEYS the Keys are the island chain trailing off the state's southern tip
  • wife → W W is the genealogist's shorthand for wife
  • or → OR taken at face value: the two letters of the word itself
  • daughter → D D is the matching shorthand for daughter, and W-OR-D drops in ahead of the final S
  • In — The clue opens with its container instruction: everything built afterwards goes inside the place named right here.
  • located — 'Located' asks where a thing sits rather than what it means, so these letters are being placed in a gap, not defined.

The joinery word 'or' is fodder, not grammar, and that is the step people miss here: a wife or a daughter reads as a choice rather than as letters to write down.

13 down CONSTRUCTS

Builds lead in constituency, embraced by Tory supporters (10)

Definition: Builds · charade + container + first letter

To construct is to build, the formal verb a report reaches for where speech would say 'put up'.

  • Tory → CON a Tory is a Conservative, CON on election night
  • supporters → STRUTS struts are the supports that hold a structure up
  • constituency → C its leading letter, dropped in between the U and the T
  • lead in — A word's lead is the letter at its head, so 'constituency' is being asked for its initial and nothing else.
  • embraced by — An embrace closes round whatever it holds, so that letter finishes up inside the other pieces rather than beside them.

The whole surface is an election night and none of it is politics underneath: 'supporters' means scaffolding, and a lead is a letter rather than a margin.

15 down DOWN UNDER

Doctor’s beginning new round, confused and extremely distant (4,5)

Definition: extremely distant · anagram + first letter

From Britain, Australia and New Zealand sit at the far side of the globe, and Down Under is the nickname that says so.

  • Doctor’s → D the first letter of the word Doctor, not the abbreviation DR
  • new round → NEW ROUND eight more letters, taken exactly as they stand
  • beginning — A word's beginning is its opening letter, so the doctor contributes one character and no more.
  • confused — Something confused has lost track of its proper order, which is what these letters are being told to do.

The surface has a doctor starting his ward round, which is precisely the wrong picture: nothing here is medical and the letters are all that matter.

16 down ACROSTIC

Wordplay known to Greeks, Socratic sort (8)

Definition: Wordplay known to Greeks · anagram

The word is Greek itself, and Greek poets were writing verses whose initials spell out a name centuries before crosswords existed.

  • Socratic → ACROSTIC the philosopher's adjective, its eight letters re-sorted
  • sort — On the surface 'sort' is a kind of thing; as a verb it means to put into a different order, and that is the job it is doing.

The philosopher supplies the letters and his countrymen supply the definition, so the whole clue stays inside ancient Greece.

17 down FOURSOME

Little Women, for example, in half a reel? (8)

Definition: Little Women, for example · double definition

The March sisters are four, so the title names the answer by instance; and the foursome reel really is the eightsome reel halved.

  • Little Women, for example → FOURSOME Alcott's March sisters, Meg, Jo, Beth and Amy, come as a set of four, and 'for example' flags a definition by instance
  • half a reel → FOURSOME the Scottish eightsome reel takes eight dancers; halve it and the dance is named for the number left

Both halves point at the number four without ever printing it, one through a novel and one through a dance floor.

19 down HIDDEN

Like certain answers in relation to clues, some of which I’d denigrate (6)

Definition: Like certain answers in relation to clues · hidden word

A hidden-word answer lies concealed inside the clue's own letters, so the word describes the relation between that answer and the text carrying it.

  • which I’d denigrate → HIDDEN the answer runs unbroken across the tail of the first word and on through the next two
  • some of — 'Some of' wants a piece of the words that follow rather than their meaning, a run of letters lifted straight out.

The answer is doing to this clue exactly what it describes, which is Brendan's favourite kind of joke and worth watching for elsewhere in the grid.

20 down INGRID

Girl’s name forming part of solution (6)

Definition: Girl’s name · charade

It is a Scandinavian first name, familiar in Britain through Ingrid Bergman, so the clue defines it by category rather than by meaning.

  • forming part of → IN whatever forms part of a thing is in it
  • solution → GRID a newspaper prints tomorrow's solution as a filled-in diagram, so the solution and the grid are the same picture

The name says where it lives. Brendan's puzzle is full of clues about crosswords, and this one quietly describes its own position on the page.

23 down MADAM

Way to address lady in Mother’s Union (5)

Definition: Way to address lady · charade

Madam is the polite form of address to a woman, the female counterpart of 'sir' and the one a shop assistant still uses.

  • Mother → MA MA is the nursery shortening of mother
  • Mother’s → DAM a dam is a mother too, the breeder's word for the female parent of a foal or a calf
  • Union — A union is a joining, so the two pieces are simply written one against the other with nothing done to either.

The Mothers' Union is a real Anglican society, which is what the surface is counting on. Underneath, 'mother' is asked for twice and answered two different ways.

24 down LAST

Endure like this clue (4)

Definition: Endure · double definition

'Endure' is the verb, as in a thing that lasts goes on, while the adjective describes the final item in a series, which is where 24 down sits.

  • Endure → LAST the verb for something that keeps going without giving out, like a battery
  • like this clue → LAST run down the numbering, and nothing follows 24 in the down list

The second definition points off the page at the clue's own position, so the only way to check it is to look at where the clue is printed.
