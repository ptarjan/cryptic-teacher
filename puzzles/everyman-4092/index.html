Observer Everyman Crossword No 4,092

Across

1 across OPEN SESAME

One-time passcode that might make sense, a poem (4,6)

Definition: One-time passcode · anagram

Open sesame is the phrase that swings the robbers' cave door in the Arabian Nights, and it works only for whoever knows it — a passcode.

  • sense, a poem → SENSEAPOEM anagram fodder, ten letters — exactly the length the grid wants
  • might make → anagram indicator
  • might make — What letters MIGHT make is not what they already spell, so the words beside it are raw material to be built with rather than read.

Somebody wondering whether a one-off login code could be read as a short piece of verse.

Arabian Nights magic worked exactly like a one-time passcode: say the words once and the rock opens. Counting settles the parse, since 'sense, a poem' is ten letters and nothing else is.

6 across WAIF

Spouse scratching backside stopped by a ragamuffin (4)

Definition: ragamuffin · deletion + container

A waif is a thin, uncared-for child adrift with nobody to claim them, which is the ragged street urchin 'ragamuffin' names.

  • Spouse → WIFE the married partner, the female one
  • scratching backside → the E at the rear is scratched off, leaving three letters
  • a → A the letter of the word 'a' itself, dropped into the gap behind the opening letter
  • scratching backside — A backside is the rear end, and scratching it out here means the letter at the back is rubbed away.
  • stopped by — Being stopped by something means it gets in your way, so the letter named is planted inside the word rather than added at either end.

A spouse caught scratching an itch, interrupted by a scruffy child.

'Backside' is anatomy on the surface and a position in the wordplay, and that one word does all the misleading in a four-letter clue.

9 across TAE-BO

Primarily trademarked aerobic exercises, boogieing occasionally? (3-2)

Definition: Primarily trademarked aerobic exercises, boogieing occasionally? · &lit + first letters

Tae-Bo is Billy Blanks's trademarked workout, taekwondo kicks crossed with boxing, so the sentence also reads straight as a description of it.

  • trademarked aerobic exercises → TAE the opening letter of each of the three words, taken in order
  • boogieing occasionally → BO the opening letter of each of the last two words, which finishes the entry
  • Primarily — Primarily means first of all, so what is wanted from each of the words that follow is the letter it opens with.

A fitness class sold as branded aerobics with a bit of dancing thrown in now and then.

Every word after the first hands over its initial, and the sentence they spell is itself a fair description of the thing — a clue with no separate definition anywhere in it.

10 across NAKEDNESS

Raw beside Loch – because of this under kilt? (9)

Definition: this under kilt · charade

Tradition insists a true Scot wears nothing beneath the kilt, so what is under there is bare skin — nakedness.

  • Raw → NAKED raw in the sense of bare and uncovered, as raw flesh is
  • Loch → NESS Loch Ness is the Highland lake with the monster, and setters use the second half of its name on its own
  • beside — Beside puts one thing alongside another, so the two pieces are simply written next to each other in the order the clue gives them.

Something raw sitting by a Scottish loch, and the old question about what is worn under a kilt.

'Loch' is a definition by example running backwards: the setter names the category and expects the most famous instance of it, which is how crossword lochs, rivers and dukes all work.

12 across MISSTEP

Overturning favourite sofas, gutted, Everyman’s making a gaffe (7)

Definition: a gaffe · charade + reversal + deletion

A misstep is a wrong move, and in ordinary speech a blunder of conduct rather than of walking — which is what a gaffe is.

  • favourite → PET a favourite is a pet — the teacher's pet
  • sofas → SS the middle is taken out and the two outer letters of the word are left
  • Everyman’s → IM the setter signs himself Everyman, so 'Everyman is' is him saying I'm
  • Overturning — Overturning a thing tips it end over end, so the whole run of letters just built is read back from its far end.
  • gutted — Something gutted has had its insides taken out and only the shell left, which for a word means its first and last letters.

The setter admitting he has tipped his favourite sofas over and wrecked them.

Everyman writes himself into his own clues: whenever the name appears it is the setter speaking in the first person, so it is worth a pronoun rather than a search for a proper noun.

13 across SAWBILL

Bird noticed notice (7)

Definition: Bird · charade

A sawbill is a merganser, the fish-eating duck whose beak carries serrated tooth-like edges, so 'Bird' names it by class.

  • noticed → SAW the past tense of see
  • notice → BILL a bill pasted on a wall is a public notice — hence billboard

Somebody spotting a poster.

Three words, two of them near-identical: 'noticed' and 'notice' read like a typo and are two entirely separate pieces of vocabulary.

14 across UNAPPETISING

Fried pig in peanuts, hardly savoury (12)

Definition: hardly savoury · anagram

Unappetising means it puts you off eating it, and 'hardly savoury' says the same by understatement — the food is not tempting.

  • pig in peanuts → PIGINPEANUTS anagram fodder, twelve letters — the full length of the entry
  • Fried → anagram indicator
  • Fried — Something dropped into hot fat comes out changed in shape, so the letters beside the word do not keep the arrangement they arrived in.

A pig fried in peanuts, which is not a dish anybody is queueing for.

Surface and definition agree with each other for once — the dish really does sound horrible — and that agreement is what stops you noticing that 'in' belongs to the letters rather than to the sentence.

18 across EXIT STRATEGY

Plan for how to get out: try flailing with axes, get it? (4,8)

Definition: Plan · anagram

An exit strategy is the route out worked out in advance — of a war, a business, an investment — so it is a plan in the plainest sense.

  • how to get out → no letters here — these words go on describing the kind of plan wanted, which is why the entry is two words rather than one
  • try → TRY the first three letters of the fodder
  • axes, get it → AXESGETIT nine more, making twelve in all
  • flailing → anagram indicator
  • flailing — Flailing arms go everywhere at once, so the letters either side of the word end up anywhere but where they started.

Somebody hacking their way out of a burning building with an axe in each hand.

'Get it?' reads as the setter checking you have followed the joke, and it is fodder: five of the twelve letters are sitting in those two little words.

21 across POMPEII

Classical city of Greek character wherein I mope about (7)

Definition: Classical city · container + anagram

Pompeii was the Roman town buried by Vesuvius in AD 79, so 'Classical city' places it by period as well as by kind.

  • Greek character → PI pi is the Greek letter mathematicians use for the circle constant
  • I mope → OMPEI five letters rearranged and dropped into the gap
  • about → anagram indicator
  • wherein — Wherein means inside which, so the letters named next are set within the piece just given rather than written after it.
  • about — Moving about is moving with no settled order, so the two words beside it are re-set in a fresh arrangement.

A tourist sulking somewhere in Greece.

'Greek character' is not a person but a letter, which is the pun hiding the outer shell — and the city it wraps is Italian, not Greek at all.

23 across NOTIONS

Fancies new liniments, scratching head (7)

Definition: Fancies · charade + deletion

Notions are passing ideas taken up on a whim, which is what fancies are when they are things thought rather than things desired.

  • new → N n = new, the standard abbreviation
  • liniments → LOTIONS a liniment is a rubbing lotion for aching muscles
  • scratching head → the opening L is rubbed out, leaving six letters
  • scratching head — The head of a word is its first letter, and scratching it means rubbing that letter out before the rest is used.

Somebody scratching their head over which new rubbing ointment to buy.

'Scratching head' works twice over: it is the puzzled gesture in the picture and, in the wordplay, the instruction to rub a first letter out.

24 across DONATELLO

Artist’s party welcoming concerning amateur archer (9)

Definition: Artist · container + charade

Donatello was the Florentine sculptor of the bronze David and one of the founding figures of the Renaissance, so 'Artist' names him by trade.

  • party → DO a do is a party in British usage — a works do, a birthday do
  • concerning → ON a book on birds is a book concerning birds
  • amateur → A a = amateur, the standard abbreviation
  • archer → TELL William Tell shot the apple off his son's head
  • welcoming — Welcoming somebody is taking them into your house, so the word beside it opens up and the rest of the wordplay goes inside.

A painter's party making room for a keen weekend bowman.

'Archer' wants to be the definition and is wordplay instead, and it points at a Swiss folk hero rather than at anybody holding a bow — Tell is the setters' standard archer.

25 across ENTER

With no entrance, tenant to go in (5)

Definition: to go in · deletion

To enter a room is to go into it, so this is the plainest possible synonym — the same act stated in three words instead of one.

  • tenant → RENTER somebody who rents a flat is a renter, and a tenant is exactly that
  • With no entrance → the letter at the front is removed, leaving five
  • With no entrance — An entrance is the way in, so the letter standing at the way into the word — the first one — is the letter taken away.

A flat with no front door, and a tenant expected to get inside somehow.

'Entrance' does the disguising: a doorway on the surface, the front of a word in the wordplay. 'Tenant' looks like the definition right up to the moment you count the letters.

26 across PREY

Loudly plead for Merlin’s victim? (4)

Definition: Merlin’s victim · homophone

A merlin is a small falcon, and what a hawk hunts and kills is its prey — the wizard in the surface is the decoy.

  • plead → PREY to plead with heaven is to pray, and it is that word said aloud that gets written down
  • Loudly — Said out loud a word is only a sound, and this one turns out to be spelt a different way when it is written down.

Somebody begging noisily for mercy from a wizard.

Merlin is a wizard on the surface and a bird in the parse: the merlin is a compact falcon, and the capital letter is doing all the misdirecting.

27 across DOWN-AT-HEEL

Poor – and potentially sole-destroying? (4-2-4)

Definition: Poor · double definition

Down-at-heel means shabby and hard up, and shoes worn down at the heel are on their way through the sole — the pun being on soul-destroying.

  • Poor → the everyday sense: shabby, worn, short of money
  • potentially sole-destroying → the literal sense: heels that have gone take the bottom of the shoe with them

Hard times described as bad news for your footwear.

'Sole-destroying' is a pun on soul-destroying, so the second definition arrives disguised as a complaint about how demoralising being broke is.

Down

1 down ON TIME

Somewhat upfront? I’m early! Not late, anyway … (2,4)

Definition: Not late · hidden word

Arriving on time is arriving at the appointed moment, which is not being late said from the other side.

  • upfront? I’m early → ONTIME six consecutive letters, beginning partway into the first word and running on across two of the gaps
  • anyway … → surface only — the shrug that keeps the sentence sounding spoken, with the ellipsis carrying it into the next clue
  • Somewhat — Somewhat means partly rather than wholly, so only a portion of what follows is wanted — a run of letters inside the words, not the words themselves.

Somebody protesting, a little too hard, that they turned up ahead of schedule.

'Somewhat' is the one word in the sentence with no business in a conversation about punctuality, and that oddity is what marks it as the instruction.

2 down EVEN SO

Yet, really – knight to take place of queen? (4,2)

Definition: Yet · substitution

Even so and yet both concede the point just made and then push straight past it: 'it rained; even so, we went' works as 'yet we went'.

  • really → EVERSO 'ever so' is the everyday intensifier — ever so kind, ever so sorry
  • knight → N N is the knight in chess notation, K having gone to the king
  • to take place of queen — R is the queen in a monarch's shorthand, Regina, and taking her place means the new letter is written exactly where that R stood.

A chess player wondering whether the knight can be moved onto the square the queen is standing on.

R for Regina is worth keeping: queens and kings both hand over R in crossword shorthand, taken from the Latin printed on the coins.

3 down SHORT AND SWEET

Ill-tempered; also ingratiating – thankfully concise (5,3,5)

Definition: thankfully concise · charade

Short and sweet describes something mercifully brief and pleasant with it, so the relief in 'thankfully' is part of the phrase's meaning rather than decoration.

  • Ill-tempered → SHORT being short with somebody is being curt and snappish
  • also → AND both words do nothing but add the next thing on
  • ingratiating → SWEET sweet in the sense of sugary charm laid on to please

A character sketch of somebody snappish one minute and oily the next, delivered briefly.

The punctuation is the disguise: a semicolon and a dash make three unrelated synonyms look like one continuous description of a person's manner.

4 down SANDPIPER

Bird generally dull in appearance? That’s rough, as Aussie tells it (9)

Definition: Bird generally dull in appearance · homophone

Sandpipers are the small shoreline waders got up in browns and greys, so 'generally dull in appearance' is a fair sketch of the whole family.

  • That’s rough → SANDPIPER the rough stuff you rub bare wood down with is sandpaper, and that is the word being said aloud
  • as Aussie tells it — An Australian broadens the vowel in words like paper and mate, so a word said in that accent lands on a sound English spells another way.

Somebody sympathising about a tough time, in a broad Australian accent.

Australian vowels are the whole mechanism: 'mate' comes out as 'mite' down there, and the same shift turns a hardware-shop abrasive into a wading bird.

5 down MIKES

Audio kit invariably preceding Novembers? (5)

Definition: Audio kit · double definition

Mikes are microphones, the working name for them in any studio; and in the NATO spelling alphabet Mike is the letter before November every single time.

  • Audio kit → the studio sense: the things singers and broadcasters talk into
  • invariably preceding Novembers → the radio-alphabet sense: M comes before N, so the word standing for M always comes before the word standing for N

A stage manager checking that the sound gear is always in place before the autumn shows.

The NATO alphabet is the second definition: pilots and police say Mike for M and November for N. 'Invariably' is the word telling you a fixed rule is being quoted rather than a coincidence.

7 down APERITIF

After outburst (anger), Dad served up drinkie (8)

Definition: drinkie · charade + reversal

An aperitif is the small drink taken before dinner to sharpen the appetite, and 'drinkie' is the coy diminutive for exactly that sort of glass.

  • outburst → FIT a fit is a sudden outburst of temper
  • anger → IRE ire is anger, and the setters' favourite three letters for it
  • Dad → PA pa is the familiar word for a father
  • served up — Serving something up presents it back to you the other way about, and in a down entry that means the assembled letters are read from the bottom upwards.

Dad, having lost his temper, pouring the drinks out afterwards.

The bracketed '(anger)' looks like the setter glossing the word in front of it, and it is a separate piece of wordplay with three letters of its own.

8 down FUSELAGE

Weld, insulate and earth aeroplane’s body (8)

Definition: aeroplane’s body · charade

The fuselage is the long central tube of an aircraft, carrying crew and cargo with the wings bolted to it — an aeroplane's body exactly.

  • Weld → FUSE to fuse two metals is to weld them into one
  • insulate → LAG in Britain you lag a hot-water tank or a pipe — wrap it against the cold
  • earth → E E is the earth terminal marked inside a plug

A list of workshop instructions for somebody assembling something in a hangar.

'Lag' is worth learning for good: British setters reach for it whenever insulation appears in a clue, and it never means anything else.

11 down DOWNING STREET

Drinkin’ G&T in which a little slice (lime?), where the powerful are (7,6)

Definition: where the powerful are · charade + container + first letter

Downing Street is the short London street holding the Prime Minister's house at Number 10, so the name stands for British government itself.

  • Drinkin’ → DOWNIN downing a glass is drinking it, and the setter drops the same final g the surface drops
  • G&T → GT the gin and tonic hands over two letters, and they end up prised apart
  • slice → S only the very front of the word is taken
  • lime → TREE a lime is a tree as well as a fruit, the question mark flagging one example standing for the class
  • in which — In which points inwards: whatever is named next goes within the letters just handed over, not after them.
  • a little — A little of a word is not all of it, and the little you take from the front of a word is its opening letter.

A gin and tonic being knocked back, complete with the little slice of lime floating in it.

'Lime' is the pun everything rests on — the fruit in the glass, the tree in the wordplay — and the G and the T finish up on either side of the whole assembly.

15 down ELTON JOHN

Pop Idol Jack in trashed hotel, sonny, stripped off (5,4)

Definition: Pop Idol · anagram + deletion

Elton John has been a superstar of British pop for fifty years, so 'Pop Idol' names him by category and the italics only dress it as the talent show.

  • Jack → J the jack in a pack of cards is written J
  • hotel → HOTEL five letters handed over whole
  • sonny → ONN the outside letters come off and three are left in the mix
  • trashed → anagram indicator
  • trashed — Trashing a room leaves nothing where it was, so the letters gathered here come out in a different order.
  • stripped off — Stripping off takes away what is on the outside, so the word named loses its first and last letters.

A rowdy night in which a lad called Jack wrecks a hotel room and loses his clothes.

The italics point at the television series and that is the decoy — the two words are doing duty as a plain description of a singer. Watch 'sonny': it is the only fodder that arrives shortened.

16 down HEAPED UP

Gathered that man, clumsy fellow, to be given upside-down cake? (6,2)

Definition: Gathered · charade + reversal

Something heaped up has been piled into a mound, which is what gathered means when it is things rather than people being brought together.

  • that man → HE the pronoun for a man already mentioned
  • clumsy fellow → APE calling somebody a great ape is calling them a lumbering oaf
  • cake → DUP pud is the British shortening of pudding, the dessert course a cake can serve as, and it is turned round
  • upside-down — Turning a thing upside-down puts its bottom at the top, so the short word beside it is written out in reverse.

A clumsy man being handed a slice of pineapple upside-down cake.

Upside-down cake is a real thing — the pineapple sort baked in an inverted tin — and that is what makes the two words look like one noun rather than an instruction and the word it operates on.

17 down DIAMANTE

Did Amy and Ted never stop getting bling? (8)

Definition: bling · charade + deletion

Diamanté is the sparkling paste jewellery stitched onto frocks in place of real stones, which is the flashy sham that 'bling' names.

  • Did → DI the final letter is never reached, so two letters arrive instead of three
  • Amy → AM the same again — the last letter is dropped before the name is used
  • and → AN the little joining word also loses its final letter
  • Ted → TE the last letter goes here too, and the entry is full
  • never stop — A word that never stops never reaches its ending, so each of the four here is cut off before its final letter.

A gossipy question about whether two people ever stopped buying flashy jewellery.

Four short words in a row, each losing its tail, is a shape worth recognising: all four are three letters long and the entry is eight, which is the arithmetic that gives it away.

19 down BOTTLE

Courage? That’s not on tap (6)

Definition: Courage · double definition

Bottle is British slang for nerve — losing your bottle is losing your nerve — and beer that is not drawn from a tap is served in one.

  • Courage → the slang sense: the nerve to go through with something frightening
  • not on tap → the pub sense: beer sold sealed in glass rather than pulled from the pump

A drinker being told the good stuff is not available on draught tonight.

Courage is a long-established British brewery as well as a virtue, which is why the surface reads as a bar conversation while the definition is talking about nerve.

20 down ASTRAL

Clip of broadcast, Rally of the Stars (6)

Definition: of the Stars · hidden word

Astral means belonging to the stars — astral bodies, astral navigation — so the phrase is the adjective's plainest sense.

  • broadcast, Rally → ASTRAL six letters in a row, beginning inside the first word and finishing inside the second
  • Clip of — A clip is a short piece cut out of something longer, so what is wanted is a run of letters taken from inside the words that follow.

A trailer for a televised motor race with a celebrity line-up.

The italics make 'Rally of the Stars' look like the title of a programme, so the eye takes it as one unit and skims straight over the join where the letters sit.

22 down IGLOO

Where a house-warming will get you a frosty reception (5)

Definition: Where a house-warming will get you a frosty reception · cryptic definition

An igloo is a dome of snow blocks, so warming the house would melt it, and a frosty welcome there is nothing but the temperature.

  • a house-warming → the surface sense: the party you throw when you move in
  • a frosty reception → the surface sense is a cold welcome from the neighbours; the setter means the walls, which are built out of the cold itself

New neighbours throwing a party and finding nobody much wants to come.

Both halves of the phrase turn literal at the same moment: warming the house really would be a disaster, and the reception really is frosty because the building is made of snow.
