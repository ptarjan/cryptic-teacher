Across

1 across OFF-THE-WALL

Eccentric newspaper hack everyone follows about (3-3-4)

Definition: Eccentric · charade

Eccentric → OFF-THE-WALL: the idiom means wildly unconventional, as though bouncing away from any straight line, and it is everyday English rather than a crossword-only sense.

  • about → OF a book about birds is a book of birds, so the two words swap in this sense
  • newspaper → FT the Financial Times, whose initials are crosswordland's favourite paper
  • hack → HEW to hack at a log is to hew it
  • everyone → ALL everyone present is all of them
  • follows — 'follows' is a placement instruction rather than a description: the pieces named before it queue up behind the short word at the end of the clue.

The surface hands you 'newspaper hack' as one job title, and the parse splits it into two unrelated pieces: a paper's initials and a verb about chopping wood.

7 across FAWN

Appear keen to serve venison one day? (4)

Definition: Appear keen to serve · double definition

Both senses of one word: to fawn is to grovel for favour, and a fawn is a young deer — the meat it will become is the question mark's joke.

  • Appear keen to serve → the verb: a courtier or a dog at your feet, showing exaggerated eagerness to please
  • venison one day? → the noun: an animal defined by what it will grow into rather than by what it is now

The two senses are unrelated in origin, which is what makes the pairing land — a servile courtier and a young deer have nothing in common but four letters.

9 across PET-SIT

Watch dog, say, beginning to splash through wee in Paris (3-3)

Definition: Watch dog, say · container + first letter

Watch dog, say → PET-SIT: to pet-sit is to mind someone's animal while they are away, and the 'say' offers a dog as one example of such an animal.

  • wee in Paris → PETIT 'petit' is French for small, and Paris is the instruction to say it in French
  • beginning to splash → S the first letter of the word that follows
  • beginning to — 'beginning to' points at where a word starts rather than at what it means, and a word starts with exactly one letter.
  • through — 'through' is passage right into the middle, so the letter is planted inside the other piece instead of tacked on an end.

'Wee' is the hinge of the joke: the surface wants the toilet sense, with the dog and the splash to help, while the wordplay wants the Scots sense, small.

10 across TOLERANT

Patient alert, not wandering (8)

Definition: Patient · anagram

Patient → TOLERANT: both describe someone who puts up with irritation without complaining, and the word is an adjective in each case, not a noun.

  • alert, not → ALERTNOT eight letters lifted straight from the clue in the order the setter printed them
  • wandering — 'wandering' is straying off any fixed route, and letters that stray will not stay in the order they were given.

The surface reads like a note on a hospital chart. 'Patient' is the definition and an adjective there, not a person in a bed, which is the whole misdirection.

11 across ANNE OF CLEVES

Spooner's family all named after original matriarch, a queen (4,2,6)

Definition: a queen · spoonerism

Definition by example: she was Henry VIII's fourth wife, and so queen consort of England for the six months that the marriage lasted.

  • family all named after original matriarch → CLAN OF EVES a family is a clan, and everyone sharing the name of the first woman in Genesis is one of the Eves
  • Spooner's → ANNE OF CLEVES say that phrase, then move the CL from the front of the first word to the front of the last
  • Spooner's — the Reverend Spooner was famous for swapping the opening sounds of two words, and his name in a clue is the instruction to do it.

Spoonerisms are worked backwards: build the innocent phrase the clue describes first, then shuffle its opening sounds and see what famous name falls out.

13 across SAFARI

Hunting trip mostly free of risk and very dry (6)

Definition: Hunting trip · charade + deletion

Hunting trip → SAFARI: Swahili for a journey, the word entered English specifically for the big-game expedition, so the hunting sense is its original one.

  • free of risk → SAF a four-letter word for out of danger, docked of its last letter
  • very dry → ARI a four-letter word for parched land, docked the same way
  • mostly — 'mostly' means all but a little, and the little you leave out is the final letter — here it does that job to two separate words.

One indicator doing two jobs is the thing to spot here: 'mostly' is placed early and reaches over the 'and' to trim both halves alike.

14 across ON STRIKE

Facing out (2,6)

Definition: Facing · double definition

In cricket the batter receiving the bowling is on strike, so is facing; workers who have downed tools and walked out are on strike too.

  • Facing → the cricket sense: of the two batters at the wicket, the one the bowler is aiming at
  • out → the industrial sense: a workforce that has stopped work is said to be out

Two words, two definitions, no wordplay at all. Short clues like this are often the hardest, because there is nothing to take apart and no indicator to find.

16 across LAGRANGE

Mathematician telephoned Euler's number after delay (8)

Definition: Mathematician · charade

Mathematician → LAGRANGE: definition by example, Joseph-Louis Lagrange being an eighteenth-century giant of mechanics and number theory.

  • delay → LAG a lag is a delay between two things
  • telephoned → RANG to telephone somebody is to ring them
  • Euler's number → E e is the constant behind natural logarithms, about 2.718
  • after — 'after' is a positional instruction: the two pieces named before it sit behind the one word named at the end.

A whole mathematical constant standing in for a single letter is a trick worth banking — 'Euler's number' looks like a fact to know and is really one character.

18 across RAGING

Stormy piece by Joplin with one sharp? (6)

Definition: Stormy · charade

Stormy → RAGING: a raging storm is the stock phrase, and on its own the word describes weather at its most violent pitch.

  • piece by Joplin → RAG Scott Joplin wrote ragtime, and one ragtime composition is a rag
  • with one sharp? → ING a key signature of a single sharp is G major, so such a piece is written in G

'Joplin' wants to be Janis and is Scott. The question mark then flags the key signature as a roundabout way of naming one particular note.

20 across SEQUESTRATED

Hunt traitor with likely winner outside kept apart (12)

Definition: kept apart · container + charade

kept apart → SEQUESTRATED: to sequestrate is to seize property and hold it aside, so what has been sequestrated is shut away from whoever owned it.

  • Hunt → QUEST as a noun, a hunt for something is a quest
  • traitor → RAT to rat on someone is to betray them, so a rat is the betrayer
  • likely winner → SEED a seed in tennis is a ranked player expected to go far in the draw
  • outside — 'outside' puts one piece around the others, which means its own letters split so that some lead and the rest follow.

'Hunt' opens the clue with a capital letter and a verb's shape, which is the misdirection: it is a noun, and it is not the definition.

23 across JOVIALLY

Royal Marine crosses Roman road in high spirits (8)

Definition: in high spirits · container

in high spirits → JOVIALLY: jovial comes from Jove, whose planet was held to make people merry, so to act jovially is to act cheerfully.

  • Royal Marine → JOLLY a jolly is old naval slang for a Royal Marine
  • Roman road → VIA via is simply Latin for road, and 'Roman' tells you which language to use
  • crosses — 'crosses' means goes over the top of, so one piece lies across the other and has to open up to let it through.

Both halves are vocabulary rather than mechanism, which is why this one is either instant or impossible: you either know the naval slang or you do not.

24 across GLAZED

Was an idler after golf given coat then fired? (6)

Definition: given coat then fired? · charade

given coat then fired? → GLAZED: pottery is glazed by brushing on a coat of glaze and baking it in a kiln, which is both halves at once.

  • golf → G G in the NATO alphabet, the same table that gives India and Whiskey
  • Was an idler → LAZED the past tense is doing real work — it is what makes the tense of the answer come out right
  • after — 'after' is positional: what it introduces goes in front, so the single letter ends up leading and the longer word trails it.

The question mark is the warning label: 'fired' here means a kiln rather than a dismissal, and 'coat' is a layer of liquid rather than something you wear.

25 across ISLE

In centre of Paris, level area for Notre-Dame? (4)

Definition: area for Notre-Dame? · hidden word

area for Notre-Dame? → ISLE: the cathedral was built on an island in the Seine, so the question mark flags one particular one standing for the class.

  • Paris, level → ISLE four letters in a row, straddling both the comma and the space between the two words
  • In centre of — 'In centre of' points at where letters sit rather than at what words mean, and the middle of the stretch that follows is where to look.

Punctuation is the disguise. A concealed answer that runs across a comma is far harder to see than one buried inside a single long word.

26 across DELAMINATE

Peel apart, eat lime and bananas (10)

Definition: Peel apart · anagram

Peel apart → DELAMINATE: a laminate is built from bonded layers, so to delaminate is for those layers to come unstuck and lift away from each other.

  • eat lime and → EATLIMEAND ten letters taken straight from the clue, in the order they are printed
  • bananas — 'bananas' means driven out of your mind, and letters sent out of their minds will not keep the places they were in.

The surface is a fruit bowl and 'Peel' plays along with it. It is the definition, and a verb — the fruit is only there to be shuffled.

Down

2 down FLEX

Move supply of iron by crossing lake (4)

Definition: Move supply · charade + container

Move supply → FLEX: 'supply' is the adverb of 'supple', so the definition asks for movement that bends and gives, which is what flexing is.

  • iron → FE the chemical symbol for the metal
  • by → X three by four is written 3 x 4, so 'by' is the multiplication sign
  • lake → L the standard map abbreviation
  • crossing — 'crossing' means passing right through something, so the letter it introduces ends up inside the other piece instead of alongside it.

The trap is 'supply of iron', which reads as a delivery of metal. Split it the other way: 'supply' is an adverb, and the definition stops right there.

3 down TOSTADA

Fried tortilla drunk flipped with cry of accomplishment (7)

Definition: Fried tortilla · reversal + charade

Fried tortilla → TOSTADA: in Mexican cooking a tostada is precisely that, a tortilla fried flat and crisp so that toppings can be piled on it.

  • drunk → SOT a sot is a habitual soak, and here the word is a noun, not an instruction
  • cry of accomplishment → TADA ta-da is what you shout when you produce something with a flourish
  • flipped — 'flipped' means turned over the way you turn a pancake, so the three letters it governs are read from their far end back.

'Drunk' sits next to a food word and looks exactly like an anagram signal. It is a noun instead, and the only thing done to it is a turn.

4 down EXTENSION

E.g. more time for homework available after turning sixteen (9)

Definition: E.g. more time for homework · anagram + charade

E.g. more time for homework → EXTENSION: an extension is any granted lengthening of a deadline, and the 'e.g.' flags a school deadline as one instance of that.

  • sixteen → SIXTEEN seven letters handed over as the number is spelled out
  • available → ON an offer that is on is one that is available
  • turning — 'turning' here is what a birthday does — you turn sixteen and become something else — so the letters it governs come out as something else too.
  • after — 'after' is positional: the short piece it introduces sits behind the shuffled letters rather than in front of them.

'Turning sixteen' is a whole idiom about birthdays, which is why it reads as scene-setting rather than as an instruction. Spelling the number out is the move.

5 down ASTRONOMER ROYAL

A starry role, with moon involved? (10,5)

Definition: A starry role, with moon involved? · &lit + anagram

The whole clue reads straight: the post is a senior crown appointment in astronomy, so its holder's work genuinely is stars and moon, and the question mark flags the whimsy.

  • A starry role → ASTARRYROLE eleven letters taken straight off the front of the clue
  • moon → MOON four more letters joining the pile before it is shuffled
  • involved — 'involved' means tangled up with something, and letters tangled together do not come out in the order they went in.

Every word does double duty here: read once it describes the job, read again it is fifteen letters and an instruction. That is what &lit means.

6 down LILAC

Shade of flowering tree in India, during visit to north (5)

Definition: Shade of flowering tree · container + reversal

Shade of flowering tree → LILAC: the pale mauve colour is named after the blossom of the shrub, so the plant's name doubles as the name of its own colour.

  • visit → CALL a visit is a call — you pay someone a call
  • India → I India is I in the NATO alphabet
  • during — 'during' means in the middle of a stretch, so the letter it introduces lands inside the other piece rather than beside it.
  • to north — north is up the page, and this entry runs down it, so the letters travel against the grid and are read the other way round.

The comma after 'India' is the misdirection. The country is not where the tree grows, and 'visit' is a noun rather than the verb the sentence wants.

7 down FORBEAR

Avoid terror gripping globe (7)

Definition: Avoid · container

Avoid → FORBEAR: to forbear is to hold back from an action, which is how you avoid something by choosing simply not to do it.

  • terror → FEAR terror is fear at full strength
  • globe → ORB an orb is any sphere, and the globe is the sphere we live on
  • gripping — 'gripping' is holding tight in a closed fist, so the piece doing the gripping shuts around whatever is named next.

'Gripping terror' is a phrase you have read a hundred times, which is exactly why the eye slides over it and misses that only one of those words is the container.

8 down WUNDERKIND

Women given orders by nice young wizard (10)

Definition: young wizard · charade

young wizard → WUNDERKIND: German for 'wonder child', it names someone brilliant at a thing while still very young, with 'wizard' in its whiz-at-something sense.

  • Women → W W is the standard abbreviation, as on a lavatory door
  • given orders by → UNDER you are under whoever gives you your orders
  • nice → KIND a plain synonym in the sense of good-natured

'Wizard' is not a robed spellcaster but the everyday sense, a wizard with numbers. The orders in the clue are the ones you take, not the ones you give.

12 down CADAVEROUS

Gaunt head of operations keeping rogue state and America apart (10)

Definition: Gaunt · charade

Gaunt → CADAVEROUS: a cadaver is a corpse, so cadaverous describes a face so hollow and fleshless that it looks like one.

  • rogue → CAD a cad is an old British word for a man who behaves dishonourably
  • state → AVER to aver something is to state it formally, as a witness does
  • head of operations → O the head of a word is its first letter
  • America → US the two-letter abbreviation for the United States
  • keeping — 'keeping' is holding things in position, which gives the single letter just named a job: it holds the two larger pieces where they are.
  • apart — 'apart' means not touching, so the two pieces either side of it must have something wedged between them.

'Rogue state' reads as one phrase straight off a news bulletin, and that is the misdirection: the two words are clued separately and never belong together.

15 down STRATAGEM

Plan huge upset after kick-off, moving right up (9)

Definition: Plan · charade + anagram + reversal

Plan → STRATAGEM: a stratagem is a plan with cunning in it, a scheme for outwitting somebody, rather than a neutral arrangement of steps.

  • kick-off → START the kick-off is the start of a match
  • huge → MEGA mega is the prefix for enormous, and on its own it is slang for the same
  • upset — 'upset' reads as set up, tipped over, so the piece it governs is read from the bottom of this down entry towards the top.
  • moving right up — 'right' names the letter R and 'up' names the direction, and up a down entry means earlier, so that one letter shifts a place towards the top.

The surface is a football result, and 'upset' looks like an anagram signal aimed at 'huge'. It is not: 'huge' is a word to be turned, and only one letter changes places.

17 down ROUTINE

Turn money into Euro abroad (7)

Definition: Turn · container + anagram

Turn → ROUTINE: a performer's turn is their routine, the set piece they go on and do — a theatrical sense of the noun, not rotation.

  • Euro → ROUE the currency's four letters, spread into a new order
  • money → TIN tin is old British slang for cash
  • into — 'into' names a destination rather than a sum of money, so the piece before it is planted inside the piece after it.
  • abroad — 'abroad' means scattered far and wide, so the four letters it governs are spread out into a different order.

'Turn' wants to be an instruction to reverse something. It is the definition instead, in the music-hall sense: a single act on a variety bill.

19 down GODDAMN

Hateful gin has one turning to rum in the morning (7)

Definition: Hateful · substitution + charade

Hateful → GODDAMN: used as an adjective it is what you spit at something you detest, so it carries exactly that loathing rather than mere emphasis.

  • gin → GIN the spirit, spelled out — these are the letters the swap happens inside
  • one → I the Roman numeral, and the letter that is about to leave
  • rum → ODD rum is an old British adjective meaning strange or peculiar
  • in the morning → AM the half of the clock before noon
  • turning to — 'turning to' is a change of allegiance: whatever it names first walks out, and whatever it names next takes over that place.

Two spirits in a row make the surface look like a drinks order, and only the first is a drink in the wordplay — the second is an adjective, the third a numeral.

21 down SOLID

Incredibly, cap's all made of the same material (5)

Definition: all made of the same material · charade

all made of the same material → SOLID: solid gold means gold right through rather than plated, so this is the uniform-substance sense, not the hard-not-soft one.

  • Incredibly → SO 'incredibly hot' just means 'hot' with an intensifier, and this is the shortest one
  • cap → LID the cap on a jar is its lid

'Incredibly' at the head of a clue, with a comma after it, looks like an anagram signal. Here it is plain fodder: a two-letter synonym is all it hands over.

22 down BEAT

Attend course for police (4)

Definition: course for police · charade

course for police → BEAT: a constable's beat is the fixed round they walk, so the ground itself takes its name from the pacing that covers it.

  • Attend → BE to attend a thing is to be present at it, and this is the verb that phrase opens with
  • Attend → AT the second half of that same two-word phrase — you attend something by being at it

A four-letter answer with only three words of clue: 'Attend' is not a definition at all but a single word that unpacks into a two-word phrase.
