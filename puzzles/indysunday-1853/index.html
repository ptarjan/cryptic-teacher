1 across SNAP UP

Eagerly buy something worthless on back of tin (4,2)

Definition: Eagerly buy · charade

Eagerly buy -> SNAP UP: to snap something up is to buy it fast, before anyone else can, with the eagerness built into the phrase.

  • tin → SN the chemical symbol for tin, element 50 on the periodic table
  • something worthless → APUP to be sold a pup is to be fobbed off with a worthless thing, and the idiom keeps its article
  • on back of — Riding on the back of something means coming along behind it, so the worthless item's letters trail the metal rather than lead it.

A shopper keenly buying a bit of junk stuck to the back of a can.

'Tin' is the metal rather than the container, and metals reach cryptics as their periodic-table symbols. The pup is the swindler's classic worthless goods.

5 across POSITION

New tipi soon standing (8)

Definition: standing · anagram

standing -> POSITION: both are nouns for the rank or status someone holds, as in a position of authority or standing in the community.

  • tipi soon → TIPISOON anagram fodder, eight letters
  • New → anagram indicator
  • New — New means made afresh, and letters remade from scratch have no obligation to come back in the order they were handed over.

A freshly pitched tipi that will soon be up and standing on the campsite.

'Standing' reads as the tent staying upright and means status instead - the rank someone holds in the world.

9 across GOLF COURSE

Holes beginning to grow naturally around lake (4,6)

Definition: Holes · container + charade + first letter

Holes -> GOLF COURSE: a course is its eighteen holes taken together, so the setter names the place by the only feature that counts on it.

  • beginning to grow → G the first letter of grow, and nothing more of it
  • naturally → OFCOURSE naturally means of course, and the phrase opens up after its first letter
  • lake → L L is the map-maker's short form for a lake
  • beginning to — The beginning of a word is its first letter, and 'beginning to grow' asks for no more of that word than its start.
  • around — What goes around something encloses it, so the letters named before this close over the lake instead of sitting beside it.

Holes that have started to appear of their own accord in the ground round a lake.

'Naturally' looks like an adverb describing how the holes are spreading, and it is a two-word phrase waiting to be spelled out in full.

10 across NEED

Want massage from lecturer? (4)

Definition: Want · homophone

Want -> NEED: both name what you lack and must have, though need carries more urgency than a mere wish does.

  • massage → NEED to massage dough or a stiff shoulder is to knead it, and the opening K goes silent as soon as you say the word
  • from lecturer? — A lecturer delivers everything out loud, so a word arriving from one reaches you as a sound and may be spelled quite differently.

Fancying a massage, and the unlikely thought of getting one from your lecturer.

The silent K is the whole trick. 'Want' works as noun or verb, and so does the answer, so the definition fits whichever way you read the clue's first word.

11 across CHELSEA BOOTS

Elasticated foot warmers from chemist on King's Road? (7,5)

Definition: Elasticated foot warmers · charade

Elasticated foot warmers -> CHELSEA BOOTS: ankle boots with an elastic gusset at each side, which is what lets you pull them on without laces.

  • King's Road? → CHELSEA the King's Road is the long shopping street that runs through Chelsea in west London
  • chemist → BOOTS Boots is the chemist on nearly every British high street
  • on — A shop described as on a road stands along it, so in an across entry the chemist's letters take up position after the road's.

Stretchy footwear bought at the chemist's shop on the King's Road.

Both pieces are proper names dressed as plain description, a shop and a street, and the footwear took its name from that same corner of London.

13 across TUNDRA

Cold plain fish sandwiches outside for docker (6)

Definition: Cold plain · container + outer letters

Cold plain -> TUNDRA: the treeless Arctic flat where the subsoil never thaws, which is a cold plain and little else.

  • fish → TUNA tuna is the fish, and the four letters open up before the last
  • docker → DR the first and last letters of docker, the pair on the outside
  • sandwiches — To sandwich something is to put it between two slices, so the fish closes over the letters named next.
  • outside — The outside of a word is its first and last letters, and that pair is all this hands over.

Plain fish sandwiches served cold and outdoors to a man working the docks.

'Sandwiches' is the verb, not the lunch, and 'Cold plain' is two words of a single definition rather than a description of the food.

14 across LITERARY

Writer's arty novel rarely containing sex? (8)

Definition: Writer's arty · container + anagram

Writer's arty -> LITERARY: the adjective for writing pursued as art, which is what a writer's arty side amounts to.

  • rarely → LERARY the six letters of RARELY resettled, and they open up after the first
  • sex? → IT 'it' is the old coy word for sex and for sex appeal, as in a girl who has it
  • novel — Novel means new, and letters made new are letters that no longer stand in the order they arrived in.
  • containing — What contains something holds it within, so the two letters named next go inside the shuffled six rather than after them.

A writer's arty new book that hardly ever has any sex in it.

'Novel' is the noun everywhere in this sentence except in the wordplay, where it is the adjective meaning new.

16 across BLOOMERS

Joey's father's packing large knickers (8)

Definition: knickers · container

knickers -> BLOOMERS: the loose gathered drawers Victorian women wore, and still the joke word for voluminous knickers.

  • Joey's father's → BOOMERS a joey is a young kangaroo and the adult male of the species is a boomer; the possessive s supplies the last letter
  • large → L L is the clothing size, as printed on the label
  • packing — Packing something means stowing it inside, so the letter named next slots into the word doing the packing.

Joey's dad packing a large pair of underwear for the trip.

'Joey' looks like a boy's name and is a kangaroo cub; boomer for the adult male is Australian usage rather than crossword convention.

19 across OODLES

Lots love line in poetry (6)

Definition: Lots · charade + container

Lots -> OODLES: oodles is the informal word for a great quantity, and like lots it takes a plural verb after it.

  • love → O love is nil in tennis, and nil is written as a nought
  • poetry → ODES odes are poetry, and the four letters open up after the second
  • line → L L is the short form of line, as on a railway map
  • in poetry — A poem is made of lines, so a line that is in poetry goes inside those letters rather than alongside them.

Plenty of people love a good line of verse.

'Lots love' reads as a crowd of admirers, and each of those words is doing another job: one is the definition, the other a nought.

21 across BOOGIE NIGHTS

Show disapproval of wobbly thingies on German porn film (6,6)

Definition: porn film · charade + anagram

porn film -> BOOGIE NIGHTS: definition by example, the 1997 picture about the seventies porn industry, so one particular film rather than the type.

  • Show disapproval of → BOO an audience shows disapproval by booing
  • German → G G is the standard short form for German, as on a school timetable
  • wobbly thingies → IENIGHTS the eight letters of THINGIES refuse to stay put and settle in this order
  • wobbly — Something wobbly will not hold its shape, and letters that will not hold their shape will not hold their order.
  • on — In an across entry one thing standing on another is written after it, so the shuffled letters follow the German.

Booing the wobbly bits on show in a German adult film.

Boogie Nights is Paul Thomas Anderson's 1997 film about the Californian porn trade, so the definition is a title and not a description of a genre.

24 across DIVA

Princess up against a woman that acts like one (4)

Definition: woman that acts like one · charade

woman that acts like one -> DIVA: a diva is a star who carries on with the grandeur of a princess and expects to be treated as one.

  • Princess → DI Princess Diana was Di in every headline of the eighties and nineties
  • up against → V two sides up against each other are written with a v between them, short for versus
  • a → A the word taken as the single letter it is

A princess squaring up to a woman with airs of her own.

'One' at the end points back at the princess, so the definition is a woman who behaves as though she were royalty.

25 across IMPECCABLE

Free of sin, one's fit to be kissed in audience (10)

Definition: Free of sin · charade + homophone

Free of sin -> IMPECCABLE: literally not able to sin, and now used of anything faultless - manners, timing, a performance.

  • one's → IM one's is I am, which shortens to two letters
  • fit to be kissed → PECCABLE a peck is a quick kiss, so anything fit for one is peckable
  • in audience — An audience takes everything in by ear, so the phrase before it is wanted as a sound and comes out spelled another way.

Being sinless, and worth a kiss in front of everybody watching.

The word you hear is a real one: peccable means liable to sin, from Latin peccare, so the negative prefix on the front of it means exactly what the definition says.

26 across DEADBEAT

Bum numb? Perhaps cane ... (8)

Definition: Bum · charade

Bum -> DEADBEAT: a deadbeat is an idle sponger who never pays their way, which is what bum means in American usage.

  • numb → DEAD a numb limb is dead - you feel nothing in it
  • Perhaps cane → BEAT 'perhaps' marks definition by example: caning is one way of beating somebody

Someone asking whether a numbed backside calls for a caning.

'Bum' is the definition and the joke at once: the American noun for a loafer sitting next to the British one for a backside.

27 across EN DASH

... mark remains on bottom (2,4)

Definition: mark · charade

mark -> EN DASH: a punctuation mark one en wide, the dash printers use for ranges such as 1914-18.

  • bottom → END the bottom of a list is its end
  • remains → ASH what remains of a fire is ash
  • on — In an across entry what sits on something else is written after it, so the remains follow the bottom.

The mark left at the bottom where something has burned away.

The two pieces break one letter later than the answer does, which is what makes them so hard to see. An en is the printer's width of a letter N, and it names this dash.

2 down NOON

Turning point for the sun going up and down (4)

Definition: Turning point for the sun · palindrome

Turning point for the sun -> NOON: the sun stops climbing at midday and starts down again, so noon is literally where its course turns.

  • Turning point for the sun → the sun climbs all morning and drops all afternoon, so its turning point is one moment in the middle of the day
  • going up and down → this is the whole of the wordplay - the four letters must read identically from either end, so the outer pair matches and the inner pair matches
  • going up and down — A word that reads the same climbing as it does descending is the only kind that survives being sent both ways, which is what this asks for.

The sun rising and sinking through the day, and the moment it changes its mind.

A down entry is read top to bottom, so 'up and down' is asking for letters that come out the same whichever way you travel them. Nothing turns into anything else.

3 down PO-FACED

Not amused picking up work after ineffective coffee (2-5)

Definition: Not amused · reversal + charade

Not amused -> PO-FACED: po-faced means humourless and disapproving, wearing the solemn expression of someone refusing to see the joke.

  • ineffective coffee → DECAF coffee with the caffeine taken out does not do coffee's job
  • work → OP an op is a work, the same shortening as a composer's opus
  • picking up — Anything picked up in a down entry travels back towards the top, so the letters assembled below arrive in the opposite order.
  • after — Coming after something puts it behind, so the work sits at the tail of the coffee before the pair is lifted.

A humourless employee collecting a job after a cup of coffee that did nothing for them.

'Picking up' reads as collecting the work and is the instruction that turns the whole assembly over, both pieces at once rather than either on its own.

4 down PROMENADE

Walk before comprehending warning notice (9)

Definition: Walk · container + charade

Walk -> PROMENADE: a promenade is both the paved walk along a seafront and the leisurely stroll you take on it.

  • before → PRE pre- is the prefix meaning before, and the three letters open up after the second
  • warning → OMEN an omen is a sign that warns of what is coming
  • notice → AD a notice in a paper is an ad, the classified sort
  • comprehending — To comprehend something is to take it in and hold it there, so what follows lands inside the word doing the comprehending.

Strolling along before you take in a sign telling you to keep off.

'Warning notice' looks like one object on a post and is two separate words to translate. The definition is a noun as well as a verb: the seafront kind you walk along.

5 down PARASOL

Painter really in friend's shade (7)

Definition: shade · container + charade

shade -> PARASOL: a parasol is carried to make shade, so the setter defines it by what it produces rather than what it is made of.

  • friend's → PAL a pal is a friend, and the three letters part after the second
  • Painter → RA an RA is a Royal Academician, a painter elected to London's Royal Academy
  • really → SO so tired is really tired, the word working as an intensifier
  • in friend's — Being in a friend rather than beside one puts the pieces just named between that friend's letters.

A painter genuinely overshadowed by a friend.

'Shade' is where the trap sits: it reads as the shadow one artist casts over another, and it means the thing that makes shade.

6 down SIENA

Insane jockeys having no end of fun here? (5)

Definition: here? · deletion + anagram

here? -> SIENA: the Tuscan city stages the Palio, so 'here' points at the one place those riders are doing this.

  • Insane → INSANE the fodder handed over whole, six letters where the grid wants five
  • fun → N the end of fun is N, and that is the letter surrendered before the jostling
  • having no end of — The end of a word is its last letter, and having none of it is the instruction to drop that letter before anything else happens.
  • jockeys — To jockey for position is to shove and jostle, and letters jostled about no longer sit where they started.

Jockeys riding like lunatics and enjoying every second of it, with a nudge at where.

The Palio is a bareback horse race twice each summer round the sloping main square of Siena, ridden hard enough to make 'insane jockeys' fair comment.

7 down TANDOOR

Oven with duck in to roast, initially (7)

Definition: Oven · container + charade + first letter

Oven -> TANDOOR: a tandoor is the clay charcoal oven of north Indian and Pakistani kitchens, so the plain word covers it exactly.

  • to → TO the word used literally, two letters that open up after the first
  • with → AND with joins two things together and so does and
  • duck → O a batter dismissed without scoring in cricket has made a duck, and no score is written as a nought
  • roast, initially → R the opening letter of roast, which is all that is asked for
  • in to — 'In' asks for an insertion and 'to' is what it inserts into, the two letters of that little word taken at face value.
  • initially — Initially means at the outset, so only the opening letter of the word beside it is wanted.

A duck going into the oven to roast, or at least starting to.

'In to' belongs to the cooking on the surface - the bird goes in to roast - and splits into an instruction plus the two letters it operates on.

8 down OVERTURNED

Upset voter rattled ballot box - Lib Dem leader? (10)

Definition: Upset · anagram + charade

Upset -> OVERTURNED: a boat or a verdict that has been overturned has been upset - tipped over, or reversed by a higher authority.

  • voter rattled → OVERT the five letters of VOTER shaken into a new order
  • ballot box → URN votes are posted into an urn, which is what a ballot box is
  • Lib Dem leader? → ED Ed Davey has led the Liberal Democrats, Britain's third party, since 2020
  • rattled — Something rattled has been shaken hard, and letters shaken hard come to rest in an order nobody chose.

A furious voter shaking the ballot box, with a glance at the man running the Liberal Democrats.

'Lib Dem leader' looks like the party's initial letter and wants the leader's first name instead, which is the one piece of the charade you cannot work out from a dictionary.

12 down GUILLOTINE

Cunning to take group on board chopper (10)

Definition: chopper · container + charade

chopper -> GUILLOTINE: a guillotine chops, whether it is taking off heads or trimming a ream of paper, so the blunt word covers it.

  • Cunning → GUILE guile is cunning, and the five letters part after the fourth
  • group → LOT a lot is a group of people or things taken together
  • on board → IN a passenger on board a boat is in it
  • to take — Taking something in is holding it inside you, so what the cunning takes ends up between its letters.

Cleverly getting a whole party on board a helicopter.

'Chopper' is the helicopter the group are boarding on the surface and a machine that chops in the definition.

15 down TOOTHACHE

Something nasty in your mug as well - husband cutting facial hair? (9)

Definition: Something nasty in your mug · charade + container

Something nasty in your mug -> TOOTHACHE: mug is slang for the face, and a bad tooth is about the nastiest thing a face can be holding.

  • as well → TOO as well means too, and it takes the front
  • facial hair? → TACHE a tache is a moustache, clipped the way British speech clips it, and the five letters open up after the first
  • husband → H H is the standard short form of husband, as on a form
  • cutting — Cutting into something opens a gap in it, and the letter named just before this drops into the gap.

Something unpleasant floating in your cup, and a husband trimming his moustache.

'Mug' is the cup you are drinking from until you remember it is British slang for a face, and the nasty thing is in your teeth.

17 down ON GUARD

Suspicious article covered with ground nuts (2,5)

Definition: Suspicious · container + anagram

Suspicious -> ON GUARD: someone on guard is wary and watchful, expecting trouble - the sense of suspicious that means alert rather than dubious.

  • ground nuts → ONGURD the six letters of GROUND in a new order, opening up after the fourth
  • article → A the indefinite article, one letter of it
  • covered with — Something covered with earth is buried in it, so the article ends up inside those letters rather than next to them.
  • nuts — Nuts means crazy, and a crazy arrangement of letters is not the arrangement you were given.

An object coated all over in crushed peanuts.

'Ground nuts' is one space away from groundnuts, which is what the surface wants it to be, and it is really two instructions: the earth and the word that scrambles it.

18 down SNIPPET

Bite dog, perhaps after son bit (7)

Definition: bit · charade

bit -> SNIPPET: a snippet is a small piece snipped off something bigger, usually of news or talk, which is exactly a bit of it.

  • son → S S is the standard short form of son, as in a family record
  • Bite → NIP a nip is a small sharp bite
  • dog, perhaps → PET 'perhaps' marks definition by example: a dog is one kind of pet
  • after — Coming after something puts you behind it, so the son's letter takes the top of a down entry and the rest queue up below.

A dog that has nipped the son, and the thought of biting it back.

'Bit' is the definition, sitting at the end where it reads as part of the story - the size of the bite rather than a piece of news.

20 down DISCARD

Reject managed to conceal stigma (7)

Definition: Reject · container

Reject -> DISCARD: to discard is to throw out what you no longer want, a card from your hand or a plan from a meeting.

  • managed → DID if you managed it, you did it, and the three letters part after the second
  • stigma → SCAR a scar is the mark an injury leaves, and a stigma is that mark on a reputation
  • to conceal — Anything concealed is hidden inside something bigger, so the stigma disappears between the letters that hide it.

Someone who has managed to keep a shameful mark hidden.

The plainest word in the clue is the one carrying letters: 'managed' is a past tense standing in for another past tense rather than describing anybody.

22 down ELITE

Cream filling for her diet (5)

Definition: Cream · middle letter + charade

Cream -> ELITE: the cream of a group is its choicest few, which is what an elite is - the pick of the crop.

  • her → E the middle letter of the three
  • diet → LITE lite is how the label spells the diet version of a drink or a snack
  • filling — The filling of a sandwich is the bit in the middle, so what is wanted from the word after it is its middle letter.

Cream filling in a pudding that is meant to be part of her diet.

'Cream' is not dairy: the cream of something is its best part. 'Filling' looks like the middle of a cake and is an instruction about the middle of a word.

23 down ALAS

That stinks in a similar way to sulphur (4)

Definition: That stinks · charade

That stinks -> ALAS: alas is the cry for something gone wrong, which is what 'that stinks' says in modern slang.

  • in a similar way to → ALA a la is the borrowed French for in the manner of, familiar from a la carte
  • sulphur → S S is sulphur's symbol in the periodic table

A smell being compared to the reek of sulphur.

The definition is today's grumble standing in for an old-fashioned cry of regret, which is why it hides so well at the front.
