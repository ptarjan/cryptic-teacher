5 across SINATRA

Frank discussions in a trade-off digested (7)

Definition: Frank · hidden word

Sinatra is named by his forename alone — a definition by example that leans on the singer being famous enough to need no surname.

  • discussions in a trade-off → SINATRA seven consecutive letters run across the word boundaries of that phrase
  • digested — something digested has been swallowed and is now inside, which is where these letters are sitting

Plain speaking in the middle of a hard bargaining session.

'Frank discussions' is a phrase you have heard a hundred times, which is exactly what stops you reading 'Frank' as somebody's first name.

9 across TULIP

Clue about bulb, essentially? (5)

Definition: bulb · container + middle letters

A tulip is a bulb by example — one of the plants a gardener buys dry in autumn rather than as a packet of seed.

  • Clue → TIP a tip is a hint — what you give somebody who is stuck
  • essentially → UL the two letters at the heart of the word the definition has just used
  • about — something placed about a thing is wrapped round it, so the short pair goes in the middle
  • essentially — the essence of a word is its middle, so this asks for the letters at the heart of it

A hint concerning a light bulb, more or less.

'Bulb' is doing two jobs at once: it is the definition and it is also the word the middle letters are dug out of. The question mark is Paul owning up to it.

10 across ROOSEVELT

A few missing artist in essence, US president (9)

Definition: US president · deletion + container

Roosevelt names two US presidents, Theodore and Franklin, so the definition points at a surname rather than at one man.

  • A few → SEVERAL several is the everyday word for a handful of something
  • artist → RA RA is a Royal Academician, the crossword's standard artist, and these are the two letters removed
  • essence → ROOT the root of a matter is its essence — get to the root of it
  • missing — what is missing has been taken away, so the two letters named beside it come out of the longer word
  • in — the bare instruction to sit one chunk of letters inside another

A few people short at a gallery, with the painter himself nowhere to be seen.

'Missing' is an instruction, not scene-setting: the artist is being subtracted. RA, the Royal Academician, is the two-letter artist the crossword uses everywhere.

11 across LOCAL DERBY

See Yorkshire flower near Sheff U v Sheff W, say (5,5)

Definition: Sheff U v Sheff W, say · charade

A local derby is a match between two clubs from the same town, which is what United against Wednesday is in Sheffield.

  • See → LO lo! is the old word for behold, as in lo and behold
  • Yorkshire flower → CALDER a flower is a thing that flows, and the Calder runs through West Yorkshire
  • near → BY by means beside: a house by the river is near it

Some Yorkshire bloom growing close to the two Sheffield football grounds.

'See Yorkshire flower' reads as an invitation to go and look at a bloom, and nothing in it is about plants. Sheff U and Sheff W are Sheffield United and Sheffield Wednesday.

12 across MEAT STEW

Last of ham we taste mixed up – in this? (4,4)

Definition: Last of ham we taste mixed up – in this? · &lit + anagram + last letter

Read straight, the clue describes the dish: the last of the ham and whatever else we taste, mixed up together in one pot.

  • Last of ham → M the final letter of ham
  • we taste → WETASTE seven letters of fodder, taken as they stand
  • Last of — points at the final letter of the word that follows and takes no more of it
  • mixed up — things mixed up are left in no particular order, which is what happens to these letters

The end of the ham and everything else worth eating, all stirred together in one pot.

The sentence is the definition and the wordplay at once: read it one way and it shuffles letters, read it again and it describes what goes in the pot.

14 across SOLID GROUND

Land idols in trouble? (5,6)

Definition: Land · anagram

Solid ground is land as opposed to sea or air — what you are glad to be standing on again after a rough crossing.

  • idols → SOLID the same five letters in a different order
  • in trouble → GROUND what you just did to those five letters — you put them through the mill — and the clue asks you to write that down
  • in trouble — letters in trouble have been knocked out of their order, and the word for what was done to them is the second half of what you write in

False gods getting themselves into difficulty.

A reverse anagram: the second word is not shuffled letters, it is the past participle of grind, naming the operation that turns the first word into 'idols'.

18 across WITHERSPOON

US actress, how might she cause a stir in speech? (11)

Definition: US actress · homophone

Reese Witherspoon is the American actress the surname points at, a definition by example dressed up as a general category.

  • how might she cause a stir → WITHERSPOON stirring is done with a spoon, and hers is the one the question is asking about
  • in speech — tells you the wordplay describes a sound rather than a spelling, so write down what you hear

Wondering how a Hollywood star might get everybody talking.

'Cause a stir' is the idiom for making a fuss and also the literal act of stirring. Said at speed, the aitch of 'her' drops and the three words run together.

21 across HEAP

Groovy chassis on a jalopy (4)

Definition: jalopy · container

A jalopy is a battered old car and a heap is the same insult, the name coming from how little holds the thing together.

  • Groovy → HEP hep was the jazz-club word for what a later decade called hip: in the know, fashionable
  • a → A the clue's own indefinite article, taken as one letter rather than looked up
  • chassis on — a chassis is the frame everything else is mounted on, so the word beside it becomes an outside with room in the middle

An old banger with a fashionable body sitting on it.

'Groovy' and 'hep' are the same slang a couple of decades apart, hep coming first out of the jazz clubs. A heap is a car well past its best.

22 across SNOOZEFEST

Sense of outrageous theatre initially dressing Australian in drag (10)

Definition: drag · anagram + first letter + container

A snoozefest is something so dull it sends you to sleep, which is what people mean when they call a thing a drag.

  • Sense of → SENSEOF seven letters of fodder, taken as they stand
  • theatre initially → T the first letter of theatre, joined to the fodder before the shuffle
  • Australian → OZ Oz is what Australians call Australia, and the crossword's shorthand for it
  • outrageous — outrageous behaviour respects no order, and these letters respect none either
  • initially — asks for the letter the preceding word starts with and nothing else
  • dressing — dressing goes on the outside of whatever it dresses, so the shuffled letters wrap round the short word

A wildly theatrical Australian got up in a frock.

'In drag' is the definition hiding inside the costume joke — a drag is a bore, and 'in' is doing nothing but joining.

25 across CONSTABLE

Boy in Blue artist … (9)

Definition: Boy in Blue · double definition

Both senses: a constable is the junior police rank, and John Constable painted the English landscapes in every gallery.

  • Boy in Blue → CONSTABLE the boys in blue are the British police, and this is the rank at the bottom of the ladder
  • artist → CONSTABLE the Suffolk landscape painter of The Hay Wain, 1776 to 1837

A painting called Boy in Blue, and the man who painted it.

The italics dress 'Boy in Blue' as a picture title, and the picture they nudge you towards is The Blue Boy — which belongs to the other painter, the one at 26 across.

26 across GAINSBOROUGH

… gets quarter (12)

Definition: … · charade

The ellipsis carries 'artist' over from 25 across, and Gainsborough is the second painter, the one who actually painted The Blue Boy.

  • gets → GAINS if you get something you gain it, and the surface keeps the verb in the third person
  • quarter → BOROUGH a quarter of a city is a district of it, and a borough is a district with its own council

The painter from the clue before coming into a quarter share of something.

The leading ellipsis borrows its definition from 25 across, where 'artist' does double duty — and the joke is that The Blue Boy hanging over that clue is this painter's work, not the other one's.

27 across SCREW IT

Team getting stuffed in flop, I give up! (5,2)

Definition: I give up! · container

'Screw it' is what you say when you abandon the attempt — the exclamation of giving up, not an instruction to a carpenter.

  • flop → SIT to flop into a chair is to sit down in it heavily
  • Team → CREW a crew is a team: a rowing eight, a film unit, a ship's company
  • getting stuffed in — to be stuffed in is to be forced inside, which is where the shorter word ends up

A side being thrashed, and a supporter who has finally had enough.

'Flop' looks like the noun, a show that failed, which suits a team getting stuffed. The clue wants the verb instead: to flop down.

28 across AIRLESS

Close, as Mexican dog in East London? (7)

Definition: Close · deletion

Close describes a room or a day with no air moving in it, the muggy sense used of August weather and of stuffy rooms.

  • Mexican dog → HAIRLESS the Mexican Hairless, or Xoloitzcuintli, is a breed bred without a coat
  • in East London → H the aitch a Cockney speaker drops from the front of the word
  • in East London — a Cockney accent drops the aitch at the front of a word, so that letter goes unsounded and unwritten

A hairless Mexican dog somewhere in the East End.

'Close' is the weather sense — stuffy, no air moving — rather than the opposite of far. The Mexican Hairless is a real breed, and East London is where its aitch goes missing.

1 down BOTTLE

Pluck item from the wine rack? (6)

Definition: Pluck · double definition

Bottle is British slang for nerve, as in losing your bottle, and the second sense is the plainest thing a wine rack holds.

  • Pluck → BOTTLE pluck is nerve, and British slang has a word for nerve that a frightened person is said to lose
  • item from the wine rack → BOTTLE what a wine rack is built to hold, one per slot

An instruction to pick something out of the wine rack for the evening.

'Pluck' reads as the imperative verb of the surface — reach out and pick one — but the answer wants it as a noun, the courage a person can lose.

2 down RELICT

Survivor brightened up again having caught cold (6)

Definition: Survivor · container

A relict is what remains when the rest has gone: a widow, or a species stranded from an earlier age.

  • brightened up again → RELIT a lamp brightened once more has been re-lit
  • cold → C C is the letter on the cold tap and the standard abbreviation for it
  • having caught — catching something means taking it in, so what is caught ends up held inside the word beside it

A person cheered up all over again, having gone and caught a cold in the process.

A relict is a widow, or a plant or animal left over from an age that has otherwise vanished — a survival rather than a survivor in the everyday sense.

3 down UNPOLISHED

Rough tongue in nude rogue (10)

Definition: Rough · container + anagram

Something unpolished has had no finishing work done to it, so its surface stays rough — the word covers manners as readily as wood.

  • nude rogue → UNED the four letters of nude, reshuffled into a shell with a gap in the middle
  • tongue → POLISH a tongue is a language, and this one is spoken in Warsaw
  • in — the plain instruction to drop one word inside the letters beside it
  • rogue — a rogue element is one that has broken ranks, so the word next to it will not stay in the order given

A naked scoundrel with a coarse, rough tongue in his head.

'Rough tongue' wants to be one phrase, a coarse mouth on somebody. It splits: only 'Rough' is the definition, and 'tongue' is the language sense.

4 down HORSE OPERA

Western Samoa discontented here with poor supply (5,5)

Definition: Western · anagram + outer letters

A horse opera is the old trade name for a cowboy picture, so 'Western' is the film-genre noun rather than a compass direction.

  • Samoa discontented → SA strip the middle out of the island's name and the outer pair is left
  • here → HERE four letters of fodder, taken straight off the page
  • poor → POOR four more letters of fodder, taken as they stand
  • discontented — read it as dis-contented: the contents are taken out and only the letters at the two ends survive
  • supply — not the noun about deliveries but the adverb from supple, done limberly, so the letters bend into a new shape

Grumbling on a Pacific island where the supplies have been arriving in a poor state.

'Western Samoa' looks like one country and splits in two: the definition is 'Western' on its own, and only Samoa goes into the mixer.

5 down SCOOBY DOO

Animated solver’s clue? (6,3)

Definition: Animated solver · double definition

Both halves name one cartoon dog: he solves a mystery every episode, and his name is rhyming slang for a clue.

  • Animated solver → SCOOBY DOO the cartoon Great Dane whose whole job is unmasking the villain each week
  • clue → SCOOBY DOO London rhyming slang, normally clipped to its first word: 'I haven't got a...'

A cartoon character stuck on a puzzle and wanting a hint.

Rhyming slang is the seam: the dog's name rhymes with 'clue', so the phrase is both the character and the thing a stuck solver hasn't got.

6 down NOEL

Christmas movie cut, cut for Christmas! (4)

Definition: Christmas! · deletion

Noel is Christmas itself — the French word for the feast, sung in carols and printed on cards every December.

  • movie → NOELLE the 2019 Disney film about Santa's daughter, which is the festive one meant here
  • cut, cut → LE the two letters that come off the end, one per cut
  • Christmas → no letters of its own: it narrows down which film, and the setter then puts the same word to work at the end as the definition
  • cut, cut — said twice because the trimming happens twice, one letter off the end each time

A festive film trimmed and trimmed again to fit the Christmas schedules.

The doubled word is a doubled instruction: 'cut' twice takes two letters off. The film is Noelle, a 2019 Disney comedy, and the exclamation mark marks the definition.

7 down THEREOUT

Hawker steals present from that place (8)

Definition: from that place · container

Thereout means out of that place, a survivor of the there- family alongside therein and thereof, all still alive in legal drafting.

  • Hawker → TOUT a tout is the man on the pavement selling tickets he should not have
  • present → HERE present as in attendance — the word you call out at a register
  • steals — to steal something is to take it, and what a thief takes he tucks away inside

A street seller helping himself to the gift somebody left lying about.

'Present' offers three senses — a gift, a tense, being in attendance — and the surface pushes hard on the gift. Thereout is an old word, more Chaucer than conversation.

8 down ALTITUDE

A cap in use tilted badly, level up (8)

Definition: level up · anagram + first letter

Altitude is height above the ground — the level a thing has got up to, which is the sense used in the cockpit.

  • A → A the clue's first word, taken as a single letter of fodder
  • cap in use → U the letter sitting at the top of use — an operation, not an abbreviation
  • tilted → TILTED six letters of fodder, taken straight off the page
  • cap — a cap sits on top of a head, and the top of a written word is the letter it begins with
  • badly — something done badly comes out wrong, and letters that come out wrong come out in the wrong order

A hat sitting crooked on somebody's head and badly in need of straightening.

The surface has you reaching up to straighten a hat, so 'level up' passes as the end of that sentence rather than as a height a pilot would report.

13 down PRINCE IGOR

That said in support of stinker, describing nicer comic 23 down (6,4)

Definition: 23 down · container + anagram + charade

23 down is OPERA, and this is Borodin's, so the definition names the art form while the wordplay supplies which one.

  • stinker → PIG a pig of a job is a stinker of one
  • nicer comic → RINCE the five letters of nicer, rearranged
  • That said → OR 'or' in the sense of then again, the concession a speaker makes when they say that said
  • in support of — supporting something means holding it up from underneath, so in a down clue these letters go below the rest
  • describing — to describe a circle is to draw one round something, so one chunk of letters encloses another
  • comic — comic here is odd and laughable rather than funny-ha-ha, and letters treated as a joke will not stay put

Something kinder offered in defence of a thoroughly horrible person.

'Comic 23 down' wants to be one phrase naming a genre, and the definition stops at the cross-reference: Borodin's opera is a grim tale of a captured prince, not a comedy.

15 down LOSING BET

Chance gone, lots being wasted? (6,3)

Definition: Chance gone · anagram

A chance gone is an opportunity that has come to nothing, which is what a bet turns into once it starts losing.

  • lots being → LOTSBEING nine letters of fodder, taken straight from the clue
  • wasted — wasted carries the squandered sense and the drunk sense at once, and neither leaves anything in order

Someone squandering their opportunities and a good deal else besides.

'Lots' looks like a quantity word doing surface duty and 'being' like grammatical glue. Both are fodder, which is how the letter count comes out exactly.

16 down AW SHUCKS

Expression of embarrassment while burying head in whelk shell (2,6)

Definition: Expression of embarrassment · container + charade + first letter

Aw shucks is what a bashful American says when praised, to the point that an aw-shucks grin means an embarrassed one.

  • while → AS as means while — 'as I was leaving' is 'while I was leaving'
  • head in whelk → W the letter at the front of whelk, picked out rather than looked up
  • shell → SHUCK to shuck is to shell: what you do to oysters and to corn
  • burying — bury a thing and it ends up under the surface, so these letters go inside the pair around them
  • head — the head of a word is its front, so this takes the opening letter of the word that follows

A red-faced person hiding their head inside a whelk's shell.

'Whelk shell' looks like one object and is two separate jobs: the whelk hands over its first letter, and 'shell' is wanted as the verb.

17 down STRAINER

Riddle from Sphinx’s first teacher (8)

Definition: Riddle · charade + first letter

A riddle is a coarse sieve for sifting gravel or ash, so it names the same job as the kitchen implement that separates solids from liquid.

  • Sphinx’s first → S the opening letter of Sphinx, picked out by position
  • teacher → TRAINER a trainer teaches — the one who takes you through the drills
  • first — takes only the opening letter of the name in front of it and discards the rest

A puzzle handed down by whoever first taught the Sphinx its trade.

'Riddle' is the sieve, not the puzzle, which is why the Sphinx is standing there as a decoy with its famous question.

19 down PETITE

Little bird in leak (6)

Definition: Little · container

Petite means small and neatly made, used of a person's build and borrowed straight from French, where it is simply the feminine of small.

  • leak → PEE to pee is to leak, in the nursery sense of both
  • bird → TIT a tit is a small garden bird: blue tit, great tit, coal tit
  • in — the bare instruction to place one word inside the other, with no dressing up

A small bird caught in a dripping pipe.

'Little bird' reads as one phrase, the one that tells you things. It splits, and only 'Little' is the definition.

20 down STASIS

Definition: blockage · charade + first letter

Stasis is a standstill — in medicine the stopping of flow in a vessel, which is exactly what a blockage produces.

  • Force once → STASI the Stasi were East Germany's secret police, dissolved along with the country in 1990
  • sink → S the letter sink starts with, taken by position
  • opening — the opening of a word is the letter it starts with, one letter and no more

Pressure put on the plughole to shift whatever is stuck down there.

'Force once' is a police force that no longer exists, and 'once' is the clue's only warning that the word is historical rather than current.
