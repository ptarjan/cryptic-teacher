Across

1 across TRIM

Car's upholstery and part of its wheel as described in Yorkshire (4)

Definition: Car's upholstery · charade

  • as described in Yorkshire → T Yorkshire speech clips 'the' down to a bare t' — t'pub, t'match — so the article shrinks to one letter.
  • part of its wheel → RIM The outer hoop of a wheel, the part the tyre sits on.

The surface talks about a car from start to finish, and the accent is doing real work: written down, that dropped definite article is nothing but an apostrophe and a single consonant.

3 across CHESS PIECE

Musical number that may be promoted on the boards? (5,5)

Definition: may be promoted on the boards · charade

  • Musical → CHESS Chess is a stage musical, written by Tim Rice with the two men from Abba and set at a world championship.
  • number → PIECE A number is a single item in a programme — a song, or any short work a musician performs.

Both halves of the wordplay are musical and the definition is not. 'The boards' pulls you towards a theatre, where a show gets promoted, and away from the board where a pawn does.

9 across BUTS

Objections: proof of payment returned (4)

Definition: Objections · reversal

  • proof of payment → STUB The counterfoil you keep when a ticket or a cheque is paid, and the thing you wave when someone asks whether you paid.

The colon does the joining, which makes the first word look like a heading rather than the definition. Turn the counterfoil round and four letters of grumbling fall out.

10 across BLUEBOTTLE

Fly down with Spirit (10)

Definition: Fly · charade

  • down → BLUE Feeling down is feeling blue — the colour has stood for low spirits for centuries.
  • Spirit → BOTTLE British slang for nerve or courage: someone who loses their bottle has lost their nerve.

'Spirit' looks like it wants a drink and the capital letter sells the idea, but the sense wanted is courage. Both halves come out of everyday idiom rather than any dictionary of insects.

12 across GROUNDSWELL

Shift in public opinion having sound basis at first (11)

Definition: Shift in public opinion · charade

  • basis → GROUNDS The grounds for a decision are the basis it rests on, and the word is plural in that sense.
  • sound → WELL Sound here is the adjective for good condition: of a person, being sound is being in health.

The trap is hearing 'sound' as a noise, which sends you looking for the wrong kind of word. It is the adjective, and the clue's last two words say which piece goes first.

15 across AMUSE ME

An inspiration? Yours truly? I could do with a laugh (5,2)

Definition: I could do with a laugh · charade

  • An → A The indefinite article, taken in its single-letter form.
  • inspiration? → MUSE A muse is the classical source of a poet's inspiration, one of nine goddesses.
  • Yours truly? → ME Yours truly is how a writer signs off when referring to himself.

Two question marks in a row look like the setter hedging his bets; they are just flagging loose definitions. The definition is the whole back half, and it is a sentence rather than a noun.

16 across YEMENIS

The old group of chaps is group of Arabians (7)

Definition: group of Arabians · charade

  • The old → YE Ye is the archaic form of the definite article, as in 'Ye Olde Tea Shoppe'.
  • chaps → MEN Chaps is the everyday British word for a group of blokes.
  • is → IS The verb is taken as two letters and used just as it stands.

The trick is that the verb in the middle joins nothing at all — it is fodder, which is why the sentence reads so oddly if you try to take it as a statement.

17 across EYESORE

Everyman regarded, in report, as one unpleasant to behold (7)

Definition: one unpleasant to behold · homophone

  • Everyman → EYE The setter's pen name, and a setter writing about himself is I — one spoken letter with a longer spelling.
  • regarded → SORE To have regarded something is to have looked at it, and that past tense sounds, in British English, exactly like a word for tender.

Everyman naming himself is a house signature, and here it is doing wordplay rather than decoration. Both halves have to be said out loud before they will spell anything.

19 across OOH LA LA

Primarily ostentatious outcry heaping liberal and lavish admiration? (3,2,2)

Definition: ostentatious outcry heaping liberal and lavish admiration? · &lit + first letters

  • ostentatious outcry heaping liberal and lavish admiration → OOHLALA Seven words in a row, each giving up the letter it starts with.

Even 'and' is pulling its weight, which is the tell: when a clue's small words look load-bearing, count the initials. Read straight, the same sentence is what the exclamation means.

20 across COSTA BLANCA

Estimate price of a Chardonnay, peut-être, one in resort (5,6)

Definition: resort · charade

  • Estimate price of a → COSTA To cost a job is to work out what it will come to, and the indefinite article follows to round the chunk off.
  • Chardonnay, peut-être → BLANC Peut-être is French for perhaps, flagging Chardonnay as one example of a vin blanc, a white wine.
  • one → A A stands for one, the way 'a dozen' means a single dozen.

Everyman likes a foreign holiday, and two languages are working at once: the French flags an example, and the Spanish that comes out of it means the white coast.

23 across OSCILLATOR

Clip from radio's Cilla, to remember one that's Swinging (10)

Definition: one that's Swinging · hidden word

  • radio's Cilla, to remember → OSCILLATOR Ten letters in an unbroken run across three words, an apostrophe and a comma.

Cilla Black was a Liverpool singer who became one of British television's biggest hosts, so the surface is pure nostalgia — and the punctuation between the words is what hides the run.

24 across BRIO

After change of heart, writer showing élan (4)

Definition: élan · reversal

  • writer → BIRO A Biro is the everyday British name for a ballpoint pen, after its Hungarian inventor Laszlo Biro.

'After' is doing nothing but holding the sentence upright. The convention worth keeping is the pen, which turns up constantly as a four-letter writer, and here only its middle moves.

25 across EARED SEALS

Erased 'sea lions' at first - rewritten as: ___ (5,5)

Definition: 'sea lions' · anagram + first letter

  • Erased → ERASED Six letters straight off the first word of the clue.
  • 'sea lions' at first → SEAL The first of the two quoted words in full, then only the opening letter of the second.

The blank at the end is where the answer goes, and the joke is that the quoted phrase does double duty: it defines the answer and supplies most of its letters at once.

26 across RYES

Grasses grow, you hear? (4)

Definition: Grasses · homophone

  • grow → RYES To grow is to rise, and rise said aloud matches a plural word for a cereal grass.

Four words with nothing to spare: one is the definition, one is the sound, and the question mark flags that you are being asked to hear rather than to read.

Down

1 down TOBOGGANED

Went rapidly downhill? (10)

Definition: Went rapidly downhill? · cryptic definition

  • Went rapidly → Read figuratively this is how a business, a career or someone's health is said to decline; read plainly it is simply moving at speed.
  • downhill → The setter means an actual slope, and the question mark is the nudge: on snow, going down one at speed is a winter sport with its own verb.

There is nothing to take apart here — the whole clue is one phrase read twice. The idiom about decline is cashed in for a real hillside, a sledge and a lot of snow.

2 down INTRODUCES

Launches risible reductions (10)

Definition: Launches · anagram

  • reductions → REDUCTIONS Ten letters handed over intact, which is exactly the length of the entry.

The surface reads as a company launching laughable cuts, which is the sentence a solver wants to believe. Counting the last word first is what breaks it open.

4 down HOLY SEE

Just: that man, Rev Spooner says, in the Vatican (4,3)

Definition: the Vatican · spoonerism

  • Just → SOLELY Just here is the adverb meaning merely or only.
  • that man → HE The pronoun a clue reaches for when it means a male person at a distance.
  • Rev Spooner says → HOLY SEE Say those two words with their opening sounds traded, S for H, and the pious result is what you write in.

Say the plain version first and it is a flat little phrase nobody would write for its own sake — only him. That flatness is the tell: Spooner clues hide behind awkward wording.

5 down SCENERY

Stylised screen at back of play? (7)

Definition: Stylised screen at back of play? · &lit + anagram + last letter

  • screen → SCREEN Six of the seven letters, handed over whole.
  • play → Y The final letter of the word, which the instruction in front of it asks for.

This is the rare clue where every word does both jobs at once. The trap is taking 'screen' for the definition — a screen at the back of a stage — when it is the fodder.

6 down PROBLEM CHILD

Truant perhaps having issue following bit of Maths (7,5)

Definition: Truant perhaps · charade

  • bit of Maths → PROBLEM A bit of maths set to be worked out is a problem, the word for one exercise in a textbook.
  • issue → CHILD Issue in the legal sense means a person's offspring, as in 'died without issue'.

'Issue' is the word to own: in wills and law reports it means offspring, and setters use it constantly. The classroom surface is a decoy built round the other half.

7 down EATS

Scoff at tablets swallowed (4)

Definition: Scoff · container

  • tablets → ES E is the standing crossword letter for the drug ecstasy, sold as tablets, so more than one of them is ES.
  • at → AT The word is used exactly as it stands, dropped inside the pair around it.

The surface is somebody sneering at a pill-popper. 'Tablets' is the giveaway: a single E is the standard abbreviation, and a plural of it makes a two-letter shell.

8 down EPEE

Physical exercise - cutting: 'en garde' discharged? (4)

Definition: Physical exercise - cutting: 'en garde' discharged? · &lit + container + outer letters

  • 'en garde' → EE The French phrase opens and closes with the same letter, and everything between the two is thrown away.
  • Physical exercise → PE PE is the timetable abbreviation for physical education, the school lesson.

The clue never stops talking about fencing, which is why there is no separate definition to underline: the whole sentence is the picture, and the wordplay hides inside the French.

11 down UNRECONCILED

Iffy clue - 'Cord (9)' - not resolved (12)

Definition: not resolved · anagram

  • clue - 'Cord → CLUECORD Eight of the twelve letters come straight from these two words.
  • (9) → NINE The numeral in the quoted mini-clue is spelled out as a word and joins the pool.

The clue quotes a little clue at itself, enumeration and all, and that bracketed figure is not decoration — you write the number out in letters before shuffling.

13 down UNMANNERLY

Rude, curt male nunnery reformed (10)

Definition: Rude · anagram + deletion

  • male → MAL The word arrives with its last letter already trimmed away by the adjective in front of it.
  • nunnery → NUNNERY All seven letters join the pool, which is where the doubled N comes from.

Everyman's surface is a small joke about a religious house. The mechanism runs in two steps, and the second only comes out right once the first has thrown a letter away.

14 down ESCALATORS

Alas! Escort lost in flights (10)

Definition: flights · anagram

  • Alas! Escort → ALASESCORT The exclamation and the noun together supply exactly ten letters.

The exclamation mark is the disguise: 'Alas!' reads as the setter's aside rather than as fodder, so the letter count comes out four short until you count it in.

18 down ENSLAVE

Bind some of Gwen's lavender (7)

Definition: Bind · hidden word

  • Gwen's lavender → ENSLAVE Seven letters run consecutively through this phrase, straddling the apostrophe and the gap between the words.

A hidden word lives or dies by its surface, and a woman with a bunch of lavender is about as innocent as they come. The apostrophe is the join your eye slides straight over.

19 down ON A ROLL

Where butter is put is repeatedly lucky (2,1,4)

Definition: Where butter is put · double definition

  • Where butter is put → The literal reading: a bread roll, buttered.
  • repeatedly lucky → The gambler's idiom for a run of good fortune that keeps going.

The two readings sit next to each other with only a verb between them, so the whole thing looks like a single statement. Split it after 'put' and both halves stand up on their own.

21 down POLE

Rod's European (4)

Definition: Rod · double definition

  • Rod → The plain object: a long thin length of wood or metal.
  • European → Read as a nationality rather than the adjective for the continent — a native of one particular country in the east of it.

'Rod's' reads as a man's name with a possessive, and that is the whole disguise; it is really 'rod is'. Only one of the two definitions takes a capital letter.

22 down SCAR

Athletic events set back with lack of energy - and evidence of injury (4)

Definition: evidence of injury · reversal + deletion

  • Athletic events → RACES Races on a track are the athletic events, and five letters is one more than the entry has room for.
  • energy → E E is the physicist's symbol for energy, and the crossword's standard single letter for it.

'Lack of energy' is a two-part instruction rather than a mood: the word has to be turned into its single letter before it can be taken away. The dashes are surface punctuation only.
