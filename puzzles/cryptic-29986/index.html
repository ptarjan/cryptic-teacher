Across

5 across POPS IN

Old man’s at home, thus visits for little while (4,2)

Definition: visits for little while · charade

To pop in is to call on somebody briefly and be gone again, so the phrase means visiting for no more than a little while.

  • Old man’s → POPS 'pop' is the familiar word for a father, and the apostrophe-s comes along as the fourth letter
  • at home → IN somebody at home is in, the everyday sense rather than any instruction

Father is at home, so he calls round on people for a short stay.

'At home' is where a solver expects a container instruction, since 'in' usually means something goes inside something else, and here it is only a two-letter synonym.

6 across TOWERS

Recovery vehicles? Some have clocks (6)

Definition: Recovery vehicles · double definition

One sense is the agent noun from 'tow', the trucks that haul wrecks away; the other is the building, and many carry a public clock.

  • Recovery vehicles → the question mark flags a coined agent noun: things that do the towing when a car breaks down
  • Some have clocks → the tall stone kind, and the ones on churches and town halls carry a public face and hands

A question about breakdown trucks and which of them come fitted with clocks.

Anything that tows can be called a tower, which is what the question mark apologises for; the second reading has left vehicles behind entirely and gone to architecture.

9 across ASCEND

Climb in a second, somehow dropping nothing (6)

Definition: Climb · anagram + deletion

To ascend is to move upward under your own effort, so 'climb' is a verb-for-verb synonym in its ordinary intransitive sense.

  • a second → ASECOND seven letters of raw material, the article and the unit of time together
  • nothing → 'nothing' is the letter O, which comes out of that material first
  • somehow — 'Somehow' means by an unspecified route, so the letters beside it arrive in an order nobody promised.
  • dropping — What is dropped falls out and never arrives, so the letter named here leaves the material before it is rearranged.

Someone about to start climbing in a second, and not dropping a thing on the way.

'Nothing' as the letter O is the crossword's most-used piece of shorthand — zero drawn as a ring — and here it goes before any shuffling happens.

10 across PRECIOUS

Affected as minutes may be (8)

Definition: Affected · double definition

Precious covers both a mannered, over-refined person and anything highly valued, which is what minutes become when they are running out.

  • Affected → the adjective for someone mincing and over-refined in manner
  • as minutes may be → the everyday sense: highly valued, especially when there are few left

The affected sense — a precious little man, all airs and delicacy — is the harder of the two, and the theme of this grid makes the minutes reading the easier one.

11 across EONS

Lots of the past one’s misrepresented (4)

Definition: Lots of the past · anagram

Eons are immense stretches of elapsed time, so 'lots of the past' names them by sheer quantity rather than by any event in them.

  • one’s → ONES four letters of raw material, the word and its apostrophe-s together
  • misrepresented — Something misrepresented has been put across the wrong way round, so the letters beside it come out in the wrong order.

A version of history that somebody has told wrongly.

The apostrophe-s is fodder, not grammar — a favourite setter's trick, because 'one's' looks like a possessive rather than four letters waiting to be moved.

12 across PHILANDERS

Two-times quiet husband one defames first off (10)

Definition: Two-times · charade + deletion

To philander is to carry on flirtations and affairs behind a partner's back, which is exactly what two-timing somebody means.

  • quiet → P P is piano in a score, the composer's mark for playing quietly
  • husband → H H is the standard abbreviation for husband, as on a form
  • one → I the Roman numeral for one
  • defames → LANDERS to defame somebody is to slander them, and the S at the front is what walks off
  • first off — 'First off' takes the front letter away from the word beside it and leaves the remainder standing.

A mild, quiet husband whose name somebody is busy blackening.

'Two-times' looks like arithmetic at the head of the clue and is the verb for cheating on a partner, which leaves the rest of the sentence as four pieces in a row.

13 across TIME IS MONEY

Saying there’s no difference between porridge and bread (4,2,5)

Definition: Saying · charade

The answer is the proverb that hours spent are money earned or lost, so 'Saying' defines it by class rather than by meaning.

  • porridge → TIME doing porridge is British slang for serving a prison sentence, after the oatmeal that was the old jail breakfast
  • there’s no difference between → IS declaring two things no different is declaring one to be the other, and that is the little verb in the middle
  • bread → MONEY bread is long-standing slang for cash, the same joke as dough

A claim that prison porridge and a slice of bread amount to much the same thing.

Porridge and bread look like a grumble about stodgy food, and each is doing slang duty instead — one for a jail term, one for cash.

18 across BIOLOGICAL

Starts off by installing one sensible kind of clock (10)

Definition: kind of clock · first letters + charade

The body's built-in cycle of sleeping and waking is called the biological clock, so the definition points at the word that qualifies it.

  • by installing one → BIO the opening letter of each of the three words, taken in order
  • sensible → LOGICAL a sensible decision is a logical one, straight synonym
  • Starts off — 'Starts off' asks for the letters the following words start off with, one from the front of each.

A workman who begins the job by putting in one sensible sort of clock.

The clock meant is the body's own, the internal rhythm that decides when you feel sleepy, and not a thing anybody could install.

21 across ITEM

Broadcast time for piece of news (4)

Definition: piece of news · anagram

An item is one of the separate stories read out in a news bulletin, so 'piece of news' defines it in the newsroom's own sense.

  • time → TIME four letters of raw material, the word taken as it stands
  • Broadcast — Broadcasting is the farmer's word for scattering seed by hand, so what it touches lands in no particular order.

A bulletin going out on air at the slot booked for it.

'Broadcast time' reads as the hour a programme is scheduled to go out, and the two words are really an instruction and the material it works on.

22 across DURATION

Apply pressure about ten to three, say? As long as it takes (8)

Definition: As long as it takes · container

Duration is how long something lasts, and 'as long as it takes' is the idiom for the same span: whatever amount of time the job needs.

  • Apply pressure → DUN to dun a debtor is to keep pressing them for payment, an old word crosswords have kept alive
  • ten to three, say? → RATIO ten to three is a proportion of the sort written with a colon, and 'say' marks it as one example of the kind
  • about — Something placed about another goes right round it, so the first letters found here wrap themselves around the ones that follow.

Somebody leaning on a debtor at around ten to three in the afternoon.

'Ten to three' is a clock time in the surface and a proportion in the wordplay, and the question mark is the setter's signal that it is only one instance of the second.

23 across CLOCKS

Records hits (6)

Definition: Records · double definition

To clock a speed is to register it on a dial, and to clock somebody, in British slang, is to thump them; both are third-person verbs.

  • Records → the verb for registering a speed on a dial: the radar recorded him at ninety
  • hits → British informal, the verb for landing a punch on somebody

A list of chart-topping records.

Both words look like the music business, records and hits sitting side by side, and both are verbs here — one for measuring a speed, one for throwing a punch.

24 across ELEVEN

Team’s schedule for tea-break (6)

Definition: Team · double definition

An eleven is a full cricket or football side, and eleven o'clock is the hour Britain sets aside for its mid-morning tea break.

  • Team → in cricket and football a side is named for the number of players it puts out
  • schedule for tea-break → elevenses is the British mid-morning pause for tea and a biscuit, named after the hour it happens at

A sports side's fixture list, worked out around when they stop for tea.

A cricket or football side is an eleven because that is how many take the field, and elevenses is the British break taken at that hour — two senses of one number.

25 across TIMELY

Tiny fellow, see, is punctual (6)

Definition: punctual · charade

Something timely arrives at the right moment, which is what punctual means, though timely stretches further to cover anything opportune.

  • Tiny fellow → TIM Tiny Tim is the small boy in Dickens's A Christmas Carol, so a tiny fellow hands over his name
  • see → ELY a see is the district a bishop governs, and Ely is the cathedral city in Cambridgeshire that heads one

A remark that the little chap, you see, always turns up on time.

'See' is the crossword's standing request for a bishop's seat, and the short English ones — Ely, Wells, Ripon — are the ones worth knowing by heart.

Down

1 down APRES-SKI

A smooth king I joined for relaxing stretch after runs (5-3)

Definition: relaxing stretch after runs · charade

Apres-ski is the drinking and lounging that follows a day on the slopes, so the runs are ski runs and the stretch is what comes after them.

  • A → A the clue's opening word, taken as the single letter it already is
  • smooth → PRESS to press a shirt is to smooth it flat under an iron
  • king → K K is the king in chess notation and on a playing card
  • I → I the pronoun stands for itself, one letter
  • joined — Things joined are set end to end in the order given, so each piece named simply follows the one before it.

Teaming up with an unruffled monarch for a rest after a spot of running.

'Runs' reads as jogging and means the pistes, while 'smooth' reads as the king's manner and is the verb for what an iron does to cloth.

2 down WIND UP

Storm increased? Ensure operation of watch (4,2)

Definition: Ensure operation of watch · charade

To wind up a watch is to turn the crown and tighten the spring that drives the hands, which is precisely ensuring it will run.

  • Storm → WIND the loudest part of a storm is its blowing, and weather talk swaps the two words freely
  • increased? → UP prices, numbers and volumes that have increased are all said to be up

A weather bulletin warning the gale has strengthened, and a reminder to check your watch works.

A mechanical watch runs only while its mainspring is tight, so the definition is the whole phrase about keeping it going, not the single word 'watch'.

3 down MODERATO

Guidance on tempo, contemporary inspiration for poets (8)

Definition: Guidance on tempo · charade

Moderato is the Italian word written into a score telling the player to hold a middling speed, which is guidance on tempo.

  • contemporary → MOD a mod is somebody bang up to the minute; the word is clipped from modern and British youth of the 1960s wore it as a name
  • inspiration for poets → ERATO Erato is the Greek muse of lyric poetry, one of the nine sisters a poet was supposed to call on

A shopping list for a young writer: advice on rhythm and a thoroughly up-to-date muse.

The nine Muses are permanent crossword furniture — Erato for love poetry, Clio for history, Terpsichore for dance — and their names repay learning by heart.

4 down PERIOD

Spell as part of school learning (6)

Definition: Spell · double definition

A period is a spell of time, and it is also the block of the school day given over to a single lesson.

  • Spell → the noun meaning a stretch of time — a spell of fine weather, a spell in office
  • part of school learning → the named slot on a timetable in which one subject is taught

Learning to spell, presented as one of the things schoolchildren do.

'Spell' reads as the classroom business of s-p-e-l-l-i-n-g, which is what the mention of school is there to encourage, and the sense wanted is the time one.

5 down PASTOR

Minister in bygone days supported by some troops (6)

Definition: Minister · charade

A pastor is the minister of a congregation, the person who leads its worship, so this is a plain synonym in the ecclesiastical sense.

  • bygone days → PAST bygone days are the past, one phrase for the other
  • some troops → OR OR is Other Ranks, the British Army's term for the soldiers who are not officers
  • supported by — In a down clue whatever supports a thing sits underneath it, so the letters named afterwards go below the ones already found.

A government minister of former times propped up by a section of the army.

'Minister' looks political, especially with troops standing behind him, and the word wanted is the church one — a fine piece of misdirection from a two-word charade.

7 down SQUARE

Piece of crossword that’s out of date (6)

Definition: Piece of crossword · double definition

A crossword grid is ruled into squares, so the first definition names one cell; the second is the old slang for somebody unfashionable.

  • Piece of crossword → a grid is ruled into these, one per letter
  • out of date → the slang sense: conventional, unhip, behind the times

A single cell of a crossword grid, dismissed as old-fashioned.

Calling a person square — dull, conformist, hopelessly behind the fashion — is 1950s jazz slang that has survived in crosswords long after it left the street.

8 down SPLIT SECOND

Left supporter for very brief moment (5,6)

Definition: very brief moment · charade

A split second is the smallest slice of time anyone bothers to name, a second cut into pieces, so it is a very brief moment indeed.

  • Left → SPLIT to split is to leave in a hurry, 1960s slang whose past tense is the same word
  • supporter → SECOND to second a motion is to back it, so the backer is a second — as is the boxer in the corner

Somebody who backed the political left, and then only for a moment.

'Left' wants to be the political wing, especially sitting next to 'supporter', and it is the past tense of a verb meaning departed.

14 down EGG TIMER

Send up kind of person to begin with that can help one cook breakfast (3,5)

Definition: that can help one cook breakfast · charade + reversal

An egg timer is the little sand glass that tells you when a boiled egg is done, so it earns its keep at the breakfast table.

  • kind of person → EGG a good egg is an old-fashioned way of calling somebody a decent sort, so an egg here is a person
  • Send → TIMER to send money is to remit it, and this down clue stands that word on its head
  • up — In a down clue 'up' means reading back up the column, so the word beside it is entered the other way about.
  • to begin with — 'To begin with' puts the piece it follows at the very front, before anything else is added.

A send-up of the sort of person who will help you get breakfast cooked.

'Send up' looks like a single phrase meaning to mock, and the setter is using its two words separately. The person who turns out to be an egg is the other stretch.

15 down EPISODES

Is dividing lyric poems – they’re presented on separate occasions (8)

Definition: they’re presented on separate occasions · container

Episodes are the separate instalments of a serial, each shown or published on its own occasion, so the definition describes them by how they arrive.

  • lyric poems → EPODES an epode is a Greek lyric form, a short line answering a long one
  • Is → IS the little verb is taken at face value, two letters lifted straight out of the clue
  • dividing — What divides a thing goes into the middle of it and pushes the halves apart, which is where these two letters land.

A remark that some lyric poems are being split up and performed on different nights.

'Is' is not doing grammar here but supplying letters, and 'lyric poems' points at a Greek form most solvers only ever meet in crosswords.

16 down MINUTE

Extremely small fraction of hour (6)

Definition: Extremely small · double definition

Minute is both the adjective meaning tiny and the noun for a sixtieth of an hour: one spelling doing duty for two quite separate words.

  • Extremely small → the adjective for something tiny enough to need a lens
  • fraction of hour → the noun: one sixtieth of one

The two definitions run together into a single phrase, which is the trap. They are pronounced differently too — my-NEWT for the tiny sense, MIN-it for the sixty seconds.

17 down WEEKLY

Reported with minimal impact in periodical (6)

Definition: periodical · homophone

A weekly is a magazine or newspaper that comes out every seven days, so 'periodical' names it by class: a publication appearing at fixed intervals.

  • with minimal impact → WEEKLY something done with minimal impact is done feebly, and that adverb said aloud can be written a second way
  • Reported — Something reported reaches you by ear, so what counts about the words beside it is the noise they make, not their spelling.

A story that made the papers but barely left a mark on anybody.

'Periodical' is the definition and also the word holding the surface up, since a report appearing in a magazine is exactly what the sentence appears to describe.

19 down LOANER

Recluse concealing a source of something for days or more (6)

Definition: source of something for days or more · container

A loaner is one who loans, so it is the source of whatever you have been given the use of for days or longer.

  • Recluse → LONER a recluse keeps away from company, which is what this word for a solitary sort means
  • a → A the clue's own indefinite article, one letter taken as it stands
  • concealing — What conceals a thing closes around it, so the letter named next is hidden inside the word before it.

A hermit keeping quiet about where something he has had for days came from.

'A source of something for days or more' is a roundabout way of naming whoever lends you a thing — the garage that hands you a car while yours is being fixed.

20 down LOCK IN

Part of clock I never restrict the movement of (4,2)

Definition: restrict the movement of · hidden word

To lock something in is to fasten it so it cannot shift, which is restricting its movement exactly.

  • clock I never → LOCKIN six consecutive letters, beginning one letter into the first word and running through the next two
  • Part of — 'Part of' says the answer is a piece of what follows, sitting there already and in order.

A component of a clock whose movement the speaker says they never restrict.

'Movement' does double work: it is the horologist's word for the works of a clock, which is what the surface leans on, and the definition wants the plain sense.
