9 across UP-AND-DOWN

Not on the level? Dunno, Paw tricked over 500 (2-3-4)

Definition: Not on the level? · anagram

Something up-and-down is not level, and the phrase carries the further sense of erratic — the reading the crooked surface is nudging you towards.

  • Dunno, Paw → DUNNOPAW eight letters of fodder, taken as they stand
  • 500 → D the Roman numeral for five hundred, joined to the fodder
  • tricked — Someone tricked has been turned about by another hand, and these letters likewise end up in an arrangement nobody put them in.

A boy mumbling 'dunno' at his father, who has just been swindled out of five hundred.

The question mark is doing real work: 'not on the level' reads as dishonest in the surface, which is the sense the swindle sets up, and it means literally not flat.

10 across RHINO

Neighbour of 7 right at home wearing nothing (5)

Definition: Neighbour of 7 · charade

The animal at 7 down shares the African plains and the zoo enclosure next door with this one, so the cross-reference defines by neighbourhood rather than by meaning.

  • right → R R is right, as on a map or in a stage direction
  • home → H H marks the home team in a printed fixture list
  • wearing → IN whatever everyone is wearing this season is what is in
  • nothing → O the letter shaped like a zero stands for nothing

A neighbour answering his front door with no clothes on.

'Wearing' looks like a container instruction telling you to slot one piece inside another, and it is a plain synonym here — four small pieces laid end to end, nothing nested.

11 across ON PAPER

How many like to solve crosswords in theory? (2,5)

Definition: How many like to solve crosswords · double definition

Solvers work on paper rather than on a screen, and a scheme that works on paper is one that works in theory but may not survive contact with reality.

  • How many like to solve crosswords → ONPAPER with a pen and the printed grid, rather than on a screen
  • in theory → ONPAPER how a plan looks before anybody tries to carry it out

A survey question about how crossword solvers prefer to tackle the puzzle.

The join between the two definitions is invisible because 'in theory' finishes the question so naturally that it reads as part of it rather than as a second meaning.

12 across XENOPUS

When reviewing, I used to love her work about new frog genus (7)

Definition: frog genus · container + reversal

Xenopus is the genus that African clawed frogs belong to, so the definition names a rank in the scientific classification rather than offering a synonym.

  • I used to love her → XE the woman you used to love is your ex, taken backwards
  • new → N N is the standard abbreviation for new
  • work → OPUS an opus is a composer's or a writer's work
  • When reviewing — Reviewing is looking back over something, so the piece it introduces is read from its end to its start.
  • about — About means round, so the letter it governs is wrapped by the pieces either side of it instead of being parked at one end.

A critic looking back over the output of a woman he was once in love with.

Xenopus is the African clawed frog, the laboratory animal that made much of twentieth-century embryology possible. Nothing in the surface sounds like taxonomy, which is the point of writing it as a critic's reminiscence.

13 across SERFS

They’re not free to work (5)

Definition: They’re not free to work · cryptic definition

Serfs were bound to a lord's land and could not leave it or hire themselves out elsewhere, so they were literally not free to work where they chose.

  • They’re not free → The surface pushes 'free' towards costing nothing: labour somebody would have to be paid for, rather than labour anyone is at liberty to withhold.
  • to work → The setter's sense. These are people with no say over whether they work, for whom, or on whose land — the freedom in question is legal, not financial.

Workers who will not do the job for nothing.

There is no wordplay to unpick: the whole clue is one description, and the solve is spotting that 'free' means at liberty rather than unpaid, so the sentence is describing a legal status.

14 across BERING SEA

Breeding grebes in a N Pacific location (6,3)

Definition: N Pacific location · anagram

The Bering Sea lies between Siberia and Alaska at the northern limit of the Pacific, so the definition fixes it geographically instead of defining a word.

  • grebes in a → GREBESINA nine letters of fodder, taken as they stand
  • Breeding — Breeding throws two sets of characteristics together into a new one, so what comes out of it is made of the same material in a different combination.

Grebes nesting somewhere on a stretch of cold northern water.

Water birds and a sea belong to the same picture, and that is what hides the anagram: 'breeding grebes' reads as a description of the place rather than an instruction about its letters.

16 across GIVE IT SOME WELLY

Yes, I will get move to Reform … and put the boot in? (4.2,4.5)

Definition: put the boot in · anagram

Welly is British for a wellington boot and by extension for force, so giving something some welly and putting the boot in are both kicking hard.

  • Yes, I will get move → YESIWILLGETMOVE fifteen letters of fodder, taken as they stand
  • Reform — To reform a thing is to shape it again, and letters reformed are the same letters given a new shape.

A defector agreeing to a move to Reform and promising to kick his old party while he is at it.

'Reform' is capitalised so that it reads as the political party, and that capital is the whole misdirection — it is an instruction about letters, not a destination.

19 across TOOTHLESS

Moves casually round hotel, speed initially being ineffective (9)

Definition: ineffective · container + first letter

Toothless means unable to bite, and so, of a law or a watchdog or a threat, unable to have any effect at all.

  • Moves casually → TOOTLES to tootle is to travel in an unhurried way, as in tootling down the lane
  • hotel → H H is hotel in the NATO alphabet
  • speed → S the opening letter of the word
  • round — Going round a thing is going about it on every side, so the word before closes over the letter that comes next.
  • initially — Initially means at the start, so only the opening letter of the word in front of it is taken.

A leisurely amble around a hotel, with speed nowhere in the picture.

Nothing in the surface suggests teeth: the hotel and the speed build a picture of somebody dawdling, and 'being' is joinery holding that picture against the definition.

21 across MOTEL

Books sweetheart into empty Montreal accommodation (5)

Definition: accommodation · container + outer letters + middle letter

A motel is roadside accommodation, rooms built for travellers arriving by car, so the definition names the plain category it belongs to.

  • empty Montreal → ML the city's name with its insides removed, leaving the outer pair
  • Books → OT the Old Testament, the books of the Bible in crossword shorthand
  • sweetheart → E the heart of the word sweet is its middle letter
  • into — What goes into a thing ends up inside it, so the pieces named before land within the letters that follow.
  • empty — An empty word keeps only its walls, the first letter and the last, with everything between them gone.

Somebody booking their sweetheart a room in a deserted Montreal.

'Books' looks like the verb the sentence needs and is a noun in the wordplay, the Old Testament — the booking the surface describes never happens at all.

22 across TANTRIC

Type of sex from Thailand grotesque – runs to join in (7)

Definition: Type of sex · container + charade

Tantric names the slow ritual practice drawn from the Tantras, Hindu and Buddhist scriptures, so 'type of sex' points at a tradition rather than an act.

  • Thailand → T T is Thailand's international vehicle code
  • grotesque → ANTIC an antic is a grotesque gesture or caper
  • runs → R R is runs on a cricket scorecard
  • to join in — Joining in means going in among the others, so the letter it introduces is dropped inside the word already assembled.

A lurid holiday story out of Thailand, with somebody hurrying to get involved.

The dash makes 'grotesque' look like the end of a description of Thailand, when it is a noun the wordplay needs whole before a cricketer's letter is pushed into it.

23 across MANKINI

Skirt covering nether regions the French rejected for beachwear (7)

Definition: beachwear · container + deletion

The mankini is a men's sling swimsuit, so 'beachwear' gives the broad category and stays deliberately bland, since naming the garment would give the joke away.

  • Skirt → MINI a mini is the short skirt of the sixties
  • nether regions → ANKLES the ankles are the lowest region of the body
  • the French → LES les is one of the French words for the, and it is taken away
  • covering — A garment covering something has that thing underneath it, so these letters wrap round the piece that follows.
  • rejected — What is rejected is thrown out, so the three letters named just before it are removed from the word they sit in.

A skirt that covers the lower body, turned down by the French in favour of something for the beach.

A mankini is the sling swimsuit Borat made famous in 2006, so 'beachwear' is being tactful rather than descriptive — and the French here are two syllables of a body part, not a nation.

24 across SIREN

Wrong about hidden warning (5)

Definition: warning · container

A siren is the wailing alarm that warns of a raid or an emergency, so 'warning' names what the thing exists to do.

  • Wrong → SIN a wrong is a sin
  • about → RE re means about, as at the head of a memo
  • hidden — Something hidden is tucked away inside something else, so these two letters sit within the word beside them.

Some wrongdoing kept quiet, and the warning that ought to come with it.

'Hidden' is not the extraction instruction it usually is — nothing is spelled out along the clue's own letters here, it is simply saying that one piece goes inside another.

25 across SET FIRE TO

Do something about sealing gap over light (3,4,2)

Definition: light · container + reversal

To light something is to set fire to it, so the definition matches as a verb in the everyday sense of lighting a fire or a cigarette.

  • Do something about → SEETO to see to a thing is to deal with it
  • gap → TFIR a rift is a gap, written the other way round
  • sealing — To seal something is to close it up around whatever is inside, so the phrase before shuts over the letters that follow.
  • over — Turned over is upside down, so the word this follows goes in the other way round.

A handyman being told to deal with the draughty gap above the light.

'Light' is the definition and it is a verb — the surface has it as the lamp on the ceiling, which is why the last word looks like part of the job description.

1 down OUT OF SIGHT

Revealed by this fog breaking up? Hardly (3,2,5)

Definition: Revealed by this fog breaking up? Hardly · &lit + charade + anagram

Read straight, the clue says the thing is hardly revealed even as the fog breaks up, which is what being out of sight amounts to.

  • Revealed → OUT a secret that has been revealed is out
  • this fog → THISFOG seven letters of fodder, taken as they stand
  • breaking up — Fog breaking up is fog coming apart into scattered patches, and letters told to come apart land in a new order.

Fog thinning over a landscape, and the question of whether it has shown you anything yet.

Every word works twice: the sentence asks whether the lifting fog has revealed anything and answers its own question, while the same words hand over OUT and seven letters to reorder.

2 down KASPAROV

Master on board climbing wooden mast stops playing (8)

Definition: Master on board · container + reversal

Garry Kasparov was world chess champion, so 'master on board' picks him out as the master at the board rather than a ship's officer.

  • wooden → KAO oak is the wood, and it climbs the entry rather than descending it
  • mast → SPAR a spar is the pole a sail is set on
  • playing → V v for versus, the way a fixture list writes two sides playing each other
  • climbing — Something climbing a down entry is written from the bottom upwards, so the wood named here goes into the grid back to front.
  • stops — To stop a hole is to plug it, so the piece named next is driven into the middle of what is already there.

A ship's master swarming up the wooden mast, and somebody's game being brought to a halt.

The board is a chessboard, not a deck: 'Master on board' is the entire definition, and every nautical word after it — climbing, mast, spar — is there to keep you at sea.

3 down ADOPTS

Assumes trouble with Potus regularly (6)

Definition: Assumes · charade + alternate letters

To adopt a name, a pose or a habit is to assume it — both verbs mean taking something on rather than supposing it.

  • trouble → ADO much ado about nothing: ado is fuss and trouble
  • Potus → PTS the first, third and fifth letters, counted off in twos
  • regularly — Anything done regularly happens at a fixed interval, so letters are taken at every second position instead of all of them.

Trouble turning up on schedule wherever the American president goes next.

POTUS is the standing acronym for the President of the United States, which is what lets the surface read as a news headline instead of a set of letters to count through.

4 down BOOR

I don’t like that Republican lout (4)

Definition: lout · charade

A boor is a coarse, ill-mannered man, which is what 'lout' names, though lout leans more on thuggishness and boor on rudeness.

  • I don’t like that → BOO the noise a crowd makes when it does not like what it is watching
  • Republican → R R is the standard abbreviation for a Republican in American politics

A heckler writing off a Republican politician as an oaf.

'I don't like that' is not a synonym but a piece of theatre: the crowd's own noise of disapproval, spelled the way a novel would print it.

5 down IN EXTREMIS

One following gets careless, almost dying (2,8)

Definition: dying · charade + deletion

In extremis means at the point of death, so 'dying' translates it; the phrase also survives in the weaker sense of a desperate last resort.

  • One → I the Roman numeral for one
  • following → NEXT the following item is the next one
  • careless → REMIS careless work is remiss, here one letter short
  • almost — A word given almost in full stops one letter short, so the word just before it arrives without its last letter.

A follower growing sloppy, and very nearly paying for it.

In extremis is the Latin that both the church and the law use for the point of death, which is why a plain English participle can define a phrase in another language.

6 down BRAND-NEW

Mint tea with a touch of nutmeg added (5-3)

Definition: Mint · container + first letter

Something in mint condition has never been used or handled, which is exactly what brand-new claims.

  • tea → BREW a brew is a pot of tea, and it opens up to take the rest
  • with → AND and does the same joining job as with
  • a touch of nutmeg → N a touch of a word is the first letter of it
  • added — What is added to a brew goes down into it, so these letters are dropped inside the word rather than tacked on the end.

A pot of herbal tea being improved with a scrape of nutmeg.

'Mint' is the trap: it sits in front of 'tea' looking like the herb, and it is the definition, from the condition of a coin fresh off the press.

7 down HIPPOS

‘Animals’ in Post Office beginning to sweat (6)

Definition: ‘Animals’ · charade + first letter

The answer is one kind of animal among thousands, so the definition works only by example — which is what the quotation marks are owning up to.

  • in → HIP what is in is what is hip — fashionable
  • Post Office → PO PO is the abbreviation on every British high street
  • sweat → S the opening letter of the word
  • beginning to — A word only beginning is a word not finished, so nothing but its opening letter is taken.

Animals loose in the post office, and somebody starting to perspire about it.

The quotation marks flag the first word as a definition by example rather than a synonym, and 'in' is the first piece of wordplay, not the preposition the sentence appears to need.

8 down NOUS

Sense it could be largely pointless (4)

Definition: Sense · deletion

Nous is the down-to-earth kind of sense — gumption, knowing what to do — so 'sense' names it in its everyday British use.

  • pointless → NOUSE something pointless is no use, written as two words run together
  • largely — Largely means for the most part and not the whole of it, so the phrase gives up its final letter.

Somebody dismissing a thing as having no point to it whatever.

Nous came into English from the Greek for mind and means practical common sense. The wordplay only opens once 'pointless' is read as the phrase 'no use' rather than as an adjective.

14 down BASKET CASE

Like Kelvin and the others in bed, not a reliable type (6,4)

Definition: not a reliable type · container + charade

A basket case is someone who cannot cope, so 'not a reliable type' names the person; the phrase began as brutal military slang for a maimed soldier.

  • bed → BASE a bed is the base a thing rests on — a bed of concrete, a bed of gravel
  • Like → AS as does the same job as like in a comparison
  • Kelvin → K K is the kelvin, the SI unit of temperature
  • and the others → ETC etc is short for et cetera, Latin for and the rest
  • in — In is the plainest container instruction there is: everything named before it goes inside the word that follows.

Kelvin and his friends tucked up in bed, one of them not to be counted on.

'Like Kelvin' invites you to picture a bloke, and the wordplay wants the temperature unit's symbol instead — the whole sleeping arrangement is decoration around a single letter.

15 down ANY OLD IRON

Scrap music hall staple (3,3,4)

Definition: Scrap · double definition

The rag-and-bone man's cry meant waste metal for sale, which is the first sense, and the same phrase titles the music hall song that made it famous.

  • Scrap → ANYOLDIRON the rag-and-bone trade's name for whatever waste metal is worth collecting
  • music hall staple → ANYOLDIRON a song from 1911 that every hall in the country could get an audience singing

Somebody proposing to scrap the act every music hall used to rely on.

'Any Old Iron' was Harry Champion's 1911 music hall song, still sung at British singalongs, and its title was lifted straight from the scrap dealer's street cry.

17 down INHERENT

Where you may find me contracted not natural (8)

Definition: natural · charade + deletion

An inherent quality belongs to a thing by its own nature, so 'natural' matches it as an adjective in the sense of innate rather than unspoilt.

  • Where you may find me → INHERE the speaker is in here, wherever here happens to be
  • not → NT contracted to n't, the way it is in can't and won't
  • contracted — A contracted word is written short with an apostrophe standing in for what has gone, which costs this one its middle letter.

The definition is the single word at the end, and it does the least work in the sentence: everything before it is a set of directions dressed up as somebody speaking.

18 down LATRINES

Where do soldiers go? Are they required at the front during occupations? (8)

Definition: Where do soldiers go? · container + first letters

Latrines are the trench or camp toilets an army digs for itself, so 'where do soldiers go' is a euphemism standing in as a definition.

  • occupations → LINES a line of work is an occupation — and an army holds lines too
  • Are they required → ATR the opening letters of the three words
  • at the front — The front of a word is its opening letter, and the front is also where soldiers serve, which is what makes this instruction so easy to walk past.
  • during — Whatever happens during something happens inside it, so these letters are set within the word that follows.

Questions from somebody planning an army camp: where the men go, and what the front line needs.

Definition and wordplay both end in question marks, so nothing marks the seam between them: the first question is the definition and the second is pure instruction.

20 down OWNERS

They have a point, debtors admitting (6)

Definition: They have · container

Owners are the people who have things, so 'they have' defines them by the bare fact of possession, which is all the word claims.

  • debtors → OWERS people who owe are owers, the noun the crossword builds straight off the verb
  • a point → N N for north, one of the four points of the compass
  • admitting — To admit somebody is to let them in, so the word before opens up and takes the other letter inside it.

An argument about money in which the people in debt turn out to be right.

'They have a point' reads as one phrase conceding an argument, and the definition stops two words in — the point is a compass bearing and belongs to the wordplay.

21 down MANTIS

Insect (a different one) is trapped under Tom’s foot (6)

Definition: Insect · charade + last letter

A mantis is the insect that waits with its forelegs folded as though praying, so 'insect' is the plain category and does nothing more than name it.

  • Tom’s → M the last letter of the name, standing at its foot
  • a different one → ANT a different insect from the one being defined
  • is → IS the word itself, lifted straight into the answer
  • trapped under — Trapped under something is held below it, and below in a down entry means later in the answer.
  • foot — The foot of a word is its bottom end, which in a down entry is its final letter.

An insect caught under Tom's shoe, and not the one you would expect to find there.

The bracketed aside is the fairest thing in the clue: it warns you outright that the second creature is wordplay rather than the answer, a courtesy setters rarely extend.

22 down TOSH

Both gutted he’s out? Flipping rubbish! (4)

Definition: rubbish · outer letters + reversal

Tosh is British slang for nonsense, so an exclamation of 'rubbish!' is the same dismissal in a different register.

  • he’s → HS the outer letters, with the middle one taken out
  • out → OT the outer letters again, the same operation on the next word
  • Both gutted — Gutted means the insides have been taken out, and 'both' says to do it to each of the two words that follow, leaving their outsides.
  • Flipping — Flipping a thing turns it over, so what has been gathered is read the other way round — and it doubles as the mild swearword the surface wants.

Two supporters distraught at a batsman being given out, and somebody insisting it was never a dismissal.

'Gutted' works twice over: it is the surface's slang for bitterly disappointed and the instruction to hollow a word out, and 'both' is what tells you the hollowing happens twice.

23 down MUTT

My best friend’s letter to Times? (4)

Definition: My best friend · charade

A mutt is a mongrel dog, and the dog is proverbially man's best friend, so the definition works through that proverb rather than any dictionary sense.

  • letter → MU mu is the twelfth letter of the Greek alphabet
  • Times → TT t is time, and a pair of them is times

A letter to the newspaper, apparently written by the writer's closest friend.

The dog is proverbially man's best friend, which is the whole definition, and 'Times' is capitalised to point at the newspaper rather than at multiplication.
