Observer Everyman Crossword No 4,149

Across

1 across MACE

Masculine one's club (4)

Definition: club · charade

club → MACE: the medieval weapon is a heavy staff with a spiked head, and the word still names the ceremonial staff carried into parliament.

  • Masculine → M M is the standing abbreviation for male or masculine, on forms and in dictionaries alike
  • one → ACE the card with a single pip on it, which is also the one on a die

Four letters and two pieces is as small as a charade gets, so the whole clue rests on spotting that 'one' is a card rather than a number.

3 across UPPER CRUST

Posh people's stilted, curt supper (5,5)

Definition: Posh people · anagram

Posh people → UPPER CRUST: society's top layer, the phrase borrowed from a pie, where the crust is the bit sitting above everything else.

  • curt supper → UPPERCRUST ten letters handed over entire, and every one of them is used
  • stilted — Speech that is stilted is stiff and unnatural, not sitting the way it ought to, and letters that will not sit properly are letters to be reordered.

The surface is a dinner party where nobody is enjoying themselves. A curt supper is an odd thing to say, and oddness of phrasing is usually the fodder announcing itself.

10 across TRIPOLI

Caught thrice as much in port on the Med (7)

Definition: port on the Med · homophone

port on the Med → TRIPOLI: the Libyan capital sits on the North African coast of the Mediterranean and is that country's main harbour.

  • thrice as much → TRIPOLI three times the amount is triply, and said at speed that is what you write down
  • Caught — To catch what somebody says is to hear it, so the words after this one are to be taken by ear and then spelled some other way.

'Caught' looks like the cricket scorer's C, which is the commonest one-letter piece in the puzzle world. Here it is the ear doing the catching, not the fielder.

11 across VULTURE

My food is usually late (7)

Definition: My food is usually late · cryptic definition

My food is usually late → VULTURE: a scavenger that eats carrion, and since 'the late Mr Smith' is how English says dead, its dinner is already deceased.

  • My food → the sentence is spoken in the first person, so the speaker is whatever eats the meal being complained about
  • is usually late → the surface grumbles about dinner arriving behind schedule; read 'late' as it is read in 'the late Mr Smith' and the meal has already died

There is nothing here to take apart: the whole sentence is one sly description, pivoting on a single word that means tardy in the surface reading and deceased in the setter's.

12 across HOODWINKS

Tricks gangster; makes cheeky gesture (9)

Definition: Tricks · charade

Tricks → HOODWINKS: both are verbs for deceiving somebody, the answer keeping an older sense of blindfolding — cover the eyes and the victim sees nothing.

  • gangster → HOOD hood is short for hoodlum, the American word for a thug on somebody's payroll
  • makes cheeky gesture → WINKS the small cheeky gesture one eye can make on its own

The semicolon does the misdirection, making the clue look like two separate observations about a criminal, when the first word alone is the definition and everything after it builds.

13 across LAUGH

Roar in slaughterhouse? (5)

Definition: Roar · hidden word

Roar → LAUGH: to roar is to do this loudly and helplessly, as in a roar of laughter, so the noise stands in for the thing itself.

  • slaughterhouse → LAUGH five letters in a row inside the long word, waiting where nobody would think to look
  • in — The little word says the letters are sitting inside the long one that follows, unbroken and in the order given, rather than being clued separately.

A slaughterhouse is the least amusing place in the language, and that is the joke — the question mark is Everyman conceding that nobody makes this noise in one.

14 across OUTSTANDINGLY

Unsettled, Lucy's vacuous to extraordinary degree (13)

Definition: to extraordinary degree · charade + outer letters

to extraordinary degree → OUTSTANDINGLY: the adverb built on standing out from everything around, so it measures how much rather than simply praising.

  • Unsettled → OUTSTANDING an unsettled bill is an outstanding one — money owed and not yet paid
  • Lucy → LY hollowed out, the name is left holding only its first and last letters
  • vacuous — A vacuous remark has nothing inside it, so the name beside the word keeps its outside and loses everything in the middle.

'Unsettled' begs to be read as an anagram indicator and is not one: it is a whole word standing in for another, which is the trap Everyman most often lays in first position.

18 across DIRTY MARTINIS

Blue bird is getting drinks (5,8)

Definition: drinks · charade

drinks → DIRTY MARTINIS: definition by example — a martini clouded with olive brine is one specific cocktail, and both words are plural together.

  • Blue → DIRTY a blue joke is a dirty one — the colour has meant smutty since the music halls
  • bird → MARTIN a small bird of the swallow family, the house martin being the common British one
  • is → IS two letters taken exactly as printed

A blue bird sounds like a species and is not one: the colour is doing slang duty at the front while the actual bird waits in the next word.

20 across SALVO

Some colossal voles attack (5)

Definition: attack · hidden word

attack → SALVO: a volley of guns fired at once, and by extension any sudden concerted burst of aggression, verbal as readily as military.

  • colossal voles → SALVO five letters in a row that take no notice of the gap between the two words
  • Some — Some of a thing is a part of it, so what is wanted is a stretch of letters out of the words that follow, not the words themselves.

Giant voles are odd enough to be worth staring at, and staring at the picture is precisely what stops a solver reading the letters straight through the space.

21 across SKINFLINT

Miser's ciggie paper and lighter (9)

Definition: Miser · charade

Miser → SKINFLINT: somebody so tight they would skin a flint sooner than pay for a new one, an old insult that survives as one word.

  • ciggie paper → SKIN a skin is British slang for a cigarette paper, the kind you roll your own with
  • lighter → FLINT the little stone inside a lighter that the wheel strikes a spark from

Everyman builds the whole clue out of one smoker's pocket, which is why the two halves sit together so comfortably that neither announces itself as a separate piece.

23 across RISOTTO

Mashed roots: it is a carby dish (7)

Definition: a carby dish · anagram

a carby dish → RISOTTO: the Italian rice dish, and rice is carbohydrate, so the definition names it by what it is mostly made of.

  • roots → ROOTS five letters straight from the clue
  • it → IT the two letters printed next, joining the mix
  • Mashed — Mashing food breaks it up and pushes it into a new shape, so the letters beside the word are to be broken up and reset the same way.

Mashed roots sounds like the dish being described, which is the misdirection: the vegetables are only fodder, and the meal at the end of the clue is a quite different one.

24 across AVARICE

A rector gripped by one sin - and another (7)

Definition: another · container

another → AVARICE: greed for money, a second entry on the same list of deadly sins as the failing the wordplay has just spelled out.

  • one sin → AVICE a single moral failing is a vice, and 'one' supplies the article standing in front of it
  • A rector → AR the article A, then R, the abbreviation for a rector in a church listing
  • gripped by — To be gripped is to be held tight on all sides, so these letters are pushed in between the letters doing the holding.

'One sin ... and another' is a favourite setter's join: the first phrase is raw material for the wordplay and the second is the definition, quietly borrowing its noun from the first.

25 across MASTER KEYS

Pass grade-eight piano? Such should open many doors (6,4)

Definition: Such should open many doors · charade

Such should open many doors → MASTER KEYS: single keys cut to work every lock in a building, and the phrase carries its figurative sense of opportunities too.

  • Pass grade-eight → MASTER grade eight is the top rung of the British music-exam ladder, and getting through it means you have the instrument mastered
  • piano → KEYS what the fingers press on that instrument

The wordplay reads as a verb and its object — what you must do to pass the exam — while the answer is a pair of nouns, and 'Such' is the pivot between the two readings.

26 across ERIE

Receding land seen in Great Lake (4)

Definition: Great Lake · reversal

Great Lake → ERIE: one of the five great lakes on the Canada-US border, so the clue defines by example rather than by any synonym.

  • land → EIRE Eire is the Irish state's own name for itself, four letters of country
  • Receding — Something receding is going back the way it came, so the letters beside it are read from the far end towards the near one.

A country and a lake made of the same four letters, one written the way the other is read the other way round — the smallest joke in the grid, and the neatest.

Down

1 down MATCHBOX

See fight as source of illumination (8)

Definition: source of illumination · charade

source of illumination → MATCHBOX: strike one of the sticks it holds and you have a flame, which is where household light came from before switches.

  • See → MATCH at the poker table, to see a bet is to match it — you put up the same amount
  • fight → BOX to box is to fight with gloves on, under rules

The surface is a scuffle witnessed in the street. 'See' sitting at the front looks like an instruction to look at something, and the poker sense is what rescues it.

2 down CHINO

Bit of starch in old cotton (5)

Definition: cotton · hidden word

cotton → CHINO: the hard-wearing twill that trousers are cut from, so the fabric is named by the raw material it is woven out of.

  • starch in old → CHINO five letters in a row that pay no attention to either gap between the words
  • Bit of — Only part of what follows is wanted, and the part is a run of letters lifted out of the middle rather than any whole word.

'Bit of starch' reads like an instruction to take the S off the front, which is the trap: the bit wanted is five letters long and does not begin where the word does.

4 down POIGNANCY

Tattered canopying giving aura of sadness (9)

Definition: aura of sadness · anagram

aura of sadness → POIGNANCY: the quality of touching something tender in you — a sharpness of feeling rather than plain grief.

  • canopying → POIGNANCY nine letters, every one reused and none added
  • Tattered — Something tattered has been torn about and left in disarray, which is exactly the treatment the word beside it is being promised.

A tattered canopy over a graveside is the picture the surface paints, and it is a good one: the sadness feels like part of the story rather than the definition waiting at the end.

5 down ELVIS

Glimpse of Everyman, 55, is one gyrating hips no more (5)

Definition: one gyrating hips no more · first letter + charade

one gyrating hips no more → ELVIS: the swivelling hips were the King's trademark, and 'no more' is the gentle English way of saying he is dead.

  • Everyman → E the letter the byline starts with
  • 55 → LV fifty is L and five is V in Roman numerals, written side by side
  • is → IS two letters taken exactly as printed
  • Glimpse of — A glimpse is the briefest possible look at something, so what is taken from the name after it is the letter it starts with and nothing more.

Everyman signs his puzzles, so his name in a clue normally means the setter himself. Here it is being read one letter at a time, and the age beside it is numerals.

6 down COLD LIGHT OF DAY

Fact: Holly did go bananas: it's easier to see now (4,5,2,3)

Definition: it's easier to see now · anagram

it's easier to see now → COLD LIGHT OF DAY: the morning-after clarity in which a thing is looked at plainly, without whatever excitement was blurring it.

  • Fact → FACT four letters straight from the clue
  • Holly did go → HOLLYDIDGO ten more, taken entire, name and all
  • bananas — To go bananas is to go wildly out of control, and letters out of control will not stay in the order they were printed.

Two colons make this read as a piece of gossip about somebody, and the italics on 'did' are the surface insisting on the story. Neither the punctuation nor the emphasis carries an instruction.

7 down URUGUAYAN

A S American, you are heard, you said, touring Guyana (9)

Definition: A S American · charade + homophone + anagram

A S American → URUGUAYAN: S is the usual shorthand for South, and the clue defines by example — this is one particular kind of South American.

  • you are → UR said aloud, the two words are the names of two letters, and the names are all that is kept
  • you → U the same trick once more, this time on a single word
  • Guyana → GUAYAN the six letters of the country next door, shuffled into another order
  • heard — What is heard arrives by ear, so the words before it are to be listened to and then written down however English spells that sound.
  • said — Said means spoken rather than printed, so this scrap too is handed over as a noise rather than as a spelling.
  • touring — A tourist wanders about with no fixed route, and letters sent wandering do not come back in the order they left.

Both sounds lean on the same trick, that a letter's name said aloud is a whole word, and the country supplying the rest is the one a solver would find on the same map.

8 down TEETHE

Support article that's cut, in a way (6)

Definition: cut, in a way · charade

cut, in a way → TEETHE: a baby cutting its first teeth is doing this, so 'cut' is fair in that one sense and no other.

  • Support → TEE the little peg a golf ball is balanced on
  • article → THE the definite article, the commonest three letters in the language

'In a way' is the setter admitting the definition is a special case, and it is doing honest work: no ordinary sense of cutting will get you there, only the nursery one.

9 down HOT-WATER BOTTLE

Trouble: spirit that's taken to bed (3-5,6)

Definition: taken to bed · charade

taken to bed → HOT-WATER BOTTLE: the rubber bottle filled from the kettle and carried under the covers, named here by what anyone does with it.

  • Trouble → HOTWATER to be in hot water is to be in trouble, deep enough to matter
  • spirit → BOTTLE bottle is British slang for nerve or courage — somebody who loses their bottle has lost their nerve

The surface promises a ghost story, or a night on the spirits. Both halves turn out to be idioms instead, and neither means the thing the sentence wants it to.

15 down TO-DO LISTS

Fuss over tips in lists of chores (2-2,5)

Definition: lists of chores · charade

lists of chores → TO-DO LISTS: the written reminders of jobs still outstanding, which is the everyday name for exactly that piece of paper.

  • Fuss → TODO a to-do is a commotion — 'what a to-do' is what you say when a small crisis breaks out
  • over tips → LISTS a ship that tips over to one side is said to list

'Fuss over' begs to be read as one phrase, and that is where the join is hidden: the second word belongs with what comes after it, not with what comes before.

16 down IMAGINARY

Fabulous Armani suit's third piece granny vacuously altered (9)

Definition: Fabulous · anagram + third letter + outer letters

Fabulous → IMAGINARY: not 'marvellous' but the older sense, of fable — the dragon and the unicorn are fabulous because nobody has ever met one.

  • Armani → ARMANI six letters of the Milanese fashion house, taken whole
  • suit → I count three letters into the word and stop
  • granny → GY emptied of its inside, the word is down to its outer pair
  • third piece — The suit here is a word rather than a garment, and the piece wanted is the single letter standing third as you count in from the front.
  • vacuously — To be vacuous is to be empty inside, so the name keeps its two outside letters and surrenders the middle.
  • altered — Something altered is no longer as it was, and letters told to alter will not stay in the order the clue prints them.

Three instructions in a row is a great deal to spot, and the surface — a grandmother taking in a designer suit — is smooth enough to hide every one of them.

17 down AS IT WERE

Weariest: 'done in', so to speak (2,2,4)

Definition: so to speak · anagram

so to speak → AS IT WERE: both are the aside you drop in to flag that what you just said is a figure, not the literal truth.

  • Weariest → ASITWERE eight letters used once each, nothing added and nothing left over
  • done in — Somebody done in has been wrecked or worn out, and a word in that state cannot hold its letters in their proper order.

'So to speak' looks like an invitation to listen for a sound. It is not — it is the definition, and the phrase in quotation marks is what stirs the letters.

19 down ASHRAM

Sheep behind tree: retreat! (6)

Definition: retreat · charade

retreat → ASHRAM: a Hindu religious community set apart from the world, so it is a retreat in the sense of a place rather than a flight.

  • tree → ASH a common British hardwood whose three letters make it a crossword favourite
  • Sheep → RAM the male of the flock
  • behind — Whatever is behind something comes after it, so the animal is set down following the tree rather than in front of it.

The exclamation mark sells 'retreat!' as an order to run for it. The word is a noun here, and a quiet one: the place people go to, not the thing an army does.

21 down STORK

Principally, supposedly, the one relaying kids? (5)

Definition: Principally, supposedly, the one relaying kids? · first letters + &lit

Read straight, the whole clue describes the bird folklore credits with delivering babies — 'relaying kids' is the flippant version, and 'supposedly' concedes it is only a story.

  • supposedly, the one relaying kids → STORK the opening letter of each of the five words, read in the order they are printed
  • Principally — A principal is the chief or first one, so the letters wanted are those standing at the head of each word that follows.

The sentence works twice over: read straight it describes the bird, and read as an instruction it spells that same bird out of its own beginnings. That is what an &lit is.

22 down ICIER

More risky - heading off - more remote (5)

Definition: more remote · deletion

more remote → ICIER: said of a person rather than a place — an icier greeting is a colder, more distant one, so the remoteness is manner, not miles.

  • More risky → DICIER dicey means chancy, and the comparative of dicey is what these two words ask for
  • heading off → the D at the front is the letter that leaves; nothing is put in its place
  • heading off — A head is the front of a thing, and heading off says that front letter departs, leaving the rest of the word to close up behind it.

The dashes make 'heading off' look like an aside dropped into the middle of a sentence. It is the instruction, and the two phrases either side are doing straightforward work.
