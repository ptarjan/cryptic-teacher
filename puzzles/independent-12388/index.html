Across

1 across DUNDERHEAD

Idiot's late checking French and German articles on hotel (10)

Definition: Idiot · container + charade

Dunderhead is a plain old word for a blockhead, someone too thick to take anything in, so it swaps straight in for idiot.

  • late → DEAD the late Mr So-and-so is the dead one, and this word is the one prised open
  • French → UN un is the French for a or an, the indefinite article
  • German articles → DER der is the German for the, and the plural 'articles' covers this one and the French one before it
  • hotel → H H is hotel in the radio alphabet, where every letter is given a word
  • checking — To check a thing you open it up and look inside it, so the word named closes round whatever is being inspected.

A guest arrives late and the desk goes through his French and German paperwork.

'Late' is the euphemism for dead rather than for unpunctual, and 'hotel' is H in the radio alphabet that pilots and police use.

6 across SCAR

Cliff Wilde's leaving capital (4)

Definition: Cliff · deletion

In northern England a scar is a bare cliff of rock on a hillside, as in the Yorkshire Dales' Attermire Scar.

  • Wilde's → OSCAR Oscar Wilde, on first-name terms
  • capital → O the capital letter is the big one standing at the front of a name, and it is the one that goes
  • leaving — what leaves is gone, so the letter named is simply no longer there when the word is written out

Oscar Wilde walking out on the capital city.

'Capital' is not London and not a country's chief city but the upper-case letter, and 'Cliff' is dressed up as a first name to match Wilde.

10 across TITHE

Name hospital hiding large sum paid to church (5)

Definition: sum paid to church · substitution

A tithe was the tenth of a household's produce owed to the parish church, so it is literally a sum paid to church.

  • Name → TITLE a title is the name a book or a person goes by
  • hospital → H H is the hospital on a British road sign, and it is the letter moving in
  • large → L is the size on a clothing label, and it is the letter being covered up
  • hiding — hiding a thing puts it out of sight, so one letter disappears and the letter doing the hiding stands in its place

A hospital keeping quiet about a big donation to the parish church.

'Name' is the noun and not the verb, and 'hiding' does two jobs at once: the furtiveness of the surface and the swap in the wordplay.

11 across RECEIVERS

Wind-up merchants in playground serve nuts around one (9)

Definition: Wind-up merchants · charade + anagram + container

A receiver is appointed to wind up a company and sell off its assets, so the definition uses that sense of wind up rather than the teasing one.

  • playground → REC the rec is the recreation ground, the scrap of park a British town keeps for its children
  • serve → SERVE five letters of fodder, taken as printed
  • one → I the Roman numeral for one, which the shuffled letters close around
  • nuts — nuts means out of one's mind, and letters in that state cannot be trusted to stay in the order given
  • around — what goes around something finishes up on both sides of it, so the single letter named ends up in the middle

Playground teasers dishing out nuts at about one o'clock.

'Wind-up merchants' is the joke: not people who tease for sport but the insolvency officials brought in to wind a failing company up.

12 across CAROTID

Dishonest bloke stealing bread in vessel (7)

Definition: vessel · container

The carotid carries blood up the neck to the head, so vessel is being used in its anatomical sense rather than its nautical one.

  • Dishonest bloke → CAD a cad is a man who behaves dishonourably, the old British word for a rotter
  • bread → ROTI roti is the flatbread of the Indian subcontinent
  • stealing — a thief carries the goods off inside his coat, so what is stolen ends up within the word that took it

A scoundrel pinching a loaf from a boat.

'Vessel' is the trap: the surface has a boat in it and the answer wants the anatomical sense, the artery running up each side of the neck.

13 across TORONTO

Dog from Kansas biting director Howard in Canadian location (7)

Definition: Canadian location · container

Toronto is Canada's largest city, on Lake Ontario, so the definition names the class and the answer supplies one instance of it.

  • Dog from Kansas → TOTO Dorothy's little dog in The Wizard of Oz, blown out of Kansas along with her
  • director Howard → RON Ron Howard directed Apollo 13 and A Beautiful Mind
  • biting — teeth close right around whatever they bite, so the letters named are held inside the word doing the biting

A film director being nipped by a dog that has wandered up from Kansas.

'Dog from Kansas' has to be one particular dog rather than a breed, and 'director Howard' one particular Howard — two names dressed up as descriptions.

14 across WINTER SPORTS

Women footballers (Italian) exercise with special competitions on cold surfaces (6,6)

Definition: competitions on cold surfaces · charade

These are the competitions held on snow and ice — skiing, skating, bobsleigh — so the cold surface is exactly what defines the group.

  • Women → W W is the standard abbreviation for women, as on a changing-room door
  • footballers (Italian) → INTER Inter Milan are one of Italy's great football clubs, known by the first half of their name
  • exercise → SPORT sport is exercise taken for its own sake
  • special → S S is the short form of special, as in special edition

Italian women footballers training with special events staged on frozen ground.

'Footballers (Italian)' is one named club rather than a nationality, and the bracket is the only signal you get that a proper noun is wanted.

18 across UNREGISTERED

Not yet signed in, urge resident to get moving (12)

Definition: Not yet signed in · anagram

Someone unregistered has not been entered on the list — not yet signed in, whether at a hotel desk or on an official roll.

  • urge resident → URGERESIDENT twelve letters of fodder, the two words taken as printed
  • to get moving — told to get moving, a thing will not stay where it was put, and neither will these letters

Chivvying a tenant who still has not put their name down.

The definition sits at the front and reads like the start of the same sentence about a tenant, so the hard part is deciding where it stops.

21 across COMMITS

Promises to take away support from governing bodies (7)

Definition: Promises · deletion

To commit to something is to promise it, binding yourself to a course — a different sense from committing an offence.

  • governing bodies → COMMITTEES committees are the bodies that govern clubs, schools and institutions
  • support → TEE a tee is the little peg that supports a golf ball, and those three letters are the ones taken out
  • to take away — taking something away subtracts it, and the letters on either side close up behind the gap

Pulling the backing that the governing bodies had pledged.

'Support' is the golfer's peg rather than money or a supporter, and the long word to find first is handed to you as a plain description.

23 across ELEGANT

Graceful English member introducing a collection of books (7)

Definition: Graceful · charade

Elegant means graceful — pleasingly restrained in line or movement, said of a dancer, a dress or a mathematical proof.

  • English → E E is the standard abbreviation for English
  • member → LEG a member is a limb, and the leg is the limb wanted here
  • a → A the word a goes straight into the assembly, letter for letter
  • collection of books → NT the New Testament is a collection of books and abbreviates to NT
  • introducing — to introduce something is to bring it in, so what follows joins on to the letters already in place

An English MP bringing a whole library into the chamber.

'Member' is the anatomical sense rather than the MP the surface is drawing, and the New Testament is the shorter, second half of the Bible.

24 across IN TRANSIT

International trains crashing en route (2,7)

Definition: en route · anagram

Something in transit is en route — on its way between sender and destination, the phrase a parcel tracker prints.

  • International → INT int is the usual short form of international on a timetable or a fixture list
  • trains → TRAINS six letters of fodder, the word taken as printed
  • crashing — a crash leaves everything in a heap in no particular order, which is what becomes of these letters

A news bulletin about cross-border rail services colliding somewhere on the line.

'Crashing' is the anagram signal and the accident in the picture at once, which is why the clue reads as a headline rather than as an instruction.

25 across TALON

Abbreviated lecture covering predatory avian's weapon (5)

Definition: predatory avian's weapon · deletion + charade

A talon is the hooked claw of a bird of prey, an eagle's or a hawk's tool for seizing and killing.

  • lecture → TALK a talk is a lecture, and it is the word that gets clipped
  • covering → ON a talk on birds is a talk covering birds, so the word supplies two letters of its own
  • Abbreviated — an abbreviated word is cut short, so the last letter of the word named never arrives

A shortened lecture that deals with the claws of hunting birds.

'Covering' looks like an instruction to wrap one piece round another and is really a word in its own right, the small one meaning about.

26 across NORM

Standard minor majorette carries (4)

Definition: Standard · hidden word

A norm is a standard — the level or pattern taken as usual, as in 'below the norm for this time of year'.

  • minor majorette → NORM four consecutive letters straddle the gap between these two words
  • carries — what a word carries it holds inside it, in order and unchanged, waiting to be noticed

A junior baton-twirler carrying the regulation kit.

Minor and majorette are chosen to look like a matched pair of size words, which keeps the eye on their meanings instead of on the join between them.

27 across CANDIDATES

May and Penny, I see, meeting society contenders (10)

Definition: contenders · charade

Candidates are the people contending for a post, a seat or a prize, so contenders is the plain plural synonym.

  • May → CAN may and can are the two everyday words for being permitted
  • Penny → D d was the old penny, from the Latin denarius, until Britain went decimal in 1971
  • I → I the pronoun goes straight in as a single letter
  • see → DATE to see someone, in the romantic sense, is to date them
  • society → S S is the abbreviation for society in the names of clubs and institutions

The speaker notices that May and Penny are moving in society circles.

May and Penny read as two Conservative politicians who really were leadership contenders, and 'I see' reads as an aside when it is two more pieces of the build.

Down

1 down DETACH

Cut off end of beard plus other facial hair when cycling (6)

Definition: Cut off · charade + last letter + cycling

To detach something is to cut it off — unfasten it from what it was joined to, as a locomotive is detached from its train.

  • beard → D the last letter of the word beard, taken on its own
  • other facial hair → ETACH a tache is a moustache, with its final letter rolled round to the front
  • end of — the end of a word is its final letter, and that single letter is all that is taken
  • when cycling — a cycle brings the same point round again, so one letter travels from the back to the front while the rest keep their order

A cyclist trimming the end of his beard and his moustache too.

'Tache is how a moustache is spoken of in Britain, apostrophe and all, and the pedalling in the surface hides an instruction about where a letter travels.

2 down NATURE

Character periodically going through in last quarter (6)

Definition: Character · alternate letters

A person's nature is their character — the settled disposition they are credited with, as in good nature or a suspicious nature.

  • in last quarter → NATURE take every second letter across these three words, starting from the second letter of the first
  • periodically — something done periodically happens at regular intervals, so the letters are picked at a regular interval too
  • going through — going through a phrase means travelling along it from one end to the other, collecting as you go

Somebody drifting in and out of the office during the final three months of the year.

'Last quarter' reads as the accountant's three months or the moon's phase and is neither: those words are only a strip of letters to count along.

3 down ELECTRIC GUITAR

Regulate critic playing instrument (8,6)

Definition: instrument · anagram

Instrument covers the answer by example: the clue names the class and one particular member of it has to be supplied.

  • Regulate critic → REGULATECRITIC fourteen letters of fodder, the two words taken exactly as printed
  • playing — playing about with something is messing it around, and letters messed around come out in a different order

A reviewer having a go on stage instead of writing about the show.

'Playing instrument' wants to be the definition; 'playing' is the shuffle and only the last word defines. Everything before it is fodder.

4 down HERODOTUS

Champion Mark crowning America's old historian (9)

Definition: old historian · charade

Herodotus wrote his Histories in the fifth century BC and is called the father of history, so old historian names him exactly.

  • Champion → HERO the champion of a cause is its hero
  • Mark → DOT a dot is the smallest mark you can make on paper
  • America's → US US is the standard short form of the United States
  • crowning — what crowns a thing sits on top of it, and in a down entry the top is where the letters start

A champion named Mark placing the crown on an elderly American historian.

Every capital letter here is a decoy: Champion is a common noun and Mark is a mark, and the only proper name wanted is the one being defined.

5 down ASCOT

Place where you can get better type of neckwear (5)

Definition: Place where you can get better · double definition

Ascot is the Berkshire racecourse where a better goes, and an ascot is also the wide silk cravat worn with morning dress.

  • Place where you can get better → ASCOT a better is one who bets, so the place wanted is a racecourse and not a hospital
  • type of neckwear → ASCOT the broad silk cravat, looped and pinned, worn with morning dress

A shop that stocks a better class of tie than the one you are wearing.

The clue reads as one sentence about a shop with smarter ties, and it splits after 'better' into two senses, neither of them retail.

7 down CLEANERS

Initially Narnia author's welcoming less bulky dailies (8)

Definition: dailies · container

A daily is a domestic help who comes in every day, so the plural names them by how often they turn up rather than by the work.

  • Narnia author's → CS the man who wrote the Narnia books is known by two initials and a surname
  • less bulky → LEANER something less bulky than another thing is leaner
  • Initially — initially asks for initials only, so the man is taken by the two letters he signed himself with rather than his full name
  • welcoming — to welcome someone is to take them indoors, so the letters outside open up and let the rest in

The creator of Narnia making room for slimmer newspapers.

A daily, in British usage, is the person who comes in each day to clean, which has nothing to do with the newspapers the surface is selling.

8 down RESPONSE

Reaction of press with one running riot (8)

Definition: Reaction · anagram

A response is a reaction — the reply or effect that something provokes, from a person, a market or the body.

  • press → PRESS five letters of fodder, the word taken as printed
  • one → ONE the number goes in spelled out in full, not as a numeral
  • running riot — a crowd running riot is beyond anyone's control, and letters out of control will not stay in the order they were given

The newspapers and a single protester going wild over something.

'Press' is journalism in the picture and only a bag of letters in the wordplay, and 'one' joins in as the word rather than as I or 1.

9 down MISREPRESENTED

Gave wrong account of my boss supporting girl reportedly dealing with gift (14)

Definition: Gave wrong account of · charade + homophone

To misrepresent something is to give a false account of it, and the past tense of the definition matches the -ed ending exactly.

  • girl → MIS an unmarried girl is a miss, and saying that word aloud needs only one S when it is written into the build
  • dealing with → RE re heads a memo to mean concerning, which is what dealing with a matter amounts to
  • gift → PRESENT a gift is a present
  • my boss → ED ed is the editor, the boss of anyone filing copy to a paper
  • reportedly — reportedly means as reported aloud, so it is the sound of the word that counts and not its spelling
  • supporting — a support holds up whatever rests on it, so in a down entry these letters sit right at the bottom

Somebody told lies about their editor for backing a girl said to be handling a present.

The setter's own boss is the editor, a standing crossword joke, and 'supporting' puts him at the foot of the column rather than behind the girl.

15 down RE-ELECTED

Once again put in bookstand sailors left behind (conserved by Oliver in film) (2-7)

Definition: Once again put in · deletion + container

To be re-elected is to be put in again, voted back into the seat or office you already held.

  • Oliver in film → REED Oliver Reed, the film actor, and his surname splits after three letters to make room
  • bookstand → LECTERN a lectern is the sloped stand a reader's book rests on
  • sailors → RN RN is the Royal Navy, and its two letters are the ones that do not come
  • left behind — what is left behind does not come along, so those two letters stay where they were and only the rest travels
  • conserved by — to conserve something is to keep it safe within, so the name closes around whatever is left

A bookstand the sailors abandoned, put back into place once more.

The bracket is a definition by example pointing at one particular Oliver — the actor, not the Dickens boy or Cromwell — and 'in film' is all you get to tell you which.

16 down JUNCTION

Maybe crossroads where Jack gets oil (8)

Definition: crossroads · charade

A crossroads is one kind of junction, the general word covering any place where roads, railway lines or wires meet.

  • Jack → J J is the jack in a pack of cards, printed in the corner
  • oil → UNCTION unction is the consecrated oil of anointing, as in the last rites' extreme unction
  • Maybe — maybe flags a for-instance, so what follows is one example of the answer rather than a word meaning the same thing

A man called Jack striking oil where two roads cross.

Unction is a churchy word for anointing oil and it is worth banking: setters reach for it whenever they want seven letters of oil.

17 down PROMOTER

Organiser spilling more port (8)

Definition: Organiser · anagram

A promoter is the person who organises and puts on an event — a gig, a fight, a festival — so organiser is the plain synonym.

  • more port → MOREPORT eight letters of fodder, the two words taken as printed
  • spilling — spilled drink goes everywhere at once, and letters that spill land in no particular order

A party organiser knocking over yet another glass of port.

The port is the fortified wine rather than the harbour or the left-hand side, and 'spilling' belongs to the clumsy host as much as to the letters.

19 down BALLET

Risk involving everyone producing Romeo and Juliet? (6)

Definition: Romeo and Juliet · container

Prokofiev's Romeo and Juliet is one of the standard works of the repertoire, so the title names an example of the form rather than a synonym.

  • Risk → BET a bet is money put at risk on an outcome
  • everyone → ALL all is everyone taken together
  • involving — to involve someone is to draw them into the middle of the business, which is where these letters end up

A gamble that draws in the whole company and ends up staging Shakespeare's lovers.

Shakespeare wrote the play, but the italics and the question mark ask for the danced version — Prokofiev's — standing in for a whole art form.

20 down STANDS

Puts up with two saints (6)

Definition: Puts up with · charade

To stand something is to put up with it, as in 'I can't stand him' — a sense of the verb quite apart from being on your feet.

  • saints → ST St is the everyday abbreviation of saint, as in St Paul's
  • two → AND naming two of a thing spells out the little word that joins one to the other
  • saints → S S is the shorter abbreviation for saint, used where space is tight

Somebody enduring the company of a pair of holy men.

Saint has two abbreviations and the clue wants one of each, with the joining word written out between them — a trick worth remembering whenever a count appears.

22 down SASHA

Young Obama's first belt? (5)

Definition: Young Obama · charade

Sasha is the younger of Barack and Michelle Obama's daughters, so the definition names one particular young Obama rather than describing her.

  • belt → SASH a sash is a broad band worn round the waist or across the chest
  • first → A A comes first in the alphabet, and the question mark covers its being named ahead of the band rather than after it

A boy called Obama showing off the first belt he has won at judo.

The definition is a real person, the younger of the two Obama daughters, and the wordplay names the two pieces of her name in the opposite order to the way they stack.
