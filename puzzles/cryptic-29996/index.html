1 across ANYBODY

Who will volunteer in a big city matter? (7)

Definition: Who will volunteer · charade

'Who will volunteer?' is answered by naming nobody in particular, and ANYBODY is exactly that pronoun — any person at all, chosen by none.

  • a → A the clue's own single letter, taken exactly as it stands
  • big city → NY NY is New York, the big city a setter reaches for when two letters are wanted
  • matter → BODY matter as opposed to spirit — the physical stuff a thing is made of

A show of hands being called for over some piece of metropolitan business.

A call for a volunteer is answered by naming no one in particular, which is how a question can do a definition's job here.

5 across NO CAN DO

In retrospect working both sides of casino is impossible (2,3,2)

Definition: impossible · charade + reversal + outer letters

NO CAN DO is the flat spoken refusal — it cannot be done — so the phrase stands in for 'impossible' as a whole idiom.

  • working → NO a machine that is working is on, and that little word is set down backwards
  • both sides of casino → CANDO the outer letters of the word are C and O, and calling for both of them — C and O — writes the joining word into the middle
  • In retrospect — Looking back over what has just been said, so in an across entry the letters are written out the other way, right to left.
  • both sides of — The sides of a word are its first and last letters, and both of them are wanted with nothing from the middle.

Someone who has been playing both ends of a casino thinking better of it afterwards.

'Both sides' looks as though it can only ever hand over two letters, and the pair named as a pair carries an extra three between them.

10 across SAT-NAV

This might help vehicle that’s regularly going the wrong way (3-3)

Definition: This might help vehicle that’s regularly going the wrong way · &lit + charade + alternate letters + reversal

A sat-nav is precisely the thing that helps a driver forever heading the wrong way, so the whole sentence reads straight as its description.

  • vehicle → VAN a van is one, and it arrives back to front
  • that’s regularly → TAS letters one, three and five of this word, the apostrophe ignored
  • regularly — Taken at regular intervals — the first, third and fifth letters of the word beside it, and none of the ones between.
  • going the wrong way — The wrong way is the reverse of the right one, so in an across entry the pieces are written out from right to left.

A driver who keeps taking wrong turnings and could do with some help.

Every word is working twice over: the sentence describes what the gadget is for while the same words hand over a vehicle and three letters to turn round.

11 across FLEA-BITE

Rather get away without minor inconvenience (4-4)

Definition: minor inconvenience · container

A flea-bite itches for a moment and does no real harm, so the phrase stands for any inconvenience too small to be worth minding.

  • get away → FLEE to flee is to get away, and the word opens up before its last letter
  • Rather → ABIT 'rather cold' and 'a bit cold' say the same thing, so the phrase goes in whole
  • without — Without is used in its old sense of outside — as in 'without the city walls' — so the piece before it wraps round the one before that.

Preferring to slip off rather than put up with a small annoyance.

The everyday 'without' meaning lacking is the trap: here the word is the archaic preposition for outside, and it carries the clue's only instruction.

12 across OUT OF THE QUESTION

Abroad with old newspaper – ambassador has issue 5 (3,2,3,8)

Definition: 5 · charade

5 across is the flat refusal that means it cannot be done, and this phrase says the same: a thing ruled out before anyone discusses it.

  • Abroad → OUT somebody abroad is away, and out is the plain word for it
  • old → O o = old, the standard abbreviation
  • newspaper → FT the FT is the Financial Times, the pink-paper City daily and the crossword's stock newspaper
  • ambassador → HE an ambassador is styled His or Her Excellency, shortened to HE on the envelope
  • issue → QUESTION the issue before a meeting is the question before it

An envoy overseas with a back number of a newspaper, and a matter he wants raised.

The definition is the bare number 5, which sends you to another clue for the meaning — a Guardian habit worth knowing, and easy to read as part of the sentence.

13 across BARNET

Bob maybe in N. London borough (6)

Definition: Bob maybe · double definition

In rhyming slang a barnet is a head of hair, and a bob is one style of it; Barnet is also a genuine borough of north London.

  • Bob maybe → a bob is one particular cut, and 'maybe' flags it as an example of the wider thing rather than the thing itself
  • N. London borough → one of the local authorities at the top of London, out past Finchley and Hendon

A man called Bob who lives somewhere in north London.

Barnet Fair is Cockney rhyming slang for hair, cut down to its first word the way rhyming slang always is, and the borough of that name is a real place.

14 across HANDMAID

Attendant doctor possessing perfect penmanship at the outset (8)

Definition: Attendant · charade + container

A handmaid is a female personal servant, so 'Attendant' names her by the job itself: one who attends on somebody else.

  • penmanship → HAND your hand is your handwriting — a fair hand, an illegible hand
  • doctor → MD MD is the doctor's degree, and the two letters part company to make room
  • perfect → AI A1 means first-rate, and in a grid the 1 is written as the letter I
  • possessing — What you possess you have about you, so the piece named first opens up and takes the next one into its middle.
  • at the outset — At the outset means at the very start, so this piece goes to the front of the answer rather than where the clue names it.

A doctor who turns out to have beautiful handwriting.

Doctors' handwriting is the joke the surface runs on. 'At the outset' is not part of that description at all — it is the instruction sending the penmanship to the front.

15 across WALTZ

Unknown joining Disney to make music (5)

Definition: music · charade

A waltz is a piece in three-four time as well as the dance done to it, so 'music' names the tune rather than the steps.

  • Disney → WALT Disney's own first name, the man rather than the studio
  • Unknown → Z algebra's unknown quantity is x, y or z, and only one of the three fits here
  • joining — Joining says the two pieces simply run together end to end, one written straight after the other.

A stranger teaming up with Disney to record something.

'Unknown' is algebra's word rather than a mystery guest, and the two pieces arrive in the answer in the opposite order from the one the clue names them in.

16 across SQUEAMISH

Crush West when revolutionary invades – being easily shocked (9)

Definition: easily shocked · container + reversal

Someone squeamish is turned queasy by blood or cruelty, so 'easily shocked' describes the same low threshold for anything unpleasant.

  • Crush → SQUISH to squish a thing is to crush it, and this word splits after three letters
  • West → EAM Mae West, the Hollywood star, lends her first name, and it turns round on the way in
  • revolutionary — A revolution turns a thing full circle, so the name beside it is written out the other way round.
  • invades — An invader forces a way in, so those letters end up inside the word already assembled rather than beside it.

A revolutionary invading the West and crushing it.

'West' is a person rather than a direction — Mae West, the 1930s film star — and the political surface is what keeps you reading it as territory.

19 across SUBSIDIES

Financial assistance from founders securing a little investment (9)

Definition: Financial assistance · container + first letter

Subsidies are sums paid out to keep a service or an industry going, so 'financial assistance' is the category they belong to.

  • founders → SUBSIDES a boat that founders subsides — goes down — and the word opens before its last two letters
  • investment → I the opening letter of the word and no more of it
  • securing — To secure a thing is to hold it fast, so the word beside it closes round the letter named next.
  • a little — A little of a word is not the whole of it — here only the letter it begins with.

Backers putting up a modest sum to keep something afloat.

'Founders' looks like the people who started the business and is really the verb — a ship that founders sinks — which is the whole difficulty in reading the first three words.

21 across SCOPE

Small PC close to price range (5)

Definition: range · charade + last letter

The scope of a law or a job is how far it reaches, which is what a range is — the extent something covers.

  • Small → S s = small, as on the label in a shirt
  • PC → COP a PC is a police constable, and a cop is the same officer in slang
  • price → E the last letter of the word
  • close to — The close of a word is its end, so only the final letter of the word named after it is taken.

A cheap little computer, priced to shift.

'PC' reads as the machine on your desk and means the constable on the beat, and 'Small PC' looks like one description when it is two separate pieces.

24 across VIRULENT

Particularly noxious six books on order (8)

Definition: Particularly noxious · charade

A virulent disease or a virulent attack is poisonous in the extreme, so 'particularly noxious' catches both the sense and the force of the word.

  • six → VI six written in Roman numerals
  • order → RULE a rule is an order handed down, and the two nouns swap freely
  • books → NT the New Testament, the crossword's usual books alongside the OT
  • on — In an across entry a piece placed on another follows it, so the books come after the word for order rather than before it.

Half a dozen books waiting on an order at a shop.

'Six books' reads as one quantity on an invoice, which is what keeps a Roman numeral and the Bible's second half from separating in your head.

26 across HURDLE

Uhura discovered characters bringing up the rear in red will die in difficulty (6)

Definition: difficulty · charade + middle letters + last letters

A hurdle is a barrier to be got over, so it stands for any difficulty in the way — the figurative use of the racing obstacle.

  • Uhura → HUR the Star Trek communications officer's name with the letter at each end taken off
  • red will die → DLE the last letter of each of these three words, read in order
  • discovered — Taking the cover off a word leaves what was underneath, so the first and last letters go and the middle stays.
  • characters bringing up the rear — The character bringing up the rear is the one at the back, so it is the final letter of each word that is wanted.

The Star Trek crewmen in red shirts, the ones who never make it to the end of the episode.

Red shirts are the old Star Trek joke — the anonymous crewman beamed down in a red tunic is the one who dies — and Uhura wore red on the bridge herself.

27 across ICE SHEETS

They’re melting variety of cheeses with it (3,6)

Definition: They’re melting · anagram

The polar ice sheets, the vast caps over Greenland and Antarctica, are the things famously melting, so the clue defines them by the news about them.

  • cheeses → CHEESES anagram fodder, seven letters
  • it → IT two more letters for the shuffle, lifted from the clue as they stand
  • variety of — A variety is another form of the same thing, so the letters beside it come back arranged a different way.

A cheeseboard left out too long, the whole selection going soft.

'Variety of cheeses' is such a natural phrase that the instruction hides inside it, and 'with it' sounds like the rest of the same menu.

28 across TOODLE-OO

Also manage to hold sign saying farewell (6-2)

Definition: saying farewell · charade + container

Toodle-oo is a jaunty British goodbye, dated now but still heard, so the whole phrase stands in for a wave at the door.

  • Also → TOO also and too are the same word in different registers
  • manage → DO to do is to manage — 'that will do' — and the word splits in two
  • sign → LEO Leo the lion is one of the twelve signs of the zodiac
  • to hold — To hold a thing is to keep it within you, so the word before opens up and takes the sign into its middle.

Someone managing to hold up a placard as well, on the way out.

'Sign' has a dozen crossword meanings and the zodiac is the one setters reach for most, while 'to hold' carries the only instruction in the sentence.

29 across ESKIMO

Originally, everyone sees knitting is my love language (6)

Definition: language · first letters + charade

Eskimo names the language of the Arctic peoples — a term now usually avoided in favour of Inuit, but the one dictionaries still file the language under.

  • everyone sees knitting is my → ESKIM the opening letter of each of these five words in turn
  • love → O love is nothing in tennis, and nothing is written as O
  • Originally — The origin of a word is the letter it starts from, and the run of words after this one gives up one letter each.

Someone declaring that knitting is how they show affection.

Five initials in a row are hard to see when the words make a sentence that means something, and 'love language' is a modern phrase the surface leans on.

30 across ENGROSS

Occasionally teenagers obsess and completely engage (7)

Definition: completely engage · alternate letters

To engross someone is to take up the whole of their attention, so 'completely engage' spells out the verb with its intensity included.

  • teenagers obsess → ENGROSS every second letter across both words, beginning with the second letter of the first
  • Occasionally — Something done occasionally happens every so often rather than all the time — take every second letter and leave the rest behind.

Teenagers now and then getting fixated on something.

The two words have to be read as one continuous run, because the count carries straight over the space between them — which is what makes this kind of clue hard to spot.

31 across OSTRICH

Huge egg supplier in Germany – east of Berlin – I must get right involved (7)

Definition: Huge egg supplier · charade + container

An ostrich lays the largest egg of any living bird, so 'Huge egg supplier' identifies it by the one thing it is famous for producing.

  • east of Berlin → OST east as they say it in Berlin
  • right → R r = right, the standard abbreviation
  • I → ICH the German for 'I', which is what the clue's own pronoun turns into
  • in Germany — Said in Germany means said in German, so two of the words that follow are wanted in that language rather than in English.
  • involved — Getting something involved brings it in among the rest, so the letter named lands inside the pieces already assembled.

A German supplier of enormous eggs, somewhere east of Berlin.

'In Germany' is doing translation work on two separate words a long way apart, and the dashes make the middle of the clue read as an aside about geography.

2 down NIAGARA

Falls over climbing crag out of bounds (7)

Definition: Falls · charade + reversal + middle letters

Niagara is the great waterfall on the border between Canada and the United States, so 'Falls' defines it by the class of thing it is.

  • over → NIAGA to do a thing over is to do it again, and that word is written upwards here
  • crag → RA the pair left when the first and last letters of this word go
  • climbing — Climbing goes upwards, and in a down entry that means these letters are read from the bottom of the answer towards the top.
  • out of bounds — The bounds of a word are the letters at either end, and they are sent out, leaving only what sat between them.

A climber going over a cliff face somewhere off the permitted route.

'Falls over' reads as one phrase about a tumble; the definition stops at the first word and 'over' is a piece of the wordplay.

3 down BANKNOTES

Cash that folds inside turban knot essentially (9)

Definition: Cash that folds · hidden word

Banknotes are the paper half of money, the cash that literally folds into a wallet, as against the coins that do not.

  • turban knot essentially → BANKNOTES nine letters in a row, starting partway through the first word and running across two gaps
  • inside — Inside means already sitting within, so a run of consecutive letters is lying untouched in the words that follow.

Money folded away inside the knot of a turban.

A long hidden run is the easiest kind to walk past, because the eye reads 'turban knot' as one object and never spells it out letter by letter.

4 down DEVOTE

Commit seconds to advising Gen X? (6)

Definition: Commit · charade + second letters

To devote time to a cause is to commit it there, and the two verbs take the same objects — commit an hour, devote an hour.

  • advising Gen → DE the letter standing second in each of these two words, in the order given
  • X? → VOTE the X pencilled on a ballot paper is a choice cast, and the question mark flags that this is one use of the letter
  • seconds — A word's second is the letter standing in second place, and one is taken from each of the two words named after it.

Someone spending their spare seconds handing out advice to Generation X.

'Gen X' reads as one label for a generation and then splits in two, the Gen lending a letter and the X sending you off to a polling booth.

6 down OLEANDER

Refined lead ore contains note – it’s poisonous if ingested (8)

Definition: it’s poisonous if ingested · anagram + container

Oleander is a common garden shrub whose every part is toxic to eat, so the clue defines it by its one notorious property.

  • lead ore → LEADORE anagram fodder, seven letters
  • note → N n = note, the standard abbreviation, taken inside the shuffle
  • Refined — Refining ore breaks it down and casts it again, so the words beside it will not stay in the order they are printed.
  • contains — What a thing contains sits within it, so the letter named next goes into the middle of the shuffled letters.

A smelter turning out refined lead ore with something lethal mixed into it.

Lead is poisonous too, which is what holds the surface together and makes it easy to attach the definition to the metal instead of a plant.

7 down ALBUM

Record American left behind (5)

Definition: Record · charade

An album is a record in the sense of a released collection of songs, the disc a band puts out rather than a written account of events.

  • American → A a = American, the standard abbreviation
  • left → L l = left, as in stage directions or on a map
  • behind → BUM bum is the everyday British word for the backside, which is what a behind is

An American who has been left behind somewhere.

The whole sentence reads as a note about somebody abandoned abroad, and its last word is the rude one — a setter's favourite, because 'behind' hides it so politely.

8 down DETAINS

Stays in ground instead (7)

Definition: Stays · anagram

To stay someone is to hold them where they are, the older transitive sense alive in 'a stay of execution', which is what DETAINS does.

  • instead → INSTEAD anagram fodder, seven letters
  • ground — Grinding a thing reduces it to loose material, and letters ground down go back together in some new arrangement.

Staying on the ground as a substitute for whatever was planned.

'Stays' is the transitive verb here — to stay something is to hold it back — which is why a word about remaining can describe an act done to someone else.

17 down MUCK-RAKER

Scandalmonger’s best mate covers chest up (4-5)

Definition: Scandalmonger · container + reversal

A muckraker digs up dirt about people and sells it, which is the trade a scandalmonger follows.

  • best mate → MUCKER a mucker is British slang for a pal, and the word opens after three letters
  • chest → KRA an ark is a chest — the Ark of the Covenant was a box — and it is written upwards
  • covers — A cover goes over a thing and round it, so this word closes over the letters named after it.
  • up — In a down entry up means read from the bottom towards the top, so those letters run in the opposite direction.

A gossip columnist's closest friend keeping his chest covered up.

Both halves of the surface change job at once: 'chest' is a box rather than a body part, and 'covers ... up' is two separate instructions with a word between them.

18 down IDLENESS

Vegetating, comic Eric Morecambe is finally visiting Naxos on vacation (8)

Definition: Vegetating · charade + outer letters + last letters + container

Vegetating is doing nothing whatever, and idleness is the state of doing nothing, so the gerund and the noun name the same condition.

  • comic Eric → IDLE Eric Idle of Monty Python, one of two comic Erics the clue could mean
  • Naxos on vacation → NS the Greek island emptied out, keeping only the letter at each end
  • Morecambe is finally → ES the last letter of each of these two words, in the order given
  • finally — At the finish — the last letter of each word named before it, and nothing else from either.
  • visiting — A visitor goes inside the house, so these letters sit within the piece named after them.
  • on vacation — A place on vacation stands empty, so this word keeps only the letters at its two ends and loses everything between.

Eric Morecambe holidaying on a Greek island and doing nothing much at all.

Two Erics collide: the surface hands you Morecambe, the great British comic of the seventies, while the wordplay wants Idle — and Morecambe keeps only his last letter.

20 down UNICORN

Everyone can see this news about independent business run as symbol of Scotland (7)

Definition: symbol of Scotland · charade + container

The unicorn is Scotland's heraldic animal, facing England's lion on the royal arms, so the definition names it by what it stands for.

  • Everyone can see this → U a film classified U is universal, passed for viewers of any age
  • news → NN n = new, and the plural asks for a second one, which is what leaves a gap between them
  • independent → I i = independent, as printed beside a candidate's name on a ballot
  • business → CO co = company, the abbreviation in half the firm names on a high street
  • run → R r = run, the cricket scorer's abbreviation
  • about — Round about — the letters named before it end up on either side of everything that follows.

A story everyone can read about an independent firm and the way it is run.

Turning 'news' into two N's is the cheeky step: new is N, and the plural asks for more than one of them. The beast is Scotland's, chained on the royal arms.

22 down POLEMIC

Compile 12 to make argument (7)

Definition: argument · anagram

A polemic is a fierce written attack on a position, so 'argument' names it in the sense of a case pressed against something.

  • Compile → COMPILE anagram fodder, seven letters
  • 12 — The number sends you to 12 across, which supplies the word 'out', and letters that are out are no longer in the order they were given.

An instruction to put the answer at 12 together and start a row with it.

A cross-reference usually hands over letters; this one hands over the instruction instead, because the answer it points at is itself a word meaning out of order.

25 down UNDER

Two European articles below (5)

Definition: below · charade

Under and below are everyday synonyms for a position lower than something else, and either word fits the same sentence unchanged.

  • Two European articles → UN un is French for 'a', the first of the pair
  • European articles → DER der is German for 'the', the second of the pair

Continental languages keep a setter supplied with short words, and 'articles' is the signal to reach for a foreign 'a' or 'the' rather than an English one.
