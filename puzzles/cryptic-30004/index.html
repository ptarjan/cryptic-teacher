Across

1 across TITFER

Row about newspaper backing Panama? (6)

Definition: Panama · container + reversal

A Panama is one particular straw hat and a titfer is slang for any hat, so the definition works by naming an example.

  • Row → TIER a row of seats in a stand is a tier
  • newspaper → FT the Financial Times, Britain's pink business paper, signs itself FT
  • about — Something placed about a thing is wrapped round it, so the row closes over the letters that follow.
  • backing — A newspaper's backing is its support in the surface; as an instruction it asks for those two letters read from the back.

A quarrel about a paper coming out in favour of Panama.

'Row' is the line of seats, not the argument the surface wants, and Panama is a hat: a titfer is Cockney rhyming slang, from tit for tat.

4 across STUPID

Silly positions must be rejected – I would (6)

Definition: Silly · reversal + charade

Stupid answers to silly in the sense of lacking sense, not the softer silly of a silly hat or a silly mistake.

  • positions → PUTS to position something is to put it somewhere, so the verb matches in the third person
  • I would → ID I would shortens to I'd in speech, and the apostrophe goes
  • must be rejected — Something rejected is thrown back, and a word thrown back is read from its last letter to its first.

Someone agreeing that ridiculous stances ought to be thrown out.

'Positions' reads as opinions or postures in the surface and is a plain verb in the wordplay, and the dash covers the switch to a speaker.

9 across FEATHER ONE'S NEST

Exploit flyer with imperfect tenses to get personal benefit (7,4,4)

Definition: to get personal benefit · charade + anagram

To feather one's nest is to line it comfortably at somebody else's expense, so it means enriching yourself — benefit that is personal and usually improper.

  • Exploit → FEAT an exploit is a feat
  • flyer → HERON a long-legged river bird, and a flyer like any other
  • tenses → ESNEST the six letters of TENSES rebuilt
  • imperfect — Something imperfect is not as it should be, and letters not as they should be are out of their proper order.

Taking advantage of a leaflet whose grammar has all the tenses wrong.

The imperfect is itself a tense, which is what makes 'imperfect tenses' read as grammar rather than as an instruction, and 'exploit' looks like the verb the sentence needs.

10 across ATTEST

Bowler may be here to give evidence (6)

Definition: to give evidence · charade

To attest is to bear witness formally, which is what a witness does in the box: give evidence on oath.

  • may be here → AT the small word that says where somebody is
  • Bowler → TEST a Test is an international cricket match played over five days, the level a top bowler works at

A man in a bowler hat turning up at court as a witness.

'Bowler' is the hat the surface has you picturing in a courtroom and the cricketer the wordplay wants, and 'here' is a whole word doing the work of two letters.

11 across LANDLORD

Proprietor with joiner in funny comeback (8)

Definition: Proprietor · container + reversal

A landlord is the proprietor of a pub or of property let to tenants, so 'proprietor' is the general word for what he owns and runs.

  • funny → DROLL droll is funny in the dry, understated way
  • joiner → AND the conjunction that joins two clauses together
  • in — A joiner slotted in goes inside, so the word beside it opens up and takes those letters within it.
  • comeback — A comeback is a return along the way you came, so those letters are read from their far end back to their start.

A proprietor and a carpenter swapping witty retorts.

'Joiner' is the carpenter in the surface and the grammarian's word in the wordplay — a conjunction joins two clauses, and AND is the plainest one there is.

12 across HEN PARTY

Nightcap on vacation during jovial preparatory celebration (3,5)

Definition: preparatory celebration · container + outer letters

A hen party is the celebration held for a bride before her wedding, so 'preparatory' points at what it comes before rather than at anything it prepares.

  • jovial → HEARTY a hearty welcome is a jovial one
  • Nightcap → NP the two ends of the word, with everything between them gone
  • on vacation — A word on vacation has been emptied out, so only the letters at its two ends are left standing.
  • during — Something that happens during an event happens inside it, so these letters sit within the word beside them.

A last drink before bed, taken in the middle of a cheerful party.

A hen party is the bride's night out before a British wedding, and 'nightcap' does two jobs: the drink in the surface, and a word to be hollowed out.

14 across TOILET

John is one outwardly for hire (6)

Definition: John · container

John is the American colloquial word for a lavatory, so the definition is slang for the answer, dressed up as a man's name.

  • for hire → TOLET TO LET is what the sign says on a British property offered for hire
  • one → I the Roman numeral for one
  • outwardly — What is outward is on the outside, so the phrase this points at wraps round the letter named before it.

A man named John who looks, from the outside, as though he can be hired.

John is American slang for the lavatory, which is why the clue can open with what reads as somebody's first name and still be a definition.

15 across ABSENT

First couple of letters dispatched, so not here (6)

Definition: not here · charade

Absent means not present, and 'not here' says the same the short way — the state of somebody who has failed to turn up.

  • First couple of letters → AB the opening pair taken from the alphabet itself, in the order they come
  • dispatched → SENT a parcel dispatched has been sent

A note explaining that the first of the post has already gone out.

The letters wanted belong to no word in the clue but to the alphabet, which is why picking initials out of the clue itself gets you nowhere.

18 across SPARSITY

Sounds like Bath perhaps being in short supply (8)

Definition: being in short supply · homophone

Sparsity is the noun for how thinly something is spread, so whatever is in short supply is present only sparsely.

  • Bath perhaps → SPARSITY Bath, in south-west England, is built on hot springs and gave its name to bathing, so it is one city among the spa towns
  • Sounds like — This asks for the noise the words make rather than their spelling, so what gets written down is not what was handed over.
  • perhaps — Perhaps flags an example rather than a synonym: the place named is one of the kind wanted, not the kind itself.

A remark that Bath, of all places, is running short of something.

In a British accent the R at the join is not sounded, so two words run together into one, and a city on the map turns into a shortage.

21 across FOOTWEAR

Settle where stated in Oxford or Derby (8)

Definition: Oxford or Derby · charade + homophone

An Oxford and a Derby are two styles of lace-up shoe, so the definition points at the answer by giving a pair of examples of it.

  • Settle → FOOT to foot the bill is to settle it
  • where → WEAR said aloud the two are one noise, and only the spelling changes
  • stated — Stated means said out loud, so the word before it is written down as it sounds rather than as the clue spells it.

An instruction to pay the bill in the town named — Oxford, or else Derby.

Oxford and Derby are lace-up shoe styles as well as English cities, and the surface keeps them looking like two places on a map.

22 across ANTLER

Learnt to play the horn (6)

Definition: horn · anagram

An antler is the branched horn of a deer, so 'horn' defines by the general kind — headgear on a stag rather than anything you blow.

  • Learnt → LEARNT six letters, exactly the six the answer needs
  • to play — To play with a thing is to knock it about, and letters knocked about will not stay in the order they were given.

Somebody who has taught themselves to play the French horn.

The horn is not the one the surface plays but the one a stag carries, and 'learnt to play' reads as a single achievement rather than as fodder plus a signal.

24 across THE FAT OF THE LAND

Best in everything for big Brits (3,3,2,3,4)

Definition: Best in everything · double definition

Living off the fat of the land is having the best of everything a place offers; read literally, the fat of Britain is its heavier inhabitants.

  • Best in everything → THEFATOFTHELAND the idiom for the choicest of what a country has to offer — you live off it
  • big Brits → THEFATOFTHELAND read literally instead of as an idiom: heavy people, and the country they live in

A headline claiming that heavier Britons are doing better than everybody else.

Both readings turn on 'fat': in the idiom it is the richest part of what a country produces, and in the joke it is the people.

25 across RELIEF

Ease with which judge accepts falsehood (6)

Definition: Ease · container

Relief is the ease that arrives when something unpleasant stops — the noun for that lifting of strain, not comfort in general.

  • judge → REF a referee judges a match, and shortens to REF
  • falsehood → LIE a falsehood told on purpose is a lie
  • accepts — A judge who accepts something takes it in, so the word before opens up and the next one goes inside it.

A judge in court quietly letting an untruth go by.

The judge is not a courtroom one: a referee is the official who judges a game, and 'ease' is a noun here rather than the verb it looks like.

26 across ADHERE

Stick poster, but not over there! (6)

Definition: Stick · charade

To adhere is to stick fast to a surface — glue's sense of the word, though the answer covers sticking to a rule as well.

  • poster → AD a poster is an advertisement, AD for short
  • but not over there → HERE the opposite of over there is right here

An instruction to put the poster up on this wall and not on that one.

'Stick' is the verb meaning to glue rather than a stick of wood, and the exclamation mark makes the whole thing sound like somebody pointing at a wall.

1 down THEATRE

From Goethe, a tremendous play enacted here (7)

Definition: play enacted here · hidden word

A theatre is the building a play is performed in, so the definition names it by what happens inside rather than by any synonym.

  • Goethe, a tremendous → THEATRE the letters run on unbroken across the comma and the two spaces
  • From — Saying the answer comes from these words points at the words themselves, and the letters are already sitting in them in order.

A grand production of Goethe, played with enormous energy.

German drama in the surface and an English word underneath it: the run starts in the middle of Goethe's name, which is where nobody looks first.

2 down TO TIE THE KNOT

I then took odds on twenty in order to get hitched (2,3,3,4)

Definition: to get hitched · anagram + alternate letters

To tie the knot is the idiom for marrying and 'get hitched' is the same idea in slang, both standing for going through with the wedding.

  • I then took → ITHENTOOK nine of the twelve letters, taken exactly as they stand
  • odds on twenty → TET the first, third and fifth letters of t-w-e-n-t-y
  • odds — Odds are the odd-numbered ones, so the first, third and fifth letters of the word beside it are taken and the rest dropped.
  • in order — Putting something in order means arranging it, and letters arranged afresh come out in a sequence they were not handed to you in.

Someone betting on number twenty at long odds, hoping the winnings will pay for a wedding.

'Odds' is the bookmaker's word the surface needs and the letter-picking instruction the wordplay needs, and 'in order' reads as purpose while meaning arrangement.

3 down ELECTOR

One chooses shock treatment in role reversal (7)

Definition: One chooses · container + reversal

An elector is one who chooses in the narrow sense of casting a vote, so the word covers anybody entitled to choose at an election.

  • role → ROLE the word as it stands, waiting to be turned round
  • shock treatment → ECT electroconvulsive therapy, the psychiatric treatment that passes a current through the brain, is written ECT
  • in — Treatment given in something happens inside it, so these letters land within the word beside them rather than next to it.
  • reversal — A reversal turns a thing back to front, and what gets read from its last letter to its first here is the role.

Somebody volunteering for electric shock therapy when the roles get swapped round.

'Role reversal' is one idea in the surface and a pair of instructions underneath it, and ECT is a genuine medical abbreviation rather than a crossword invention.

6 down PENALTIES

Palestine suffering sanctions (9)

Definition: sanctions · anagram

A sanction in the political sense is a punishment imposed from outside, and penalties are exactly that — punishments handed down.

  • Palestine → PALESTINE all nine letters of the name, rebuilt into a different word
  • suffering — A country suffering is in a bad way, and letters in a bad way have come out of the arrangement they were given.

A news headline about a territory living under imposed sanctions.

The fodder and the definition sit side by side with a single word between them, and the headline reads so naturally that the signal to shuffle disappears into it.

7 down DESIREE

Want to start eating potato (7)

Definition: potato · charade + first letter

Desiree is a named variety of potato, so 'potato' defines by category: the answer is one particular kind of the thing named.

  • Want → DESIRE to want something is to desire it
  • eating → E the first letter of the word, taken off the front and nothing else with it
  • start — The start of a word is the letter it opens with, and only that one letter joins what has gone before.

Somebody too impatient to wait for the meal to begin.

Desiree is a red-skinned potato variety sold in every British greengrocer, which is how 'potato' can define what looks like a woman's name.

8 down MOTLEY

Disparate motel with a twist in the tail? End of story! (6)

Definition: Disparate · reversal + charade + last letter

Motley means made of clashing, ill-assorted parts — a motley crew — which is what disparate says about a collection of unlike things.

  • motel → MOTEL the word as it stands, before its last two letters are turned
  • story → Y the letter this word finishes on
  • with a twist in the tail — A twist in the tail turns the end round, so the last two letters of the word before swap places.
  • End of — The end of a word is the letter it finishes on, which is all that is taken from the word that follows.

A cheap roadside motel and a tale with a surprise ending.

'A twist in the tail' is what every good short story has, and the setter means it letter by letter; the exclamation mark is the tell that 'End of story!' is working too.

13 down PLENTIFUL

Fill up ten forms, that’s more than enough (9)

Definition: more than enough · anagram

Plentiful means present in more than sufficient quantity, so 'more than enough' catches the sense exactly: abundance rather than mere sufficiency.

  • Fill up ten → FILLUPTEN nine letters, the little words counted in
  • forms — Forms is the verb here: the letters beside it are made to form something, which they can only do by moving.

Being handed ten forms to complete and deciding that is quite enough bureaucracy.

'Fill up' looks like the instruction printed on the paperwork and 'forms' looks like the paperwork itself; the two swap jobs, one becoming fodder and the other the signal to rearrange.

16 down BROTHER

Relative souper? (7)

Definition: Relative · charade

A brother is a male sibling, so 'relative' defines by the wider family category the answer belongs to.

  • souper → BROTH the soup itself, of the thin clear kind
  • souper → ER the ending that turns a thing into the person who deals in it, as a bake becomes a baker

A relation described, oddly, as a maker of soup.

'Souper' is not a word, and the question mark admits it: the setter has coined an agent noun for somebody who makes soup, then spelled it a different way.

17 down TRESTLE

Prepared letters of support (7)

Definition: support · anagram

A trestle is the braced frame a table top rests on, so 'support' names the answer by function: the thing that holds another thing up.

  • letters → LETTERS the seven letters of the word itself, ready to be rearranged
  • Prepared — Preparing something means working it into a new state, and letters worked over like that do not come out as they went in.

A bundle of carefully prepared references vouching for somebody.

'Letters of support' is a real phrase, which is what makes it hard to see that 'support' has been cut off the end of it and set to work as the definition.

18 down STRIFE

First and last to arrive can generate conflict (6)

Definition: conflict · anagram + last letter

Strife is open conflict between people — quarrelling and fighting rather than a mere difference of opinion — which is the sense 'conflict' carries here.

  • First → FIRST the whole word, all five letters, not the initial of anything
  • to arrive → E the letter this word ends on
  • last — The last of a word is the letter it ends on, and only that one is taken from what follows.
  • can generate — What a thing can generate is something new made out of it, and a new word is made out of old letters by moving them.

Whoever turns up first and whoever turns up last making trouble between them.

'First and last' looks like one instruction to take two letters, and only the second half of it is one: 'first' is five letters of fodder just as it is written.

19 down ABASHED

Embarrassed to display university degree in an outhouse (7)

Definition: Embarrassed · container

Abashed means embarrassed in the particular sense of being shamed out of your confidence, which is the reading 'embarrassed' takes here.

  • an outhouse → ASHED an outhouse is a shed, and the article in front supplies the A
  • university degree → BA a Bachelor of Arts is a BA
  • in — A degree hung in a place sits inside it, so these two letters go within the words beside them.

Somebody too shy to hang their degree certificate anywhere but the garden shed.

The surface frames a certificate and hides it in an outhouse, and 'an' is not scenery: the A in front of the shed is a letter the answer needs.

20 down TWEENIE

Cute name that is for a youngster (7)

Definition: a youngster · charade

A tweenie is a child between infancy and the teens, roughly eight to twelve, so 'a youngster' names the answer by the age group it covers.

  • Cute → TWEE twee is cute to the point of being sickly
  • name → N N is the standard abbreviation for name
  • that is → IE i.e. is the Latin id est, meaning that is

A sweet little name given to a small child.

'That is' is an everyday phrase in the surface and the abbreviation i.e. in the wordplay, which is the join a solver looks straight past.

23 down TILDE

Finally enrol in crash diet mañana, got that, but not tomorrow (5)

Definition: mañana, got that, but not tomorrow · container + anagram

A tilde is the wavy mark written over the n in mañana, so the definition names the symbol on the page rather than what the word means.

  • diet → TIDE the four letters of DIET rearranged
  • Finally enrol → L the letter enrol ends on
  • Finally — Finally means at the end, so what is taken from the word after it is the letter it finishes with.
  • in — This puts the single letter inside the group beside it instead of alongside them.
  • crash — A crash leaves the pieces of a thing lying where they fell, so these letters are rebuilt in a different arrangement.

Signing up at the last minute for a crash diet, then putting it off until the day after.

The definition asks you to look at the Spanish word rather than translate it: what mañana has and 'tomorrow' has not is the squiggle over its n.
