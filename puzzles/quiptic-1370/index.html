Across

1 across PASTURE

Grazing land beyond river in Yorkshire (7)

Definition: Grazing land · charade

PASTURE is the grass land that livestock are turned out onto to feed, which is exactly grazing land.

  • beyond → PAST once you are beyond a point you are past it
  • river in Yorkshire → URE the Ure runs down Wensleydale in North Yorkshire, and setters reach for it whenever they want three quiet letters

Sheep country lying on the far side of a Yorkshire river.

British rivers are worth collecting — Ure, Exe, Cam, Dee — because a setter who needs two or three vowel-heavy letters will call on one of them every time.

5 across TRIGGER

Set off bouncy character taking Ritalin, primarily (7)

Definition: Set off · container + first letter

To TRIGGER something is to set it off, a sense borrowed straight from the part of a gun you pull to fire it.

  • bouncy character → TIGGER the tiger in Winnie-the-Pooh who bounces everywhere on his tail
  • Ritalin → R the first letter of the drug's name, and nothing else of it
  • taking — Somebody taking a pill has swallowed it, so the letter goes inside the longer word rather than beside it.
  • primarily — Primarily means first of all, so only the opening letter of the word it marks is used.

A hyperactive character being medicated to calm down.

Tigger is the bounciest character in Winnie-the-Pooh, so the surface reads as somebody prescribing him something to settle him, and 'bouncy character' does not look like a name.

10 across EMIT

Give out porridge on return (4)

Definition: Give out · reversal

To EMIT light, heat or a smell is to send it out from yourself, which is what 'give out' means as a verb.

  • porridge → TIME doing porridge is British slang for serving a prison sentence, so porridge is time
  • on return — Coming back means retracing the way you came, so the letters are read from their far end backwards.

Handing round the breakfast when somebody comes back.

Porridge for a prison stretch is British prison slang old enough to have named a sitcom, and it is one of the words a setter uses whenever a clue needs four ordinary letters.

11 across GULF STREAM

Fulmar gets confused in current across the pond (4,6)

Definition: current across the pond · anagram

The GULF STREAM is the warm current running from the Gulf of Mexico across the Atlantic to Europe, and 'the pond' is that ocean.

  • Fulmar gets → FULMARGETS ten letters of fodder, which is what the 4,6 enumeration asks for
  • confused — Something confused has lost track of what goes where, so the letters beside the word do not keep the arrangement they arrived in.

A seabird getting itself thoroughly muddled out at sea.

A fulmar is a stiff-winged North Atlantic seabird, so bird and definition share an ocean, and 'the pond' is the British nickname for that same Atlantic.

12 across INTENT

Earnest camping like this? (6)

Definition: Earnest · charade

INTENT as an adjective means fixed earnestly on something — an intent stare, intent on winning — which is the sense 'Earnest' carries.

  • camping → IN a camper spends the night inside something, and the two-letter word for inside comes first
  • camping → TENT the canvas that camper is sleeping under supplies the other four letters
  • like this? — The question points at the letters in front of you: split them into two words and they describe the state named just before.

A serious-minded camping trip, done in exactly this fashion.

Read as one word the answer is a state of mind; read as two it is where a camper sleeps, and the question mark is the setter admitting the join.

13 across NOTABLES

Celebrities can’t play snooker for this reason (8)

Definition: Celebrities · charade

NOTABLES are the people worth noting — the distinguished names on a guest list — which is what 'Celebrities' means.

  • can’t → NO the flat two-letter refusal that explains why something cannot happen
  • play snooker → TABLES snooker is played on big baize-covered tables, so the game hands over its own equipment

A room full of famous people who cannot get a game of snooker.

The reason really is spelled out: say the eight letters as two words and you have the shortage that stops the game, which is why 'for this reason' is a promise rather than padding.

14 across PACEMAKER

Map a creek for training frontrunner (9)

Definition: frontrunner · anagram

A PACEMAKER is the runner who leads a race and sets the speed for the field, so the definition names that position.

  • Map a creek → MAPACREEK nine letters of fodder, with the two A's and two E's easy to lose count of
  • for training — Training a squad knocks it into a different shape, so the letters put through it do not come out standing where they started.

Charting a small tidal inlet as part of somebody's training.

'Frontrunner' looks like one more piece of the training story, which is the whole disguise — a map and a creek belong to an exercise, not to a race.

16 across GLOSS

German defeat provides sheen (5)

Definition: sheen · charade

GLOSS is the shine on a polished surface — gloss paint, a glossy magazine — so 'sheen' is a plain synonym for the noun.

  • German → G g is the standard abbreviation for the language and the nationality
  • defeat → LOSS a match you did not win goes down as a loss

A German setback that turns out to have a bright side.

Both halves read as a single idea, a German defeat, so the join between the one-letter piece and the four-letter one leaves no seam to see.

17 across ESSEX

Some males sexualised in part of eastern England (5)

Definition: part of eastern England · hidden word

ESSEX is the county on the North Sea coast immediately east of London, so the definition places it geographically.

  • males sexualised → ESSEX five letters in a row, beginning inside the first word and finishing inside the second
  • Some — Some of a phrase is a piece of it taken as it stands, so a run of letters is wanted rather than the words themselves.

An observation about the way some men get sexualised.

A buried answer hides best inside a phrase that reads as ordinary comment, and this one sounds like a line from a newspaper column rather than a set of letters.

19 across ASIA MINOR

When I am wearing gold in part of Turkey (4,5)

Definition: part of Turkey · charade

ASIA MINOR is the old name for the Anatolian peninsula, which makes up the great bulk of modern Turkey.

  • When → AS as and when open a clause interchangeably
  • I am → IAM the two words used exactly as printed
  • wearing → IN somebody wearing black is in black, so the word stands in for the little preposition
  • gold → OR or is the heraldic word for gold, the metal on a coat of arms

Somebody describing themselves turned out in gold.

'Wearing' looks like the container instruction this clue never uses; it is a piece in its own right, standing in for a two-letter preposition.

23 across FRONT ROW

Some rugby players argue with impudence, at first (5,3)

Definition: Some rugby players · charade

The FRONT ROW is that trio of forwards at the head of the scrum, so the definition names them as one group within a rugby team.

  • impudence → FRONT front is cheek or nerve — 'what a front!' — said of somebody brazen
  • argue → ROW a row is a noisy quarrel, the one that rhymes with cow
  • at first — This fixes a position rather than picking out a letter: the piece just named goes at the start of the answer.

Rugby forwards squaring up and giving lip before anything else happens.

'At first' looks like an instruction to take initial letters and is only saying which piece leads. A front row is the two props and the hooker who bind at the head of a rugby scrum.

24 across EARNER

Student has left out source of money (6)

Definition: source of money · deletion

An EARNER is a thing that brings money in — 'a nice little earner' — so it is a source of money rather than the person paid.

  • Student → LEARNER a student is a learner, and those seven letters are what you start from
  • left → L l is the standard abbreviation, the one on a pair of gloves
  • out — Out marks a removal: the letter named just before it is taken out of the longer word it sits in.

A student who has walked out on a steady income.

'Has left' reads as departed, and that hides both halves of the instruction at once: 'left' names the letter and 'out' says what becomes of it.

26 across BERLUSCONI

Rub close-in when partying – this PM liked to (10)

Definition: this PM · anagram

Silvio Berlusconi was Italy's prime minister four separate times, so 'this PM' points at one particular holder of that office.

  • Rub close-in → RUBCLOSEIN ten letters of fodder, the hyphen ignored as hyphens always are
  • liked to → surface only: it ties the picture to the man and hands over no letters
  • when partying — A party is where order breaks down and everybody ends up somewhere they did not start, and so do these letters.

A politician with a taste for getting far too close to people at parties.

The 'bunga bunga' parties were front-page news across Europe for years, which is what lets the fodder read as biography instead of as a bag of letters.

27 across MIRE

Unpleasant situation of male anger (4)

Definition: Unpleasant situation · charade

To be in a MIRE is to be bogged down in a mess with no easy way out, which is an unpleasant situation.

  • male → M m is the standard abbreviation, the box you tick on a form
  • anger → IRE ire is the poetic word for anger, three letters setters lean on constantly

One man's temper making everything worse.

A MIRE is literally a bog, and it is the figurative sense the definition wants — stuck fast in something you cannot get yourself out of.

28 across WHEYISH

Why is husband claiming base is like dairy product? (7)

Definition: like dairy product? · charade + container

WHEYISH means resembling whey, the thin liquid left when milk curdles for cheese, so the definition points at that dairy product.

  • Why → WHY the question word spelled out, three letters waiting to be opened up
  • base → E e is the base of natural logarithms in mathematics
  • is → IS the little verb used exactly as printed
  • husband → H h is the standard abbreviation, the one on a family tree
  • claiming — To claim something is to take it in and keep hold of it, so the letter named is swallowed inside the word doing the claiming.

A husband staking a claim to the base, and somebody demanding to know why.

'Base' is the mathematician's e, the number at the bottom of natural logarithms, and it is the kind of one-letter convention worth keeping for good.

29 across TYRANNY

Absolute power of extremely tetchy grandma not good (7)

Definition: Absolute power · charade + outer letters + deletion

TYRANNY is rule by someone whose power has no check on it at all, which is what 'Absolute power' describes.

  • extremely tetchy → TY the two letters at the outer edges of the word, taken at its extremes
  • grandma → GRANNY granny is the everyday word for a grandmother
  • good → G g is the standard abbreviation, as in a grade on a school report
  • extremely — Extremely sends you to both far ends of the word it marks, so the first and last letters are all you keep.
  • not — Not marks an absence: whatever is named after it is missing from the letters you have just been given.

A short-tempered grandmother behaving at her absolute worst.

'Not good' reads as a verdict on the old lady and is really a pair of instructions: the abbreviation for good, and the word that takes it away again.

Down

2 down ARMENIA

Song about blokes in former Soviet state (7)

Definition: former Soviet state · container

ARMENIA was a republic of the USSR until 1991 and has been independent since, so 'former Soviet state' names it exactly.

  • Song → ARIA an aria is the big solo song in an opera
  • blokes → MEN blokes is everyday British for men
  • about — Something written about a subject is wrapped round it, so the first word's letters open out and the second word sits in the gap.

A tune going round about the lads, sung in a country once behind the Iron Curtain.

'About' is doing two jobs at once — what a song is about, and where one word goes relative to another — which is why the sentence never sounds like an instruction.

3 down TITLE

Perhaps Mr T wearing hat (5)

Definition: Perhaps Mr · container

Mr, Dr, Lady and Sir are all titles, so 'Perhaps Mr' defines by holding up a single example of the class.

  • T → T the letter is used exactly as printed
  • hat → TILE a tile is old slang for a hat, the sort of word that survives mainly in crosswords
  • wearing — You wear a hat on top of yourself, so the single letter ends up tucked inside the word for the headgear.

Mr T of the A-Team in a piece of headgear.

'Perhaps' is doing the work of 'for example': Mr is not the answer but one instance of it, and that little word is the only warning you get.

4 down REGATTA

A cottager wanting company turned up for sporting event (7)

Definition: sporting event · deletion + reversal

A REGATTA is a meeting of boat races held over a day or a week, so the definition names the class it belongs to.

  • A cottager → ACOTTAGER the two words run together, all nine letters, before anything comes out
  • company → CO co is the standard abbreviation, the one on the end of a firm's name
  • wanting — A thing that is wanting is short of something, so whatever is named next is missing from the letters you assemble.
  • turned up — In a down entry the answer runs towards the bottom, so reading it up the column means reading the letters the other way round.

A lonely cottage-dweller showing up for a day's sport.

'Wanting' reads as lonely in the sentence and as short of in the wordplay, which is the double duty that keeps the surface smooth enough to believe.

6 down RESITE

Change location of temporary relief, heartlessly (6)

Definition: Change location · deletion

To RESITE something is to take it down and put it up somewhere else, which is changing its location.

  • temporary relief → RESPITE a respite is a short break from something hard, and it is the letter at its dead centre that goes
  • heartlessly — A word robbed of its heart loses the letter at its exact centre, which a seven-letter word has.

A brief let-up granted without a scrap of sympathy.

'Heartlessly' reads as cruelly in the sentence and as an instruction underneath, and the convention is worth owning: the heart of a word is its middle letter.

7 down GARIBALDI

Content to snog a ribald Italian revolutionary (9)

Definition: revolutionary · hidden word

Giuseppe Garibaldi led the red-shirted volunteers whose campaigns unified Italy in the 1860s, so 'revolutionary' names the trade.

  • snog a ribald Italian → GARIBALDI nine letters in a row, starting inside the first word and stopping one letter into the last
  • Content — Taken as a noun rather than an adjective, the content of something is what sits inside it, so a run of letters is already lying there.

Somebody quite happy to kiss a coarse-humoured Italian.

'Content' is the trap: it reads as the adjective, meaning willing, and the noun is what flags the hiding place. Nine buried letters are harder to spot than four, because nobody scans for a span that long.

8 down EVADERS

People who dodge adverse exercises (7)

Definition: People who dodge · anagram

An EVADER is somebody who ducks a duty, a question or the taxman, so the plural matches 'People who dodge'.

  • adverse → ADVERSE seven letters of fodder, with two E's to keep count of
  • exercises — Exercising a body moves its parts about, so the letters put through the workout come out standing somewhere else.

A punishing set of drills that nobody is enjoying.

'Adverse exercises' reads as one ordinary phrase — hard training — which is exactly why neither word looks like it has a job to do.

9 down CLINT EASTWOOD

Twit canoodles with action film director (5,8)

Definition: film director · anagram

Clint Eastwood directed Unforgiven and Million Dollar Baby among many more, so 'film director' names the job behind the acting fame.

  • Twit canoodles → TWITCANOODLES thirteen letters of fodder, splitting 5 and 8 as the enumeration asks
  • with action — Something done with action is in motion, and letters in motion do not stay in the order they were printed.

A fool having a cuddle in the middle of an action picture.

The fodder is a complete little sentence in itself, which is the disguise — nothing in 'Twit canoodles' reads as an instruction, so the eye slides past to the film.

15 down ETERNALLY

Superficially less cross, for ever (9)

Definition: for ever · deletion

ETERNALLY means going on without any end, which is what 'for ever' says in two words.

  • Superficially → EXTERNALLY what is superficial is on the outside only, and the adverb for that is the ten letters to start from
  • cross → X a cross is written as the letter X, as on a ballot paper or the end of a note
  • less — Less works as minus here, so whatever is named after it comes away from the letters you already have.

Somebody only slightly less bad-tempered, and staying that way.

'Cross' reads as the mood in the sentence and is the shape of a letter in the wordplay, which is one of the standard ways a setter clues X.

18 down SCREECH

High pitched squawk rocks church (7)

Definition: High pitched squawk · charade

A SCREECH is a harsh high-pitched cry, the noise an owl or a set of brakes makes, which is the squawk described.

  • rocks → SCREE scree is the loose broken rock that piles up down a mountainside
  • church → CH ch is the standard abbreviation, the one printed on maps

A noise loud enough to shake a church.

'Rocks' reads as a verb, the noise shaking the building, and is a plain noun in the wordplay — the loose stuff lying on a hillside.

20 down AMENITY

So be it: sex with unknown for convenience (7)

Definition: convenience · charade

An AMENITY is a facility that makes life easier — a park bench, a laundry room — which is what a convenience is, public ones included.

  • So be it → AMEN the word said at the close of a prayer means precisely that
  • sex → IT it is the coy old word for sex, as in the 1920s It girl who had sex appeal
  • unknown → Y x, y and z are the letters algebra keeps for quantities not yet known

A shrug of agreement to a fling with a stranger.

Three scraps of shorthand stacked up, and the one to keep is the algebra: any of x, y or z can be an unknown, so the crossing letters decide which one is wanted.

21 down OVERRUN

Go on too long in swamp (7)

Definition: Go on too long · double definition

A talk that OVERRUNS goes past its allotted time, and to OVERRUN a country or a system is to swamp it with more than it can hold.

  • Go on too long → what a meeting or a speech does when it eats into the next slot
  • swamp → the verb, meaning to flood a place or overwhelm somebody with more than they can take

A meeting dragging on somewhere marshy.

'Swamp' is the trap: it reads as the noun, a patch of wet ground, and the clue wants the verb, to overwhelm by sheer numbers.

22 down GRASPS

Seizes power in shop (6)

Definition: Seizes · container

To GRASP something is to take firm hold of it, so 'Seizes' matches both the sense and the third-person ending.

  • shop → GRASS to shop somebody is to inform on them, and grass is the same British slang for doing it
  • power → P p is the standard abbreviation in physics
  • in — The piece named before it goes inside the word named after it, rather than being written alongside.

A power grab in a corner shop.

'Shop' is not the place you buy things but the verb, to inform on somebody — the same idea as grassing on them, which is why the two words swap so neatly.

25 down RUMBA

Dance in strange bar, mostly (5)

Definition: Dance · charade + deletion

The RUMBA is the Cuban ballroom dance, so 'Dance' defines it by naming the class it belongs to.

  • strange → RUM rum is an old British adjective for odd or peculiar — 'a rum do'
  • bar → BA the three-letter word with its tail taken off
  • mostly — Most of a word is all of it bar the last letter, so the tail is dropped before the piece is used.

A dance held in a decidedly odd little bar.

'Rum' for strange is Victorian slang that survives almost nowhere but crosswords, and it slides into a sentence about a bar without a sound.
