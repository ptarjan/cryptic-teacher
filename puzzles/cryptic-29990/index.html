Across

9 across NEAR THING

Zero protection for organ (not ball) – almost a disaster? (4,5)

Definition: almost a disaster · container + deletion

A near thing is an escape by the narrowest of margins, so what almost became a disaster is exactly what the phrase names.

  • Zero → NOTHING zero is nothing, and here it is wanted spelled out as a whole word
  • ball → a ball is round, so it stands for the letter O, and that O is the one taken away
  • organ → EAR the ear is the organ of hearing
  • protection for — Protecting something means closing round it, so the letters named after this sit inside the ones before.
  • not — The plain word for absence: what follows it is taken out of the letters rather than added to them.

Somebody left with no protection for an organ, the ball excepted, in an incident that nearly ended badly.

'Zero' wants to be the single letter O and is the whole word instead. A ball for that letter is worth keeping — anything round, a ring, a duck, an egg, does the same job.

10 across VAUNT

Relation following victory parade (5)

Definition: parade · charade

To vaunt something is to display it boastfully, which is the verb 'parade' as in parading one's wealth.

  • victory → V v for victory, the wartime sign made with two fingers
  • Relation → AUNT an aunt is a relation
  • following — What follows comes along behind, so the piece named first in the clue is the one placed second in the answer.

A family member walking along behind a victory procession.

'Parade' is the verb here, to show off — you parade your knowledge — and the trap is the noun the surface's procession is pushing at you.

11 across ROYALTY

Reward of author, King and his ilk? (7)

Definition: Reward of author · double definition

One sense is the money paid to a writer on each copy sold, the other the class of people a king belongs to.

  • Reward of author → the slice of every sale that goes to the writer rather than the publisher
  • King and his ilk? → kings, queens and the rest of the family, taken together as one class of people

An author being rewarded by the King and by others of his sort.

Both senses come from one root: the Crown's due on every sale of a licensed right, which is where a writer's percentage got its name.

12 across CAROUSE

English river on right of coach party (7)

Definition: party · charade

To carouse is to drink and make merry in company, so 'party' as a verb — they partied all night — swaps straight in.

  • coach → CAR a coach on a train is a carriage, which is a car
  • English river → OUSE the Ouse is an English river — there are several, in Yorkshire, Sussex and the Fens
  • on right of — In an across answer the right-hand side is the finishing end, so this puts the river after the coach and not before it.

A river in England running along the right-hand side of a coach party's route.

'Coach party' reads as one thing, a busload of day trippers, and the two words belong to opposite halves of the clue: one is material, the other the definition.

13 across POMP

Male circle backed out of fancy ball ceremony (4)

Definition: ceremony · deletion + reversal

Pomp is ceremonial display — the pomp of a coronation — so 'ceremony' names the same grand public show.

  • Male → M m for male, as on any form
  • circle → O a circle drawn on the page is the letter O
  • fancy ball → POMPOM a pom-pom is the fluffy decorative ball on a bobble hat or in a cheerleader's hand, and those two turned letters come out of it
  • backed — Backing a thing turns it to face the other way, so the two letters come out in the opposite order to the one given.
  • out of — Out of names removal: what has just been assembled is taken away from the longer word rather than joined to it.

A man backing out of an elaborate fancy-dress ball at the last minute.

The ball is not a dance at all but a pom-pom, and 'fancy' is describing that ball rather than telling you to shuffle anything.

14 across SHOPAHOLIC

One who might not, however, buy the idea they were compulsive? (10)

Definition: One who might not, however, buy the idea they were compulsive? · cryptic definition

The whole clue → SHOPAHOLIC: somebody who shops compulsively, with 'buy' meaning both a purchase and the accepting of an idea.

  • buy the idea they were compulsive → read straight, to buy an idea is to accept it, and the picture is somebody waving away a description of themselves
  • One who might not, however, buy → the other sense of buy is the one done at a counter, and 'however' carries the joke: this is the last person on earth who does not buy

Somebody in denial, refusing to accept that their own habits have got out of hand.

A shopaholic buys constantly and would be the first to deny being compulsive, so the single verb 'buy' has to be read both ways at once. There is nothing here to take apart into letters.

16 across SINUOUS

Curved air passage round centre of Slough (7)

Definition: Curved · container + middle letters

Sinuous means full of curves — a sinuous river, a sinuous dancer — so 'Curved' is the plain adjective sense of it.

  • air passage → SINUS the sinuses are the air cavities behind the nose and cheeks
  • Slough → OU the middle two of the six letters in Slough
  • round — Going round something puts that thing in the middle, so the letters named after this sit inside the ones before.
  • centre of — The centre of a word is the letters equally far from each end, and in a word of six that is the middle pair.

A curving flight path bending round the middle of the town of Slough.

Slough is a town west of London, close to Heathrow, so the surface sounds like an air corridor; the passage the setter means is the one inside your head.

17 across GIRAFFE

Blunder, caging wingless bird as a zoo attraction? (7)

Definition: a zoo attraction · container + deletion

A giraffe is one of the animals a zoo puts on show, so the definition names it by example — the question mark admits it is only one of many.

  • Blunder → GAFFE a gaffe is a blunder, usually a social one said out loud
  • bird → IR the word BIRD itself, with the B and the D at its two ends taken off
  • caging — Caging something shuts it in, so the word in front closes right round the letters named next.
  • wingless — The wings of a written word are the letters at its two ends, and stripped of them only the middle survives.

A zoo keeping a bird that cannot fly as the exhibit everybody comes to see.

'Wingless bird' sounds like an ostrich or a penguin and is not a bird at all: the setter's wings are the outer letters of a word, a trick worth keeping.

19 across EUROVISION

Songfest combining money with fantasy (10)

Definition: Songfest · charade

Eurovision is a festival of songs, dozens of countries each singing one, so 'Songfest' describes the event exactly.

  • money → EURO the euro is the currency of much of Europe
  • fantasy → VISION a vision is something imagined rather than seen
  • combining — Combining says the two things named are set end to end, with nothing done to either of them on the way.

A song contest put together out of prize money and wishful thinking.

Eurovision is the annual song contest between European countries, and the euro is the money of many of them, so the clue reaches the continent twice without ever naming it.

22 across SNUB

New York’s bottom, turning humble (4)

Definition: humble · reversal

To snub somebody is to cut them dead and take them down a peg, which is 'humble' used as a verb.

  • New York’s bottom → BUNS buns is American slang for the backside, and putting New York in front is how the setter marks the word as American
  • turning — Turning a thing round makes it read the other way, so the letters arrive in the reverse of the order they were given in.

The southern tip of New York, and something turning modest.

'Bottom' reads as the south end of the state on a map, and the sense wanted is the anatomical one; naming a US city is the usual flag for an Americanism.

24 across SECONDS

Boxer supports another course? (7)

Definition: Boxer supports · double definition

The men in a boxer's corner are his seconds, and a repeat helping at table is seconds too: one word for a stand-in and for a second go.

  • Boxer supports → the cornermen who attend a fighter between rounds, sponge and water ready
  • another course → a further helping at the table, asked for once the first plate is empty

A boxer coming out in favour of some other course of action.

Read as a sentence, 'supports' is a verb and the boxer is backing a plan; both halves are nouns, and the second is more food rather than a change of direction.

25 across MARSALA

Wine means the world to the French (7)

Definition: Wine · charade

Marsala is the fortified wine made around the Sicilian port of the same name, so 'Wine' names it by its class.

  • the world → MARS another world in the solar system, the red one next out from ours
  • to the French → ALA 'to the' in French is à la, the phrase in à la carte

A wine that matters enormously to drinkers in France.

'Means the world to' is the idiom the surface leans on, and the world wanted is a planet; the wine itself is fortified and comes from Sicily.

26 across NUDGE

Elbow’s exposed outside gown’s opening (5)

Definition: Elbow · container + first letter

To elbow somebody is to give them a small shove with the arm, which is exactly what a nudge is.

  • exposed → NUDE somebody exposed with nothing on at all is nude
  • gown’s → G the letter gown starts with
  • outside — What is outside sits round the other thing, so the word before takes in the letter that follows it.
  • opening — A word's opening is the letter it begins with, and that single letter is all this asks for.

A bare elbow poking out at the opening of a gown.

'Elbow' is the verb, what you do to somebody in a crowd, and 'exposed' is not describing that elbow at all — it is handing over letters.

27 across MONOLOGUE

Address of poor mug with one loo (9)

Definition: Address · anagram

A monologue is a long speech given by one person, and an address is exactly that: a speech delivered to an audience.

  • mug with one loo → MUGONELOO anagram fodder, nine letters
  • poor → anagram indicator
  • poor — Poor means in a bad state, and material in a bad state has not held on to the order it came in.

The address of some poor fool whose house has only one lavatory.

'Address' is a speech, not a house, which is what makes the estate-agent surface work — and this is the kind of speech one person delivers alone.

Down

1 down UNPREPOSSESSING

Plain stewed prune being like spirit? (15)

Definition: Plain · charade + anagram

Unprepossessing means making no favourable impression at first sight, so 'Plain' in the sense of drab-looking rather than simple or honest.

  • stewed prune → UNPRE the five letters of PRUNE, cooked into a different order
  • being like spirit? → POSSESSING what a spirit does to whoever it inhabits — the word for having somebody in your grip
  • stewed — Fruit left stewing goes soft and loses the shape it went into the pot with, and letters treated that way do not keep their order either.

A dish of stewed prunes so plain that it seems to have something ghostly about it.

'Plain stewed prune' reads as one phrase about the pudding, and the cut falls after the first word: only 'Plain' is the definition, the rest is material.

2 down LADY'S MAN

He fancies his chances as a version of Dylan Thomas (tho’ lacking) (5,3)

Definition: He fancies his chances · anagram + deletion

A lady's man is a man who pursues women and expects to succeed, so 'He fancies his chances' describes his standing habit rather than one occasion.

  • Dylan Thomas → DYLANTHOMAS the Welsh poet's full name, eleven letters of raw material
  • tho’ → these three letters come out of the poet's name first, leaving eight to work with
  • a version of — A version of something is the same material re-set in a different arrangement, which is what happens to these letters.
  • lacking — Lacking names what is missing, so the letters it points at never reach the answer at all.

A man rating his own romantic prospects, cast as a sort of Dylan Thomas with a bit missing.

An anagram that eats a famous name is easy to miss, because the name looks like knowledge you are being asked for; all the setter wants from the poet is his letters.

3 down STOLE

Lifted a garment (5)

Definition: Lifted · double definition

Both senses are the same five letters: the past tense of steal, and the shoulder wrap a priest or a bride wears.

  • Lifted → to lift something, in the slang sense, is to steal it, and this is that verb in the past tense
  • a garment → a long band of cloth worn across the shoulders, by a priest at the altar or a bride at a wedding

Somebody made off with a piece of clothing.

Three words that read as one statement are really two separate definitions side by side, and the join falls between the first word and the second.

4 down SISYPHUS

Offender of Zeus’s son is grimly pushy (8)

Definition: Offender of Zeus · charade + anagram

Sisyphus cheated death and gave away Zeus's secrets, and the endless boulder is his punishment for it, so he is the offender of Zeus by name.

  • son → S the family-tree abbreviation, s for son
  • is grimly pushy → ISYPHUS IS and PUSHY hand over seven letters between them, and they arrive in a new order
  • grimly — Grim means bad or unpleasant, and material described as being in a bad way has been knocked out of its proper order.

Whoever wronged the son of Zeus is described as relentlessly, joylessly aggressive.

Sisyphus is the king condemned to shove a boulder uphill for eternity, so 'grimly pushy' is a joke about him as well as an instruction about letters.

5 down EGGCUP

Say doctor’s confiscated copper breakfast container? (6)

Definition: breakfast container · charade + container

An eggcup is the small stand a boiled egg sits in at the table, so the definition names it by meal and by job.

  • Say → EG eg introduces an example, which is the job 'say' does in a sentence
  • doctor’s → GP a GP is the family doctor, the one whose surgery list you are on
  • copper → CU the chemical symbol for copper, taken from the Latin cuprum
  • confiscated — Confiscating a thing is taking it into your own keeping, so the letters named next end up held inside the ones before them.

A doctor confiscating a piece of copper that turns out to hold somebody's breakfast.

'Say' at the head of a clue usually flags a definition by example; here it is material instead, the two letters that stand for 'for instance'.

6 down OVERSHIRT

Past Hirst works that might be put on (9)

Definition: that might be put on · charade + anagram

An overshirt is a shirt cut loose enough to wear over another layer, so it is quite literally a thing you put on.

  • Past → OVER when something is past it is over
  • Hirst → SHIRT the five letters of the artist's surname, rearranged
  • works — To work a material is to knead it about until it takes a new shape, and the letters give way in the same manner.

An exhibition of Damien Hirst's earlier pieces that some gallery might yet stage.

Damien Hirst is the British artist of the pickled shark, and 'put on' in the surface means staged — the answer is something you put on yourself.

7 down MUTUAL

Misuse of umlaut is common (6)

Definition: common · anagram

Mutual means shared between two people, and 'common' carries that same sense: a common interest is a mutual one.

  • umlaut → UMLAUT anagram fodder, six letters
  • Misuse of → anagram indicator
  • Misuse of — Something misused has been put to the wrong purpose, and letters used wrongly are letters left in the wrong places.

A grumble that people are forever getting the umlaut wrong.

'Common' is the sense in Our Mutual Friend — shared by both parties — rather than the everyday senses of vulgar or frequent.

8 down STRETCHER-BEARER

One who exaggerates agent’s role in getting player off the pitch (9-6)

Definition: role in getting player off the pitch · charade

A stretcher-bearer is the person who carries an injured player off the field, so the definition names the answer by that job.

  • One who exaggerates → STRETCHER somebody who stretches a story out well past the truth
  • agent → BEARER a bearer acts for somebody by carrying: a bearer of news, or a bearer bond payable to whoever holds it

Somebody talking up how much an agent did to get a player taken off the field.

'Stretcher' for a person who stretches the truth is the setter's joke of the day, and the pitch is a football pitch rather than a sales pitch.

15 down CONVINCED

Party competed to secure East Coast state? Sure (9)

Definition: Sure · charade + container

To be convinced is to be sure of something — the settled state of mind, not the act of persuading somebody into it.

  • Party → CON Con is the standing short form of Britain's Conservative party, as printed beside a candidate's name
  • competed → VIED to vie with somebody is to compete with them
  • East Coast state? → NC North Carolina, on the American east coast, is NC in a postal address
  • to secure — Securing something is getting it safely into your keeping, so the letters named next are shut in by the ones before.

A political party fighting to lock down one east-coast state on election night.

'Sure' looks like a throwaway reply tacked on to the end of the sentence, and it is the definition; the question mark marks the state as one example out of fifty.

17 down GLOAMING

Look into playing online after sundown (8)

Definition: after sundown · container

The gloaming is the failing light after the sun has gone down, so the definition names it by the hour it occupies.

  • playing online → GAMING playing games on a console or over the internet is gaming
  • Look → LO lo is the old word for look, kept alive in 'lo and behold'
  • into — Into is the plainest insertion word there is: the letters before it go straight inside the word that follows.

Somebody settling down to game online once the sun has gone down.

Gloaming is the Scots word for dusk, the half-light between sunset and dark, kept in English mostly by the song Roamin' in the Gloamin'.

18 down FANDANGO

Cooler and relaxed? Try dance (8)

Definition: dance · charade + anagram

A fandango is a brisk Spanish dance in triple time, so 'dance' is the plain class word it belongs to.

  • Cooler → FAN a fan is the thing that cools a room
  • and → DAN the three letters of AND, loosened into another order
  • Try → GO a go at something is a try at it
  • relaxed — Something relaxed has gone slack and is no longer held in place, so the three letters beside it can settle any way round.

A calmer, more laid-back person being invited to have a go at a dance.

'Cooler' points you at a fridge or a prison cell, and the thing wanted is the one that stirs the air; the little word beside it is material, not joinery.

20 down RECEDE

Old advice about concealed earthworks entrances: ‘Get back!’ (6)

Definition: Get back · container + first letters

To recede is to move back and away — a hairline recedes, floodwater recedes — so 'Get back' is the same movement put as an order.

  • Old advice → REDE rede is the archaic word for counsel, surviving in the verb to rede, meaning to advise
  • concealed earthworks → CE the initial letter of each of those two words, taken in the order they stand
  • about — About means round here: the word in front of it wraps itself around the letters that follow.
  • entrances — An entrance is the way in, so this takes the letter each word it points at starts on.

An old piece of advice about hidden earthworks, ending in a shout at whoever is walking towards them.

'Old' is doing two jobs at once: it holds the surface sentence together and it is the setter's standard flag that an archaic word is wanted.

21 down SESAME

Picture framing mother’s climbing plant (6)

Definition: plant · container + reversal

Sesame is the plant whose seeds go on bread and are pressed for oil, so 'plant' names it by its class.

  • Picture → SEE to picture a thing is to see it in your mind's eye
  • mother’s → MAS ma is mother, and the s of the possessive comes along as a letter of its own
  • framing — A frame goes right round what it holds, so the word before it closes over the letters named next.
  • climbing — In a down clue climbing means going up the column, so those letters are read back from the bottom to the top.

A photograph with mother's creeper growing across the frame.

'Climbing plant' reads as one thing, ivy or a clematis over a wall, and the two words belong to different jobs: one is an instruction, the other the definition.

23 down KRILL

Sailor finally is into top sea food (5)

Definition: sea food · container + last letter

Krill are small shrimplike crustaceans that live in the sea and are eaten by whales, so 'sea food' names them by habitat and by diner.

  • top → KILL to top somebody, in the slang sense, is to kill them
  • Sailor → R the letter that sailor ends on
  • finally — Finally points at the end of the word beside it and takes only the letter it finishes on.
  • into — Into puts what has just been named inside the word that comes after it.

A sailor finally getting at the best seafood on the menu.

Krill are the tiny shrimp-like creatures whales strain out of the water by the ton, and 'top' as a verb meaning kill is the piece here worth remembering.
