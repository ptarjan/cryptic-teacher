1 across HALF HITCH

Knot tied in less-than-committed marriage? (4,5)

Definition: Knot · charade

A half hitch is the simple loop that fastens a rope round a post, one of the first knots anybody learns.

  • less-than-committed → HALF Anything done less than wholeheartedly is done by halves.
  • marriage → HITCH To get hitched is to get married, so the noun for the ceremony is right there.

A wedding entered into by two people who are not entirely sure about it.

Getting hitched is getting married and a hitch is also a sailor's fastening, so the clue keeps one foot at the altar and one on the boat.

6 across MEDOC

Wine tossed back during Cinco de Mayo (5)

Definition: Wine · hidden word + reversal

The Médoc is the strip of vineyards north of Bordeaux, so the answer names a wine by the district that makes it.

  • Cinco de Mayo → CODEM Five consecutive letters, starting in the middle of the first word and ending at the start of the third.
  • tossed back — Tossing a drink back is downing it in one, and in an across entry back means reading the letters from right to left.
  • during — What happens during something happens inside it, so the letters sit within the words that follow.

Knocking a drink back at a Cinco de Mayo party.

Cinco de Mayo is Mexico's yearly celebration of a battle won in 1862, and the drink the surface pours is tequila rather than anything French.

9 across LEANT

Favoured dictator’s time to give up (5)

Definition: Favoured · homophone

To lean towards a person or a plan is to favour it, and the past tense of that verb is what the definition asks for.

  • time to give up → LEANT Lent is the forty days before Easter when Christians give something up, and that word said aloud is written another way here.
  • dictator’s — A dictator speaks words for somebody else to write down, so it is the sound of the word that matters and not its spelling.

A popular tyrant deciding when to step down.

The apostrophe-s does two jobs: in the surface it makes the time belong to the dictator, and in the wordplay it turns him into the instruction to listen.

10 across CHICKPEAS

Spooner’s to opt for dairy product, hummus ingredient (9)

Definition: hummus ingredient · spoonerism

Hummus is chickpeas blended with tahini, lemon and garlic, so they are the ingredient the whole dish is built on.

  • to opt for → CHICK To opt for something is to pick it; that word gives its P away and takes CH in exchange.
  • dairy product → PEAS Cheese is the dairy product; it hands over its CH, takes the P, and the noise that comes back is spelt this way.
  • Spooner’s — The Reverend Spooner was famous for swapping the opening sounds of two words, so the two things clued here trade their fronts.

Choosing the cheese, in a sentence the Reverend has mangled.

Say 'pick cheese' and swap the noises at the front of each word. The vowels drift a little on the way, which is normal for a spoonerism and is where most solvers lose the thread.

11 across ORAL HEALTH

It requires brushing hale harlot off (4,6)

Definition: It requires brushing · anagram

Brushing your teeth is what keeps oral health up, so the definition describes the answer by the daily habit that maintains it.

  • hale harlot → HALEHARLOT anagram fodder, ten letters
  • off — Milk that has gone off has turned, and a word that has turned no longer holds its letters in the printed order.

Dismissing a perfectly healthy prostitute out of hand.

'Brushing ... off' reads as one phrase and is two separate jobs: the brushing belongs to the definition, and only the little word at the end shuffles the letters.

12 across ASHE

Legendary court figure that was seen all over Herculaneum, reportedly (4)

Definition: Legendary court figure · homophone

Arthur Ashe won Wimbledon and the US Open, and the US Open's main stadium carries his name, so the court in question is a tennis one.

  • that was seen all over Herculaneum → ASHE Vesuvius buried Herculaneum in AD 79, and the word for what fell on it gains a letter when written the way this entry needs.
  • reportedly — What is reported is passed on by mouth, so the word beside it is to be heard rather than read.

A famous figure of the law, known throughout a Roman town.

'Court' is the trap: it offers a courtroom or a royal court, and the man wanted made his name on neither.

14 across SUTURES

Repairs ruptured uterus, opening surgically (7)

Definition: Repairs · anagram + first letter

To suture a wound is to sew it shut, so 'repairs' is the mending sense of the verb as a surgeon uses it.

  • uterus → UTERUS anagram fodder, six letters
  • surgically → S the opening letter of the adverb, taken because the clue asks for nothing else of it
  • ruptured — Something ruptured has burst apart, and letters burst apart can be gathered up in a different order.
  • opening — An opening is what comes first, so only the initial letter of the word beside it is taken.

An operation to mend a torn womb.

Everything in the surface is medical, so no word looks like an instruction: 'ruptured' has to be read as the setter's word for scrambled rather than as part of the injury.

15 across DEADPAN

Without expression: ‘Improv rock band is dreadful’? (7)

Definition: Without expression · charade

A deadpan delivery keeps the face blank while the joke lands, so 'without expression' is precisely the manner it names.

  • Improv rock band → DEAD The Grateful Dead built whole concerts out of improvised jams.
  • is dreadful → PAN To pan a show is to review it savagely, which is what calling it dreadful does.

A brutal one-line verdict on a jam band.

The quotation marks point at a review, and a review saying a band is dreadful is a pan. Nothing in the surface hints at a straight face.

17 across TARTARS

Two turncoats left skinhead’s horde once (7)

Definition: horde once · charade + reversal + first letter

The Tartars were the Mongol horsemen of the Golden Horde, so a horde in the old spelling is exactly what the definition asks for.

  • Two turncoats → TARTAR A turncoat is a rat; two rats, each sent leftwards, run straight into one another.
  • skinhead’s → S The front letter of 'skin', with the rest of the word left where it stands.
  • left — In an across entry, letters sent to the left are read in the opposite direction to the way the answer is written.
  • skinhead’s — One word doing two jobs: 'head' asks for the letter at the front, and 'skin' is the word it asks it of, so the pair yield S.

A pair of traitors walking out on a skinhead's gang.

'Skinhead' looks like one word for a shaven thug and is two instructions bolted together. The horde meant is the Golden Horde that swept west in the thirteenth century.

19 across DINE OUT

Geraint’s beloved returns to South Central to visit restaurant? (4,3)

Definition: to visit restaurant? · charade + reversal + middle letters

To dine out is to eat at a restaurant instead of at home, so the definition names the outing rather than the meal.

  • Geraint’s beloved → DINE Enid is Geraint's wife in the Arthurian tale Tennyson retold, and the across entry reads her name backwards.
  • South → OUT The three letters at the heart of the word, once the S and the H at its ends are dropped.
  • returns — Somebody who returns comes back the way they went, and in an across entry that means these letters are read backwards.
  • Central — What is central sits in the middle, so the outer letters of the word before it drop away and the core is kept.

A cyclist's sweetheart heading back to South Central for a meal.

Geraint reads as the Welsh cyclist and is one of Arthur's knights, whose wife supplies the letters. 'South Central' is not a district of Los Angeles but an instruction about the word 'South'.

20 across ROAN

Dappled horse in river, did you say? (4)

Definition: Dappled horse · homophone

A roan is a horse whose coat carries white hairs evenly through a darker base, which gives the dappled effect.

  • river → ROAN The Rhône runs down through France to the Mediterranean, and its name said aloud is written differently here.
  • did you say — Asking what you said points at the sound of the word rather than at the way it is spelt.

A horse standing in a river.

'In river' paints a horse up to its knees in water. The river is French, and only the noise of its name is wanted.

22 across SOUSAPHONE

Tosspot taking up a largely synthesised instrument (10)

Definition: instrument · container + deletion

A sousaphone is the great coiled brass bass a marching band wears over the shoulder, named after the march composer John Philip Sousa.

  • Tosspot → SOUSE A souse is a drunkard in American slang, and tosspot is the older British word for the same person.
  • a → A The article is taken straight from the clue as a single letter.
  • synthesised → PHON Something synthesised is phony, and that word arrives here with its last letter left off.
  • taking up — To take something up is to take it in, so the letters that follow are swallowed by the word before them.
  • largely — Largely means most of a thing but not all of it, so the word it governs loses its final letter.

A drunk picking up an instrument that is mostly artificial.

The instrument is named after John Philip Sousa, so a drinker is hiding inside what is almost his surname. Tosspot for a soak is the piece most solvers never see.

25 across CIGARILLO

Garlic oil turned into smoke (9)

Definition: smoke · anagram

A cigarillo is a short slim cigar, and 'a smoke' is the everyday noun for anything you light and draw on.

  • Garlic oil → GARLICOIL anagram fodder, nine letters
  • turned — Oil that has turned has gone rancid, and letters gone the same way no longer sit in the order printed.

Cooking oil burning away to nothing in the pan.

'Turned into smoke' reads as one phrase about burning; the setter means turned in the sense of gone rancid, and the smoke is a thing you light rather than a vapour.

26 across TENOR

Singer, major part of Ronettes’ comeback (5)

Definition: Singer · hidden word + reversal

A tenor is the high male voice and the man who owns it, so 'Singer' names him by his range.

  • Ronettes’ → RONET The opening five letters of the 1960s girl group's name, which this across entry then reads the other way.
  • major part of — A major part is most of something and not all of it, so five of the group's eight letters are wanted.
  • comeback — A comeback is a return the way you came, and in an across entry those letters are read from right to left.

A star of a girl group making a return to the stage.

The Ronettes were the sixties group behind 'Be My Baby', and 'major' has nothing to do with music: it means the greater part of the name.

27 across LASSO

Young lady with not a single way to catch horse (5)

Definition: way to catch horse · charade

A lasso is the running loop of rope a rider throws over an animal's neck, which is exactly a way to catch a horse.

  • Young lady → LASS A lass is a girl, chiefly in Scottish and northern English use.
  • not a single → O Not a single one is none at all, and none is written as a nought.

A girl who has no way at all of catching a horse.

'Not a single' asks for a nought rather than reporting a shortage, and the definition then runs from 'way' all the way to the end of the clue.

28 across NOTORIETY

Ugly tie picked up by avowed liberal getting unwanted attention (9)

Definition: unwanted attention · anagram + container

Notoriety is fame of the wrong sort, being widely known for something bad, which is attention nobody asked for.

  • avowed liberal → NOTORY Somebody who declares themselves a liberal is no Tory, a Tory being a British Conservative, and the two words run together.
  • tie → IET The three letters of the word, shaken into a new order.
  • Ugly — Something ugly is misshapen, and letters in a misshapen order are letters waiting to be straightened out.
  • picked up by — Whatever picks a thing up takes hold of it, so these letters end up held inside the words that follow.

A badly chosen necktie acquired from a self-declared liberal.

'Ugly' looks like part of the picture and is the instruction, and the political label has to be heard as two separate words before it gives any letters up.

1 down HELLO

Conclusion of Moor’s pick-up line? (5)

Definition: pick-up line? · deletion

The word anybody opens with when striking up a conversation, so the definition names it by example rather than by synonym.

  • Moor’s → OTHELLO Shakespeare's Moor of Venice is Othello, and his name is where the letters are found.
  • Conclusion of — A conclusion is the part at the end, so what is wanted is the tail of the name and not its opening letter.

What a Moor might say to catch somebody's eye.

'Conclusion' reads as the end of the play and means the end of the name. Othello is the Moor of every crossword, and the pick-up line is the greeting you open with.

2 down LEAD ACTOR

Star of heavy metal, needing no introduction or finale: Plant (4,5)

Definition: Star · charade + deletion

The lead actor is the star of a film or a play, the one whose name goes above the title.

  • heavy metal → LEAD The densest of the common metals, and the one that weighs down a diver's belt.
  • Plant → ACTOR A plant is a factory; take the F and the Y off its two ends.
  • needing no introduction or finale — An introduction opens a word and a finale closes it, so the letters at both ends are struck off.

Robert Plant of Led Zeppelin, a frontman famous enough to need no announcing.

Every word points at rock and none of it matters: heavy metal is the element itself, and Plant is a building rather than a singer.

3 down HIT THE ROAD

To begin journey, addled Edith Wharton losing direction, first and last (3,3,4)

Definition: To begin journey · anagram + deletion

To hit the road is to set out on a journey, the idiom for the leaving rather than for the arriving.

  • Edith Wharton → EDITHWHARTON The American novelist's full name, all twelve letters, is the raw material.
  • direction → WN West and north are both compass points, and W and N are exactly the letters at the front and the back of the surname.
  • addled — An addled egg has gone bad and an addled head is muddled, so the name beside it holds its letters in no fit order.
  • losing — What is lost is gone from the mix, so the letters named after this word never reach the answer.
  • first and last — This picks out the letters standing at each end of the surname, one at its front and one at its back.

Edith Wharton, muddled and with no sense of direction, setting off somewhere.

The two letters thrown away are compass points hiding at either end of 'Wharton', which is why the name looks like fodder for a longer answer than it makes.

4 down TIC TACS

AI trade-off in methodology producing sweets (3,4)

Definition: sweets · substitution

Tic Tacs are the tiny mints that rattle in a flip-top box, so 'sweets' defines them as one brand among many.

  • methodology → TACTICS A methodology is the set of tactics somebody follows.
  • AI → These are the two letters that change places: the A moves back and the I comes forward.
  • trade-off — A trade-off is an exchange, and here the two letters named simply exchange their places.

A compromise over artificial intelligence written into a working method.

AI reads as artificial intelligence and is really two letters told to swap seats; the word for a method is otherwise left exactly as it is.

5 down HOISTED

Picked up off-site, collected by brick-and-mortar tool (7)

Definition: Picked up · anagram + container

To hoist something is to haul it upwards, so the past tense answers 'Picked up' in its lifting sense rather than its learning one.

  • brick-and-mortar tool → HOD A hod is the three-sided box on a pole a labourer carries bricks and mortar in.
  • off-site → ISTE The four letters of 'site', shaken out of their order.
  • off — Food that has gone off has turned, and letters that have turned are no longer in the order they were given.
  • collected by — Whatever collects something gathers it in, so the word after this one closes around the letters before it.

Goods taken from somewhere away from the premises and gathered up in a builder's kit.

'Brick-and-mortar' does double duty: in the surface it means a shop with an actual address, and in the wordplay it means literal bricks and mortar.

6 down MOKE

Equine owned by Jomo Kenyatta (4)

Definition: Equine · hidden word

A moke is British slang for a donkey, and a donkey is an equine — a member of the horse family without being a horse.

  • Jomo Kenyatta → MOKE Four consecutive letters, beginning in the middle of the first name and running into the second.
  • owned by — What somebody owns is held by them, and these letters are held within the name that follows.

A beast of burden kept by Kenya's founding president.

Jomo Kenyatta led Kenya to independence and became its first president; his name is here only for the letters that straddle the gap in it.

7 down DIETS

Legislatures, losing propositions (5)

Definition: Legislatures · double definition

A diet is a national assembly — Japan's parliament is still the Diet — and a slimming plan is literally a proposition for losing weight.

  • Legislatures → the assemblies that make a country's laws, and several of them carry this very name
  • losing propositions → read as regimes of eating rather than as bad bets, these are plans proposed for losing weight

Parliaments written off as a bad bet.

A losing proposition is an idea that cannot win, which is the reading the comma sets up; the second definition wants losing in the literal sense of shedding pounds.

8 down CASHEW NUT

Johnny, you and I stand on head for snack item? (6,3)

Definition: snack item? · charade + reversal

A cashew nut is the kidney-shaped nut sold roasted and salted by the bowlful, so 'snack item' names it by example.

  • Johnny → CASH Johnny Cash, the country singer in black, is known by his surname alone.
  • you and I → EW You and I together are we, and the down entry takes that pair upwards.
  • head → NUT Slang for the head, so this word supplies its own letters as well as steering the instruction before it.
  • stand on head — Anything standing on its head is upside down, and in a down entry that means those letters are read from the bottom upwards.

Johnny and the pair of us doing handstands to earn something to eat.

'Head' is doing two jobs at once: it turns 'you and I' upside down and it also hands over the last three letters. Johnny needs no surname here.

13 down SAINT PETER

Ten pirates slaughtered early Pope (5,5)

Definition: early Pope · anagram

Peter is counted as the first bishop of Rome, which makes him the earliest pope there has been.

  • Ten pirates → TENPIRATES anagram fodder, ten letters
  • slaughtered — Something slaughtered has been cut to pieces, and pieces can be laid out again in any order at all.

A crew of buccaneers put to the sword.

The saint was himself martyred in Rome, which makes the violence of the surface an uncomfortably good fit for the letters it hands over.

14 down SATIRICAL

Like Swift, awfully racialist (9)

Definition: Like Swift · anagram

Jonathan Swift wrote 'A Modest Proposal' and 'Gulliver's Travels', so writing like Swift means writing satirically.

  • racialist → RACIALIST anagram fodder, nine letters
  • awfully — Done awfully means done badly, and letters set down badly are not in their proper order.

An ugly charge of prejudice levelled at a writer.

'Like Swift' first offers a comparison with something fast, then a modern pop star, before settling on the eighteenth-century satirist whose targets often misread him exactly as the surface does.

16 down PRONOUNCE

I, for one, pursued by religious institution, say (9)

Definition: say · charade

To pronounce a word is to say it aloud, the everyday sense rather than the legal one of declaring a verdict.

  • I, for one → PRONOUN 'I' is one example of that part of speech, and 'for one' is what marks it as an example.
  • religious institution → CE CE is the Church of England, the established church there.
  • pursued by — A pursuer comes along behind, so whatever is named after this phrase is written second.

A single individual with a church coming after them.

'Say' at the end reads like the setter's throwaway 'for example' and is the definition itself. CE for church is worth owning: it turns up in almost every puzzle.

18 down STOLLEN

Laptop dropped into hot pastry (7)

Definition: pastry · container

Stollen is the German Christmas loaf, heavy with dried fruit and marzipan, which 'pastry' covers as a sweet bake.

  • hot → STOLEN Goods described as hot are goods that have been taken unlawfully.
  • Laptop → L The top of 'lap' — the word carries its own instruction in its second half.
  • Laptop — Read it as lap-top: the top of LAP is its first letter, and that single letter is all this takes.
  • dropped into — What is dropped into something lands inside it, so this letter goes within the longer word rather than beside it.

A laptop dropped into a hot pie.

'Laptop' hides its own instruction: read it as lap-top and one letter falls out of it. Hot meaning stolen is standard crossword-speak and worth remembering.

19 down DAS BOOT

Turning Down Wellington: movie with sub-plot? (3,4)

Definition: movie with sub-plot? · charade + reversal

Das Boot is Wolfgang Petersen's 1981 film set inside a U-boat, so its plot really does turn on a sub.

  • Down → DAS Feeling down is feeling sad, and those three letters are turned round.
  • Wellington → BOOT A wellington is the rubber boot named after the duke.
  • Turning — Something turning comes round to face the other way, so this piece is written in the reverse of its usual order.

Rejecting Wellington, apparently the title of a film.

The italics dress the first three words as a title, which is the joke: the real title is German, and 'sub-plot' promises a storyline when it means a submarine.

21 down ARGUS

Guardian is sugar-free (5)

Definition: Guardian · anagram

Argus was the hundred-eyed giant Hera set to keep watch in Greek myth, which is why local papers borrow the name for a watchful guardian.

  • sugar → SUGAR anagram fodder, five letters
  • free — Letters that are free are not held in place, so they may settle anywhere in the answer.

A newspaper that contains no sugar at all.

The italics point at a title, so the first word looks like this paper naming itself and is really the mythical watchman. 'Sugar-free' splits neatly into fodder and instruction.

23 down EARLY

Primitive, like a peer? (5)

Definition: Primitive · charade

An early version of anything is its first and crudest, which is what calling something primitive says of it.

  • a peer → EARL An earl is a British peer, ranking below a marquess and above a viscount.
  • like → Y The suffix -y turns a noun into 'like that thing', as in chalky or wintry.

An aristocrat dismissed as unevolved.

'Like' is not a comparison but a suffix, the letter English tacks onto a noun to make an adjective of it. The question mark is the setter admitting how light that is.

24 down OREO

Biscuit switcheroo (4)

Definition: Biscuit · anagram

An Oreo is the chocolate sandwich cookie with a white cream filling, which British English files under biscuits.

  • switcheroo → EROO anagram fodder, the four letters in the tail of a word whose front half is the instruction
  • switcheroo — One word doing both jobs: its front half is the instruction to change places, and the four letters behind it are what change places.

A sly swapping of one biscuit for another.

Two words, and the second of them does both jobs at once: its front half is the instruction and its back half is the letters to be rearranged.
