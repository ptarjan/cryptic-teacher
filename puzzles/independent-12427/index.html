Independent Cryptic Crossword No 12,427

Across

1 across CONFOUNDED

Defeated criminal paid for 2 charges (10)

Definition: Defeated · charade + container

To confound someone is to thwart them completely — 'confound their politics' in the anthem is a wish to see the other side defeated.

  • criminal → CON a con is a convict, the crossword's standard criminal
  • paid for → FUNDED to fund something is to pay for it
  • 2 → O 2 down turns out to be NOTHING, and nothing written as a figure is O
  • charges — to charge is to rush straight at something, and a piece that rushes at a word ends up in the middle of it

A bare number in a clue is a cross-reference: it stands for whatever 2 down turns out to be, so that entry has to be solved before this one can be.

6 across COLD

Fifty fish nets reserved (4)

Definition: reserved · container

Someone cold keeps their feelings back from you, which is what a reserved manner does — a temperature word doing social work.

  • fish → COD cod is the crossword's default fish
  • Fifty → L L is fifty in Roman numerals
  • nets — a net closes round whatever it catches, so the word doing the catching ends up wrapped about the other

'Fish nets' is one space away from fishnets, and that near-miss is the misdirection: here the fish does the catching rather than being caught.

9 across OUTSKIRTS

Faceless oafs backing industrial action, mostly in suburbs (9)

Definition: suburbs · charade + deletion + reversal

The outskirts are the outer edge of a town, where its suburbs sit: the same ring of housing named by position rather than by type.

  • Faceless oafs → OUTS oafs are LOUTS, and LOUTS with its face gone is OUTS
  • backing industrial action, mostly → KIRTS industrial action is a STRIKE, most of it is STRIK, and backed up that reads KIRTS
  • Faceless — a face is the front of a thing, so a word without one has lost its opening letter
  • backing — this is an across entry, and a piece backing along the row is written right to left
  • mostly — most of a word is all of it bar the final letter

The picture — anonymous thugs on a picket line — is built entirely out of instructions, which is exactly why none of them reads as one.

10 across SATYR

Lecher perched alongside westbound track (5)

Definition: Lecher · charade + reversal

A satyr is the goat-legged follower of Bacchus whose appetite for nymphs made his name a byword for a lecherous man.

  • perched → SAT a bird perched on a wire sat there
  • westbound track → YR RY is the railway of maps and timetables, running west to give YR
  • westbound — west is leftwards on a map and on a grid alike, so an across piece heading that way is written in reverse

Railway shrinks to RY on maps and in crosswords alike, and 'track' is one of the words setters use to ask for it.

11 across DAIS

Stage production of Aida short of an alto and soprano (4)

Definition: Stage · anagram + deletion

A dais is the raised platform a speaker stands on, so 'stage' means the low wooden thing rather than the theatre around it.

  • Aida short of an alto → AID alto is A, and AIDA without one of them leaves AID
  • soprano → S S is soprano on a choral score
  • production of — a production is a staging, something newly put together, so the letters are reassembled
  • short of — to be short of a thing is to be without it, so that letter never joins the pile

Voices are abbreviations in a crossword: A for alto, S for soprano, T for tenor, B for bass. All four turn up constantly.

12 across FLAG-WAVING

Jack Grealish's girl, maybe, supping wine in France, being patriotic (4-6)

Definition: being patriotic · charade + container

Flag-waving is patriotism performed rather than felt — the bunting and cheering kind, and usually said with a hint of scorn.

  • Jack → FLAG the jack is the flag a ship flies at the bow, the Union Jack being the famous one
  • Grealish's girl, maybe → WAG WAG is the tabloid label for a footballer's wife or girlfriend
  • wine in France → VIN vin is what wine is called there
  • supping — to sup is to take a drink in, and a word that takes another in holds it inside

Every name here is a decoy: the footballer is only present so that 'girl' can mean WAG, and 'Jack' is nautical rather than anybody's first name.

14 across EGGPLANT

Urge to bed emoji? (8)

Definition: emoji? · charade

The aubergine icon on a phone keyboard goes by its American name, so the question mark admits this defines a picture by what it shows.

  • Urge → EGG to egg someone on is to urge them
  • bed → PLANT to bed a seedling out is to plant it

The surface is a suggestive text message, which is precisely the reputation the aubergine icon has — Coot lets the surface do the winking.

15 across LIEGE

Tell stories, for example about sovereign (5)

Definition: sovereign · charade + reversal

A liege is the lord to whom homage is owed, so 'sovereign' names him by feudal power rather than by a crown or a coin.

  • Tell stories → LIE to tell stories is to lie
  • for example → GE for example is EG, turned about to read GE
  • about — turned about, a piece faces the other way, and with two letters that simply swaps them

EG for 'for example' is a workhorse abbreviation, and setters spin it round about as often as they use it the right way up.

17 across BLURT

Suddenly mention glimpse of Lune going into Lancaster (5)

Definition: Suddenly mention · container + first letter

To blurt is to say a thing before deciding to, so 'suddenly mention' catches both the speed and the missing intention.

  • Lancaster → BURT Burt Lancaster is the film star the surname points at
  • glimpse of Lune → L the opening letter of Lune, the river that runs through Lancaster
  • glimpse of — a glimpse is the briefest of looks, and the briefest look at a word takes in only its opening letter
  • going into — one thing going into another finishes up inside it, splitting the outer word in two

The surface is pure geography — the Lune really does run through Lancaster — and the answer needs a Hollywood leading man instead.

19 across NEWCOMER

Recent arrival discovered domes C Wren designed (8)

Definition: Recent arrival · anagram + deletion

A newcomer is someone who has only just turned up, which is what 'recent arrival' says with the timing and the noun the other way round.

  • discovered domes → OME DOMES loses the D and S covering its outside, leaving OME
  • C Wren → CWREN the architect with his initial supplies five more letters
  • discovered — read as dis-covered, stripped of its cover: the word loses the two letters wrapped round its outside
  • designed — a design is a fresh arrangement of the same material, which is what the letters are put through

Christopher Wren built St Paul's dome, so the surface is architectural history and every word of it is either fodder or an instruction.

22 across LOCAL DERBY

Boldly race criminal in encounter close to home (5,5)

Definition: encounter close to home · anagram

A local derby is a match between two clubs from the same town, so both the rivalry and the short journey are in the phrase.

  • Boldly race → BOLDLYRACE ten letters of fodder, exactly the length wanted
  • criminal — a criminal has no respect for the order things are meant to be kept in, so the letters are free to move

'Criminal' reads as an adjective describing the racer and works as the instruction — the double duty setters like best.

23 across SNUB

Insult by sending cakes back (4)

Definition: Insult · reversal

To snub someone is to cut them dead in public, an insult delivered by withholding something rather than by saying anything.

  • cakes → BUNS buns are the cakes on the tea trolley, and they travel backwards
  • sending — to send a thing is to set it travelling, and the direction it travels is what happens to the letters
  • back — this is an across entry, so going back means being read right to left along the row

Cakes are a crossword staple — buns, tarts, eccles — and for a four-letter answer the first of those is nearly always the one wanted.

26 across INERT

Disinclined to move from Gewürztraminer tasting (5)

Definition: Disinclined to move · hidden word

Something inert stays put and will not react: a chemist's inert gas does nothing at all, and a person described that way is just as immovable.

  • Gewürztraminer tasting → INERT the wanted letters run consecutively across the boundary between the two words, starting partway through the wine's name and finishing with the first letter of 'tasting'
  • from — a helping taken from something is only part of what was there, and the part wanted is a run of consecutive letters

A long foreign word is a gift to a hidden clue, because nobody reads it letter by letter — they are too busy trying to pronounce it.

27 across CREMATORS

Money's invested in those who produce furnaces (9)

Definition: furnaces · container

A cremator is the furnace itself, the oven in a crematorium, so the definition names the machines and not the people who work them.

  • those who produce → CREATORS the people who make things are creators
  • Money → M M is money in the economists' M0 and M1, and crosswords borrow it
  • invested in — to invest originally meant to clothe, so money invested in a word is wrapped up inside it

It reads like a business story right up to the last word, and 'furnaces' is the giveaway: this is machinery, not investment.

28 across HERD

Became aware of gathered mob (4)

Definition: gathered mob · homophone

A herd is cattle standing together, so 'mob' names it by the crowd it makes — the word Australians use of kangaroos as well.

  • Became aware of → HERD to become aware of something is to have heard it, and that word is a homophone of the noun for a group of animals gathered together
  • Became aware of — you become aware of a thing by ear as well as by eye, and a word met by ear can be written down more than one way

There is no 'we hear' or 'aloud' anywhere, because the fodder says it itself: becoming aware of something is hearing it, and hearing is the instruction.

29 across BRAKE PEDAL

What can stop British playboy duke entering insane plea? (5,5)

Definition: What can stop · charade + anagram + container

Only one of a car's three pedals stops it, so 'what can stop' picks the brake out from the clutch and the accelerator by its job.

  • British → B B is British on a passport and in a dictionary
  • playboy → RAKE a rake is the eighteenth century's word for a playboy
  • insane plea → PEAL the four letters of PLEA rearranged
  • duke → D D is duke in the peerage's shorthand
  • insane — a plea that is insane has come apart, and letters in that state are in no fixed order
  • entering — someone entering a room finishes up inside it, and the letter does the same to the word

The surface is a tabloid court report and none of it survives: the duke is a letter, the playboy is a synonym, and the plea is only letters.

Down

1 down CROWD

With a C but no L or E, Wordle could be CRUSH (5)

Definition: CRUSH · anagram + deletion

A crush is a dense press of bodies — the crush at the bar — so it names a crowd by the squeeze rather than by the head count.

  • Wordle → WORDLE the game's name is the letter stock to work from
  • With a C → C a C joins the pile, as a Wordle guess adds a letter
  • but no L or E → those two drop out again, which leaves exactly five letters
  • could be — 'could be' offers a possibility rather than a fact, and letters offered as one possible order will settle into another

The capitals are the game's colour-coded tiles rather than shouting, and that disguise is what stops you reading the last word as the definition.

2 down NOTHING

FA never fine time-wasting, ultimately (7)

Definition: FA · charade + last letter

FA is short for sweet Fanny Adams, the polite form of a ruder phrase, and both amount to none of anything at all.

  • never → NO 'no' is the flat refusal that 'never' amounts to
  • fine → THIN a fine thread and a thin one are the same thickness
  • time-wasting, ultimately → G the closing letter of time-wastinG
  • ultimately — what happens ultimately happens at the very end, so only the closing letter of the word beside it is wanted

The surface is all football — the FA, a fine, time-wasting by the corner flag — and not one of those senses is the one being used.

3 down OAKY

Suffering upset stomach - fine wine can be like that (4)

Definition: wine can be like that · anagram

Oaky describes a wine that has taken vanilla and spice from the barrel it matured in — a tasting note, not a complaint.

  • fine → OKAY 'fine' and 'okay' are the same shrug of approval
  • Suffering upset stomach — a stomach in that state has everything churning inside it, and letters churned about will not stay in the order they arrived

Read 'fine' as the wine's quality and the sentence makes sense; read it as 'okay' and it becomes four letters to shake up. That double duty is the trick.

4 down NURSLING

Ascending ladder, support youngster (8)

Definition: youngster · charade + reversal

A nursling is an infant still at the breast, so 'youngster' names it by age — the youngest thing the word can be used of.

  • Ascending ladder → NUR a ladder in a stocking is a run, and climbing the grid it reads NUR
  • support → SLING a sling is what supports a broken arm
  • Ascending — this is a down entry, so a piece that climbs the grid arrives with its letters in the opposite order

'Ladder' is the tights kind, not the climbing kind, which is why 'ascending' can sit right beside it and still read as one picture.

5 down ENSIGN

US officer training is never over houses (6)

Definition: US officer · hidden word + reversal

An ensign is the lowest commissioned rank in the US Navy, the American opposite number to a Royal Navy sub-lieutenant.

  • training is never → ENSIGN trai(ngisne)ver holds six consecutive letters, and they read the other way round
  • over — a thing turned over ends up facing the other way, so the run of letters has to be read from its far end back
  • houses — to house something is to have it living inside you, and here one phrase has the answer lodged in it

'Training is never over' is a sergeant's motto and a set of instructions at the same time, which is why nothing in it looks like wordplay.

7 down OUTSIZE

Huge public expressions of disappointment in speech (7)

Definition: Huge · charade + homophone

Outsize clothing is cut beyond the standard range, so the word means larger than what normally fits — huge by the shop's measure.

  • public → OUT news that is out is public knowledge
  • expressions of disappointment → SIZE a sigh is the noise disappointment makes, and SIGHS spoken comes out on paper as SIZE
  • in speech — said rather than written, so what is wanted is a word that sounds identical and is spelt differently

Two registers glued together: 'out' from the newsroom and sighs from the sofa. The clue reads as one long grumble and is really a charade.

8 down DE RIGUEUR

Coot, gripped by rude urge, not behaving in line with convention (2,7)

Definition: in line with convention · anagram

What is de rigueur is demanded by etiquette rather than by law — French for 'of strictness', borrowed for social obligations.

  • Coot → I Coot is the setter of this puzzle, and a setter's word for himself is I
  • rude urge → RUDEURGE eight letters of fodder for the shake-up
  • gripped by — to be gripped is to be held fast inside something, which is where the setter's own letter finishes up
  • not behaving — letters that will not behave are letters that refuse to stay in the order they were given

Setters drop themselves into their own clues constantly, so a byline turning up mid-sentence is nearly always doing duty as the single letter I.

10 down SEAN LOCK

Comedian who's missed bend cycling cracks chin? (4,4)

Definition: Comedian who's missed · container + cycling

Sean Lock died in 2021 and is still mourned by British comedy audiences, so 'missed' is what narrows the definition to one man.

  • chin → SOCK to chin someone and to sock them are the same punch
  • bend → LEAN to lean is to bend out of the upright, and this one's L pedals round to the back
  • cycling — the letters pedal round in a circle, the one at the front dropping to the back with the rest keeping their order
  • cracks — a crack is a split, and a word split open has a gap in it for something else to sit in

The surface is a cyclist coming off at a corner; the wordplay is a punch with a lean tucked inside it. Both halves of the answer are ordinary words.

13 down PLATELET

Component of blood pressure behind false start in court (8)

Definition: Component of blood · charade

A platelet is one of the cell fragments in blood that clump together to seal a wound, so the definition is plain biology.

  • pressure → P P is pressure in physics and on a weather chart
  • behind → LATE a train running behind is running late
  • false start in court → LET a serve that clips the net is a let, and it has to be taken again

Nothing in the surface is medical and nothing in the answer is legal: 'court' is the tennis kind, and that swap is what the clue turns on.

14 down EMBELLISH

Telephone call in space - somewhat elaborate (9)

Definition: elaborate · charade

To embellish a story is to add decorative detail it never had, which is one of the things elaborate means when it is a verb.

  • space → EM an em is a printer's space, as wide as the type is tall
  • Telephone call → BELL giving someone a bell is giving them a ring
  • somewhat → ISH -ish on the end of a word is how English says somewhat

'Elaborate' has to be heard as a verb rather than an adjective, and that switch is the hard step, because the surface offers the adjective first.

16 down LEG BREAK

Batsman could face this serious injury (3,5)

Definition: Batsman could face this · double definition

One reading is cricket, the ball that spins away from the bat; the other is the hospital kind, a snapped tibia.

  • Batsman could face this → LEG BREAK this cricket delivery, bowled by a spinner, turns away from a right-handed batsman
  • serious injury → LEG BREAK the same two words read as plain English: a broken leg

Both halves smell of cricket, which is the trap — 'face' belongs to the batsman and 'serious injury' belongs to the hospital, not the game.

18 down UNCLEAR

Obscure type of weapon Greek character brought round (7)

Definition: Obscure · charade + reversal

An obscure passage is one you cannot see through, and unclear says the same of writing or speech in a plainer register.

  • Greek character brought round → UN NU is the Greek letter, and brought round it reads UN
  • type of weapon → CLEAR the weapon is NUCLEAR, and what is left once its NU is taken off the front is CLEAR
  • brought round — brought round means turned to face the other way, so that pair of letters swaps ends

Setters love the Greek alphabet for its short letters — NU, PI, MU, ETA — and here one of them is already sitting at the front of a longer word.

20 down MANHOOD

Lunchbox cover located by staff (7)

Definition: Lunchbox · charade

Lunchbox is tabloid slang for the bulge in a sportsman's shorts, and manhood is the coy newspaper word for the same thing.

  • staff → MAN to staff a desk is to man it
  • cover → HOOD a hood covers an engine or a head
  • located by — to be located by something is to sit right beside it, which is how these two halves join up

Definition and answer are both newspaper euphemisms, which is how the clue gets away with being this rude while reading like an office memo.

21 down MERCER

Man of the cloth's endless compassion over late queen (6)

Definition: Man of the cloth · charade + deletion

A mercer is a dealer in fine fabric, so the definition is meant with a straight face: the trade, not the clergy.

  • endless compassion → MERC MERCY with no end to it is MERC
  • late queen → ER ER, Elizabeth Regina, is the queen crosswords mean
  • endless — a word with no end to it has given up its final letter
  • over — in a down entry whatever is over sits above, so that piece is written first

'Man of the cloth' means a vicar almost everywhere else in English, and here it means a draper.

24 down BASAL

In bottom position? That makes Bert alert (5)

Definition: In bottom position? · charade + substitution

Basal means at or forming the base of a thing — a basal leaf grows from the bottom of the stem — so it is the botanist's word for lowest.

  • Bert → B the opening letter of BERT is the one being replaced
  • That makes → AS the swap is written out as an equation, and AS is its hinge
  • alert → AL AL takes the place of that opening B, and BERT becomes ALERT
  • makes — 'makes' is the verb of transformation: one thing is turned into another, and the answer spells out the recipe for doing it

Say the answer as three words rather than one and it becomes the very instruction that produced it, which is what the question mark is apologising for.

25 down CAMP

American leaves home for university party (4)

Definition: party · deletion

A party in the sense of a faction — the Remain camp, the Corbyn camp — is a camp, so this is the political sense, not the birthday one.

  • home for university → CAMPUS a campus is the grounds a university calls home
  • American → US US is short for American, and it is the pair that walks out
  • leaves — someone who leaves is no longer there, so those letters walk out of the word they were in

'Party' is the trap: nothing here is a celebration, and the word wanted is the one for a side in an argument.
