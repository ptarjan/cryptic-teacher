Across

8 across MOLLYCODDLE

Dog-sit for Spooner and give too many treats? (11)

Definition: give too many treats · spoonerism

To mollycoddle is to indulge someone with far more care and comfort than they need, which is what handing out endless treats amounts to.

  • Dog → COLLIE a collie is a working dog
  • sit for → MODEL to sit for a painter is to model
  • Spooner → MOLLYCODDLE trade the opening sounds of those two words and say the result aloud
  • Spooner — the Reverend Spooner was famous for swapping the opening sounds of two neighbouring words as he spoke

A Spooner clue defines the answer straight and then describes it with the initial sounds swapped, so the work is done backwards: find the swapped phrase in the clue, then untangle it.

10 across HUE

Colour of uniform that man adopts (3)

Definition: Colour · container

Hue is the property that makes one colour differ from another, so it stands as a plain synonym for colour itself.

  • that man → HE the pronoun a crossword uses for an unnamed male
  • uniform → U U is uniform in the radio alphabet
  • adopts — to adopt is to take somebody into the family, so one piece takes the other inside itself

"Colour of uniform" reads as one phrase and invites you to think of khaki or navy; the uniform is a letter, not a garment.

11 across ORDER

Other ranks visibly embarrassed about group of knights (5)

Definition: group of knights · charade + reversal

An order is a fellowship bound by a shared rule; the Garter and the Bath are orders of knights, so this is definition by category.

  • Other ranks → OR army shorthand for soldiers below commissioned rank
  • visibly embarrassed → DER someone visibly embarrassed goes red, and the colour is turned round
  • about — to come about is to turn right round, the way a boat does, so the piece it touches is written the other way

Other ranks are the soldiers of a British army unit who hold no commission, and OR is the abbreviation the papers lean on constantly.

12 across HARDLINER

One often inflexible doctor reversing course to plug new inhaler (9)

Definition: One often inflexible · anagram + reversal + container

A hardliner holds to a strict position and will not compromise, so the definition names the person by that rigidity rather than by any policy.

  • inhaler → HALINER all seven letters of the word, reused in a fresh order
  • doctor → RD DR is the title before a doctor's name, here written the other way round
  • reversing course — to reverse course is to turn back the way you came, so the letters run in the opposite direction
  • to plug — plugging a gap means filling it from the inside, so this piece drops into the middle of the other
  • new — made afresh is what new means, and letters made afresh do not keep the arrangement they arrived in

"Doctor" is one of the commonest anagram signals in the business, so it reads as the instruction; here it is the abbreviation, and the small word "new" is doing the shuffling.

13 across NEAR THING

Electrician finally wiring to ground - disaster narrowly avoided (4,5)

Definition: disaster narrowly avoided · last letter + charade

A near thing is an escape by the smallest of margins, and the sense belongs to the whole phrase rather than to either word alone.

  • Electrician → N the letter the word ends on
  • wiring to ground → EARTHING connecting a circuit to the earth for safety
  • finally — the word marks the last thing in a sequence, so it takes the closing letter of the word before it

Earthing is the British term for what an electrician elsewhere calls grounding, which is why the trade surface reads so smoothly and gives nothing away.

16 across OVERT

Manifest completed before time (5)

Definition: Manifest · charade

Overt means done openly and in plain sight, and manifest in its adjective sense means obvious to anybody looking — the same idea in a different register.

  • completed → OVER when something is over it is completed
  • time → T T is the standard abbreviation for time
  • before — the word places one piece ahead of the other, so what follows it is written second

"Manifest" reads first as the ship's cargo list, a noun; it is the adjective here, meaning plain to see, and the definition has to match that.

17 across SPEED UP

Increasingly rush through half-forgotten speeches at party (5,2)

Definition: Increasingly rush · deletion + charade

To speed up is to go progressively faster, so the setter spells the sense out in two words instead of offering a single synonym.

  • speeches → SPEE the front half of the eight-letter word
  • party → DUP the DUP is the Democratic Unionist Party of Northern Ireland
  • half-forgotten — half the word has slipped the mind, so only the opening half of its letters is still on the page

British crosswords treat the party initials — DUP, SNP, SDP — as ordinary vocabulary, so "party" is very often three letters rather than a celebration.

19 across BOOTLEG

Merseyside town, origin of grisly pirate (7)

Definition: pirate · charade + first letter

To bootleg is to make and sell copies illegally, and to pirate is the verb for the same trade — both are about unlicensed copying.

  • Merseyside town → BOOTLE Bootle sits on the Mersey just north of Liverpool
  • origin of grisly → G the first letter of that word
  • origin of — an origin is where a thing starts, so this points at the opening letter of the word after it

The surface promises a grisly pirate from the north-west coast, which is a picture, not a parse; the definition is the last word on its own and it is a verb.

21 across AMISS

Wrong answer written by teacher (5)

Definition: Wrong · charade

Amiss means out of order or not as it should be — a synonym for wrong that turns up mostly after a verb, as in "something is amiss".

  • answer → A A is the standard abbreviation for answer
  • teacher → MISS British schoolchildren address a woman teacher as Miss

"Wrong answer" is a phrase the eye takes in one gulp, and that is the trap: only the first word is the definition, and the second is fodder.

22 across SMELL A RAT

Become suspicious of cryptic master holding everything back (5,1,3)

Definition: Become suspicious · anagram + reversal + container

To smell a rat is to sense that something dishonest is going on — a whole-phrase idiom resting on the rat as a byword for treachery.

  • master → SMERAT the six letters of the word in a fresh order
  • everything → LLA everything is ALL, written the other way
  • cryptic — something cryptic is hard to read straight off, and letters out of their usual order are exactly that
  • holding — to hold a thing is to keep it enclosed, so one piece wraps round the other
  • back — in an across entry back means right to left, so the piece runs in the opposite direction

"Cryptic master" reads as a compliment to a setter, which is the setter enjoying himself: the first word is the instruction and the second is the material it works on.

24 across CARTRIDGE

Round of game after pawn's lost by Charlie (9)

Definition: Round · charade + deletion

A cartridge is one complete round of ammunition — case, propellant and bullet in a single unit, which is what shooters call a round.

  • Charlie → C Charlie is C in the radio alphabet
  • game → PARTRIDGE a partridge is a game bird, shot in season
  • pawn → P P is the chess symbol for a pawn, and it is the letter that goes
  • lost — what is lost has gone missing, so the letter named beside it drops out of the longer word

"Round of game" sounds like a hand of cards or a shooting party, which is the surface doing its work; game is a bird, and round is the definition.

26 across BEFOG

Lower visibility of marshland traps leader of expeditionary force (5)

Definition: Lower visibility of · container + first letter + charade

To befog something is to cover it in fog, and fog is precisely what lowers how far anybody can see.

  • marshland → BOG a bog is wet, spongy marshland
  • leader of expeditionary → E the opening letter of that word
  • force → F F is the standard abbreviation for force
  • traps — to trap a thing is to hold it captive inside, which is what one piece does to the others here
  • leader of — the leader heads the line, so this hands over just the first letter of the word it introduces

The be- prefix turns a noun into a verb meaning cause to be covered in, the same shape as becalm and bedevil — worth knowing, because setters build words this way often.

28 across SOT

Drunkard takes Coke product half-heartedly (3)

Definition: Drunkard · deletion

A sot is a habitual drunkard, an old word that survives now mainly in crosswords and in older writing.

  • Coke product → SOOT coke is a coal-based fuel, and burning it leaves this behind
  • half-heartedly — the heart of the word is a doubled letter, and doing it by halves keeps only one of the pair

Coke next to a drunkard points straight at the drink, and it is the fuel: coal baked until the gas is driven off, which burns dirty.

29 across BANGLADESHI

Commonwealth citizen accommodated in urban glade's high-rise (11)

Definition: Commonwealth citizen · hidden word

Bangladesh is a member of the Commonwealth, so a Bangladeshi is one instance of the class the definition names.

  • urban glade's high-rise → BANGLADESHI eleven letters run straight on across the three words, apostrophe and hyphen ignored
  • accommodated in — to be accommodated is to be housed inside, so the letters are lodged consecutively within the words that follow

Eleven letters is a long span to hide, so a setter has to build a plausible sentence around it; the tower-block picture exists only to carry the letters.

Down

1 down IMPOUNDS

Seizes one million quid (8)

Definition: Seizes · charade

To impound is to take goods into official custody, so it is seizing carried out under legal authority rather than by force.

  • one → I the single stroke I is how a crossword writes the number one
  • million → M M is the standard abbreviation for a million
  • quid → POUNDS quid is British slang for pounds sterling, singular and plural alike

The surface is three money words in a row, which is exactly the wrong picture: the answer is what officialdom does to your property, not a sum you might have in your pocket.

2 down PLOD

Policeman's tips for potential overdose (4)

Definition: Policeman · outer letters + charade

PLOD is British slang for a police officer, taken from PC Plod in the Noddy children's books, and like the definition it names one man.

  • potential → PL the two ends of the word, first and last
  • overdose → OD OD is the everyday shorthand for an overdose
  • tips — tips are the extreme ends of a thing, so the word asks for the outermost letters and nothing between them

The surface offers a constable's tip-offs about a possible overdose, which reads as one continuous thought and hides the join between the two pieces.

3 down GYM RAT

Athletic person climbing in tight army gear (3,3)

Definition: Athletic person · hidden word + reversal

A gym rat is someone who all but lives at the gym, so the definition names the person by the habit rather than by build or sport.

  • tight army gear → TARMYG six letters run consecutively across the end of one word, all of the next and the start of the third
  • climbing — a down entry read by climbing runs from the bottom of the grid upwards, so the letters arrive in the opposite order
  • in — 'in' says the letters are already sitting inside the words that follow rather than being defined by them

"Climbing in" reads as one phrase about squeezing into tight kit, and that is its job on the surface; solving it means hearing two separate instructions in those two words.

4 down YOGH

Chaucerian character in Canterbury who sang Hallelujah, ultimately (4)

Definition: Chaucerian character · last letters

A yogh is a letter of the Middle English alphabet Chaucer wrote in, so "character" here means a written symbol, not somebody in a story.

  • Canterbury who sang Hallelujah → YOGH the four words in order, each contributing its final letter
  • ultimately — at the very end is what the word means, so each of the four words gives up only its closing letter

Canterbury and Chaucer push you towards pilgrims, so you look for a person; the letter wanted is a written symbol instead, which is the other sense of character.

5 down WELL-TO-DO

Flush is source of water agitation (4-2-2)

Definition: Flush · charade

Both words mean having money to spare: flush is the informal "plenty of cash on hand", and the answer is its settled, respectable version.

  • source of water → WELL a shaft sunk to reach water underground
  • agitation → TODO a to-do is a fuss or commotion

Sitting next to a source of water, "Flush" wants to be plumbing; it is the money sense, meaning comfortably supplied with cash.

6 down CHANCELLOR

Risk regressive revolution as German leader (10)

Definition: German leader · charade + reversal

Germany's head of government holds the title Chancellor, so the definition points at an office by naming the country that uses it.

  • Risk → CHANCE to take a chance is to take a risk
  • revolution → LLOR a revolution is one full roll, turned round here
  • regressive — something regressive moves backwards, so the word it describes is written in the opposite direction

"Revolution" is doing double duty on the surface, where it belongs with German history, while the wordplay only wants its plain physical sense of a single turn.

7 down LEARNT

Studied Shakespearean role at National Theatre (6)

Definition: Studied · charade

To have learnt a subject is to have studied it until you know it; both are past tenses, and this is the British spelling of the word.

  • Shakespearean role → LEAR the title part of one of the tragedies, a king who divides his kingdom
  • National Theatre → NT NT is how the London company signs itself

"Role" rather than "character" is the tell: the clue wants the name of a part an actor is cast in, and that name then needs a house to be played at.

9 down DIRIGIBLE

Airship left large current, free to go north then east (9)

Definition: Airship · charade + reversal

A dirigible is a powered, steerable airship — the word itself means steerable, which is what separates it from a balloon that drifts.

  • left → L L is the standard abbreviation for left
  • large → BIG a plain synonym, not the clothing-size L
  • current → I I is the symbol for electric current in a circuit
  • free → RID to be rid of something is to be free of it
  • east → E E is the compass abbreviation for east
  • to go north — north is up the page, so a down entry sent that way is read from the bottom back to the top

"Left" and "large" both look like the one-letter abbreviations L; only one of them is, and the count of squares is what settles which.

14 down AMELIORATE

E-mail edited to speechify better (10)

Definition: better · anagram + charade

To ameliorate is to make something better, and "better" is being used here as a verb — to improve — rather than as a comparison.

  • E-mail → AMELI five letters rearranged, the hyphen ignored
  • speechify → ORATE to orate is to make a speech, usually a grand one
  • edited — editing a text means moving its parts about, so the letters come out in a different arrangement

"Better" looks like a comparison sitting at the end of a sentence about e-mail; it is a verb, and verbs are what definitions of this answer have to be.

15 down IMPOSED ON

Put out sea-god's declaration exiling setter (7,2)

Definition: Put out · deletion

To impose on somebody is to put them out, to inconvenience them by asking too much; a two-word phrasal verb answering a two-word phrasal definition.

  • sea-god's declaration → IMPOSEIDON a sea god announcing who he is says I'M POSEIDON
  • setter → I the person who wrote the clue calls himself I
  • exiling — to exile somebody is to send them away, so the letter named beside it is banished from the phrase

The whole spoken sentence is the fodder, which is unusual and easy to miss — you are meant to hear a god introduce himself, then take one letter out of what he said.

18 down DESCRIBE

Give an account of Chester river, including top of stony bed (8)

Definition: Give an account of · container + first letter + charade

To describe something is to set out in words what it is like, which is exactly what giving an account of it means.

  • Chester river → DEE the Dee runs through Chester in north-west England
  • top of stony → S the opening letter of that word
  • bed → CRIB a crib is a baby's bed
  • including — to include something is to take it in, so the outer word closes around what comes next
  • top of — the top of a word is where it starts, which in a down entry is also its highest letter

"River bed" is sitting in the clue waiting to be read as one thing, and it never is: the river and the bed are two separate pieces with a letter wedged between them.

20 down GET A GRIP

Calm down, perhaps uplifted by signature expression of condolences (3,1,4)

Definition: Calm down · charade + reversal

Get a grip is the idiom for taking hold of yourself, used exactly where you would tell somebody to stop panicking.

  • perhaps → GE perhaps signals an example, EG, and here it stands on its head
  • signature → TAG a tag is the signature a graffiti writer sprays
  • expression of condolences → RIP RIP is carved on gravestones, short for rest in peace
  • uplifted — something uplifted has been raised, and raising a piece in a down entry makes it read the other way

Three separate scraps of shorthand in a row, and the surface — a signed sympathy card — is smooth enough that none of them looks like an abbreviation.

21 down ACCESS

Admission in court's beginning to crack criminal cases (6)

Definition: Admission · anagram + container + first letter

Access is the right or the means of getting in, and admission is the same idea named from the other side of the door.

  • cases → ACESS the five letters of the word in a new order
  • court's beginning → C the letter that word opens with
  • beginning — a beginning is the first thing there is, so only the opening letter of the word is taken
  • to crack — cracking a safe means getting inside it, so this letter forces its way into the middle of the others
  • criminal — a criminal acts outside the rules, and letters that break the rules abandon the order they were given in

"Criminal cases" is a real legal phrase and reads as one unit, which is what hides the fact that the adjective is an instruction and the noun is raw material.

23 down LIBIDO

Swimming pool inspires non-heterosexual desire (6)

Definition: desire · container

Libido is the psychoanalytic name for sexual drive, so desire is the everyday word for the same appetite.

  • Swimming pool → LIDO a lido is an open-air public swimming pool
  • non-heterosexual → BI bi is the short form of bisexual
  • inspires — to inspire is literally to breathe in, so the longer word draws the shorter one inside itself

Lido is a British word you meet far more often in crosswords than at the water's edge, and setters use it whenever they need a four-letter pool.

25 down ELLE

Beautiful women not covered in fashion magazine (4)

Definition: fashion magazine · deletion

Elle is a long-running international fashion glossy, so the definition names the answer by saying what sort of thing the title is.

  • Beautiful women → BELLES belles are beautiful women, from the French for beautiful
  • not covered — a cover sits on the outside, so with the covers gone the first and last letters are stripped away

The whole clue reads as a caption about magazine covers, and the word "covered" is doing two jobs at once: picture on the surface, instruction underneath.

27 down FAST

Rapid foxtrot by topless performers (4)

Definition: Rapid · charade + deletion

Fast and rapid are plain synonyms for moving at speed — not the other sense of fast, which means firmly fixed in place.

  • foxtrot → F Foxtrot is F in the radio alphabet
  • performers → CAST the cast is the company of performers in a show
  • topless — topless means missing whatever sits on top, and in a down entry the top is the opening letter

A dance and a troupe of dancers make a tidy picture, and both halves of it are really shorthand: one letter from the radio alphabet, one word with its head removed.
