4 across MIKADO

Fuss follows Kipling novel about Japanese emperor (6)

Definition: Japanese emperor · reversal + charade

Mikado is the old ceremonial title of Japan's emperor, so the definition names the office the answer is the word for.

  • Kipling novel about → MIK Kim is Kipling's novel of a boy spying on the North-West Frontier, entered backwards
  • Fuss → ADO much ado about nothing — an ado is a fuss
  • follows — One piece that follows another is written after it, so the fuss comes second in the build rather than first.
  • about — Turned about, a title faces the other way and its letters are entered from the end back to the beginning.

A row breaking out over a Kipling novel that features Japan's emperor.

Gilbert and Sullivan made the word familiar as an opera title, but mikado is a real style for the emperor of Japan. 'About' belongs to the machinery, not to the picture.

6 across VARIABLE

Eg x, y or z having a range of possible values (8)

Definition: Eg x, y or z · double definition

One sense is the algebraic symbol standing for an unfixed quantity; the other is the adjective for anything able to take different values.

  • Eg x, y or z → VARIABLE the letters algebra reaches for when a quantity has not been fixed yet
  • having a range of possible values → VARIABLE a quantity free to take any value across a range, rather than one settled number

A maths lesson explaining what the letters in an equation stand for.

Both halves are mathematical, which is what makes the seam so hard to find: the first names three examples, the second describes the class those examples belong to.

9 across SARNIE

Snack wherein raspberries somewhat making a comeback (6)

Definition: Snack · hidden word + reversal

A sarnie is British slang for a sandwich, which is the snack meant — the same everyday thing in a chattier register.

  • wherein raspberries → EINRAS six letters standing in a row across the join between the two words, pointing the wrong way
  • somewhat — Only a part of what has just been said is meant, and the part wanted is an unbroken run of letters inside those words.
  • making a comeback — Something making a comeback returns the way it went, so in an across entry the run has to be read from right to left.

Raspberries coming back into fashion as something to nibble on.

Nothing has been rearranged here: the letters are already sitting in the clue, facing backwards. A sarnie is what much of Britain informally calls a sandwich.

10 across ROUNDS ON

Angrily attacks plump child (6,2)

Definition: Angrily attacks · charade

To round on somebody is to turn and attack them verbally, so 'Angrily attacks' matches both the aggression and the third-person form.

  • plump → ROUND a round figure is a plump one
  • child → SON a son is somebody's male child

A tirade aimed at a chubby youngster.

To round on somebody is to turn and lay into them with words rather than fists, which is why the definition says angrily rather than violently.

11 across ASSUAGEMENT

Awful magnate uses relief (11)

Definition: relief · anagram

Assuagement is the easing of pain, grief or hunger, which is what relief names — two abstract nouns for the same softening.

  • magnate uses → MAGNATEUSES eleven letters of fodder, exactly the length the enumeration asks for
  • Awful — Something awful has gone badly wrong, and letters that have gone wrong are no longer in the order they were handed to you.

A dreadful tycoon helping himself to a relief fund.

'Uses' is where the mechanism hides: it reads as the verb of the sentence about the magnate, and it is really four more letters of fodder.

15 across COWSLIP

Outdo Kew Gardens, ultimately plugging trim variety of primula (7)

Definition: variety of primula · last letters + container

The cowslip is a wild Primula of meadows and banks, so 'variety of primula' names the genus and asks for one species of it.

  • trim → CLIP to clip a hedge is to trim it
  • Outdo Kew Gardens, ultimately → OWS the closing letters of those three words, taken in the order they stand
  • ultimately — What comes ultimately comes at the very end, so the closing letter of each word named is what gets taken.
  • plugging — A plug goes into a hole, so those letters are pushed inside the other piece rather than added on beside it.

A flower neatly grown to outshine even Kew Gardens.

'Variety of' looks like an instruction to shuffle and is part of the definition instead — a botanical variety, not a mixed-up one. Kew is London's national botanic garden.

17 across IN STORE

Imminent selection of trains to Retford (2,5)

Definition: Imminent · hidden word

Something in store for you is waiting to happen, so 'Imminent' catches the sense of an event already on its way.

  • trains to Retford → INSTORE seven letters in a row, running across the ends of these three words
  • selection of — A selection of something is a part taken out of it, and the part wanted here is a run of letters standing together.

A departure board listing the next services to Retford.

Retford is a small Nottinghamshire town, in the clue only because its opening letters complete the run. The railway picture has nothing to do with the definition.

18 across CUT AND PASTE

Updates can’t corrupt word-processing technique (3,3,5)

Definition: word-processing technique · anagram

Cut and paste is the standard editing move in a word processor: lift the text out and drop it in somewhere else.

  • Updates can’t → UPDATESCANT eleven letters of fodder once the apostrophe is set aside
  • corrupt — A corrupted file has its contents scrambled, and the same thing happens to the letters standing next to the word.

Software updates that stubbornly refuse to install.

'Corrupt' does double duty in the picture — corrupted updates are exactly what the surface describes, which is why it never reads as an instruction.

22 across ORATORIO

Composition of other ranks taken on by Nelson without introduction (8)

Definition: Composition · container + deletion

An oratorio is a large sacred work for choir, soloists and orchestra, so 'Composition' names it by its broad musical category.

  • Nelson without introduction → ORATIO the admiral's first name was Horatio, docked here of its opening letter
  • other ranks → OR OR is the British army's own term for soldiers below commissioned rank
  • taken on by — To take somebody on is to take them in, so the smaller piece is admitted inside the larger rather than tacked onto it.
  • without introduction — An introduction is the opening of a thing, and a name deprived of one loses the letter it begins with.

Nelson taking on a company of ordinary soldiers without so much as an introduction.

Nelson is here for his first name rather than his fame, which is why the surface can talk about the navy while the wordplay quietly spells a Georgian Christian name.

23 across VIENNA

Compete with sides from Nigeria outside northern capital (6)

Definition: capital · charade + outer letters + container

Vienna is the capital of Austria, so the bare word 'capital' asks for one particular city and the wordplay pins down which.

  • Compete → VIE to vie with somebody is to compete with them
  • sides from Nigeria → NA the first and last letters of the country, its middle discarded
  • northern → N n = northern, as on a compass rose or a postcode
  • sides from — The sides of a word are the letters at its two edges, so everything between them is thrown away.
  • outside — One piece standing outside another has that other within it, so the single letter is enclosed by the pair just taken.

Teams from Nigeria playing a match just outside a capital city in the north.

'Sides' is the football sense in the picture and the edges-of-a-word sense in the wordplay, and 'northern' belongs to the letters rather than to the capital.

24 across APPETITE

Pop round, given small desire for food (8)

Definition: desire for food · reversal + charade

An appetite is the hunger you feel before eating, so 'desire for food' is the plain definition of it, noun for noun.

  • Pop round → AP pop is a father, and the two letters naming him are turned back to front
  • small → PETITE petite, borrowed from French, describes a small and neat build
  • round — Turned round, a short word is written from its end back to its start, which is all the instruction amounts to.

Being asked to pop round, since you have a small hunger to satisfy.

'Pop round' reads as an invitation to call in, and neither word means what the surface wants — one of them is a father, the other an instruction.

25 across REVERE

Venerate American patriot Paul (6)

Definition: Venerate · double definition

One sense is the verb for holding something in awe; the other is Paul Revere, whose surname happens to be spelled the same way.

  • Venerate → REVERE to hold in deep respect, the verb used of saints and heroes
  • American patriot Paul → REVERE the Boston silversmith who rode through the night in 1775 to warn that the British were coming

Honouring one of the heroes of the American Revolution.

The clue reads as a single instruction to honour a named man, and the seam falls after the first word: one half is the verb, the other is his surname.

Down

1 down EDWINA

Terrible wind interrupts middle of meal. Curry perhaps? (6)

Definition: Curry perhaps · anagram + container + middle letters

'Curry perhaps?' points at Edwina Currie, a British MP of the 1980s: the answer is the forename that goes with that near-identical surname.

  • middle of meal → EA MEAL loses the letter at each end, and the two left in the centre open up to take the other piece
  • wind → DWIN the four letters of WIND, re-laid in a fresh order
  • Terrible — A terrible version of something is a botched one, and letters botched like that do not stay in the order the clue lists them.
  • interrupts — To interrupt is to cut in partway through, so the disturbed letters land inside the other piece rather than beside it.
  • middle of — Only the heart of the word is wanted: the letters at each end are dropped and the two in the centre stay.

A gale blowing through the middle of somebody's dinner, with a curry proposed instead.

'Curry perhaps?' is not food at all — it is a surname, and the answer is the forename that belongs with it. Edwina Currie was a Conservative minister in the Thatcher years.

2 down HANOVERIAN

The morning after ejecting good Hislop, say, from a royal house (10)

Definition: from a royal house · deletion + charade

A Hanoverian is somebody of the House of Hanover, so 'from a royal house' describes exactly what the answer says about a person.

  • The morning after → HANGOVER the morning after a heavy night is the hangover it leaves you with
  • good → G g = good, as on a school report, and it is the letter thrown out of the word above
  • Hislop, say → IAN Ian Hislop edits Private Eye and has captained a team on Have I Got News for You since 1990
  • ejecting — To eject something is to throw it out of where it was sitting, so the letter named leaves the word beside it.
  • say — 'Say' marks an example rather than a definition, so one particular man stands in for the ordinary first name he happens to have.

The day after a satirist was thrown out of a palace.

The House of Hanover was the German line that gave Britain its four Georges, so the definition wants a person belonging to that house rather than the house itself.

3 down TINNITUS

Overturned legal action about new fool’s complaint in hearing (8)

Definition: complaint in hearing · reversal + container + charade

Tinnitus is the ringing in the ears that has no source outside them, so it is literally a medical complaint of the hearing.

  • Overturned legal action → TIUS a suit is an action brought in court, entered here from its last letter back to its first
  • new → N n = new, the abbreviation stamped on any relaunched product
  • fool → NIT a nit is a fool in British usage, as in nitwit
  • Overturned — Something overturned has been stood on its head, so the piece beside it is written out from its last letter to its first.
  • about — A thing placed about another wraps round it, so the turned piece opens up and the smaller letters go inside.

A lawsuit thrown out of court, with a foolish man's grievance heard instead.

'Complaint in hearing' looks like an instruction to listen for a sound-alike, and it is the definition: a complaint of the ears, meaning an ailment rather than a grumble.

4 down MISMATCH

Discrepancy is holding up male striker? (8)

Definition: Discrepancy · charade

A mismatch is a pair of things that fail to agree, which is what a discrepancy is — one noun for the other.

  • male → M m = male, the abbreviation on every form you fill in
  • is → IS the clue's own word, taken as it stands and propped up from below
  • striker → MATCH a match is the thing you strike to make a flame
  • holding up — In a down entry whatever does the holding sits underneath what it supports, so the little word goes below the letter for the man.

A discrepancy in the paperwork delaying a male footballer.

'Male striker' looks like one footballer and is really two separate pieces of the build — and the striker is the thing you strike, not a player.

5 down KUROSAWA

OK, USA angry with raw Japanese film-maker (8)

Definition: Japanese film-maker · anagram

Akira Kurosawa was Japan's most internationally famous director, so the definition names him by nationality and trade instead of by name.

  • OK, USA → OKUSA five letters of fodder
  • raw → RAW three more, making the eight the enumeration asks for
  • angry — Somebody angry has lost their composure, and letters that lose theirs will not stay in the arrangement the clue hands them over in.

The United States giving grudging approval while furious about something served raw.

The surface is all grumbling about food, and there is no hint in it that a director's name is being spelled out. Akira Kurosawa made Rashomon and Seven Samurai.

7 down BASH

Crash into party (4)

Definition: Crash · double definition

One sense is a heavy blow or collision; the other is the informal British word for a large party, and the two meanings are unrelated.

  • Crash → BASH to drive hard into something, with the noise that goes with it
  • party → BASH a big informal party, as in a fiftieth birthday do

Somebody blundering uninvited into a party.

'Into' is joinery and nothing more, which is why the three words read as a single action rather than as two senses standing side by side.

8 down ETNA

Regularly observed Neptunian volcano (4)

Definition: volcano · alternate letters

Etna is the huge active volcano in Sicily, so the bare word names the answer by the category it belongs to.

  • Neptunian → ETNA start at the second letter and take every second one after it, right through the word
  • Regularly observed — Watching something regularly means taking it at fixed intervals, and the interval here is one letter in every two.

An astronomer keeping a regular watch on a volcano out at Neptune.

Neptune has no volcano of this kind, so the picture is science fiction and the real work is counting letters. Etna is the great cone on Sicily.

12 down EXPATRIATE

English pirate confused with tax exile (10)

Definition: exile · anagram

An expatriate lives outside their own country, so 'exile' is a noun for the same person, though an exile is more often driven out.

  • English → E e = English, the standard single letter, joining the fodder
  • pirate → PIRATE six letters of fodder
  • tax → TAX three more, making ten in all
  • confused — A confused thing has its parts muddled together, so the letters beside it are re-laid in an order the clue never gives.

A bewildered English pirate tangled up with the taxman.

'Tax exile' is the trap: the two words look like one familiar phrase, and only the second of them belongs to the definition — the first is fodder.

13 down NONSENSE

Rubbish collection of points almost nobody accepts (8)

Definition: Rubbish · charade + deletion

Nonsense is talk with no sense in it, and 'rubbish' is the everyday British dismissal for exactly that — not the refuse the surface suggests.

  • almost nobody → NON the four-letter word for not anybody, one letter short
  • collection of points → SENSE S, E and N are all compass points, and five of them stand here in a row
  • almost — Almost a word is not quite all of it, so the last letter is left off before the piece is used.
  • accepts — To accept a thing is to take it on, and the joining is loose here — the second piece is simply added on rather than let inside.

A worthless tally of points that hardly anybody will accept.

'Rubbish collection' reads as one phrase about bins, and the definition stops after the first word. Compass points standing in for letters are a staple worth banking: N, S, E and W.

14 down GENERATE

Create classes in biology on vacuous theme (8)

Definition: Create · charade + outer letters

To generate something is to bring it into existence, which is what 'Create' asks for — verb for verb, with no shade of difference.

  • classes in biology → GENERA the groupings a biologist sorts species into, sitting between family and species
  • theme → TE the word emptied of its middle, keeping only its first and last letters
  • on — This is a down entry, so a piece sitting on another is written above it and comes first as the answer reads downwards.
  • vacuous — A vacuous word has had its insides emptied out, leaving only the letter at each end.

A biology lesson on classification hung on a rather empty topic.

'Classes' is loose: class is a rank of its own in biological classification, and the groupings the wordplay wants sit several rungs below it.

16 down LOCK ON TO

Track automatically not changed after secure start to Olympics (4,2,2)

Definition: Track automatically · charade + first letter + anagram

To lock on to something is what radar does when it fixes on a target and follows it unaided, which is tracking automatically.

  • secure → LOCK to lock a door is to make it secure
  • start to Olympics → O the opening letter of the word, taken on its own
  • not changed → NOT three letters of fodder, waiting to be re-laid
  • changed — Letters told they are changed do not stay as they were, so the three beside this word come out in a different arrangement.
  • after — What comes after something is written behind it, so the disturbed letters are added at the end of what precedes them.
  • start to — The start of a word is its opening letter, and that single letter is all the clue takes from the name.

Nothing altered since the Games got safely under way.

'Not changed' is the joke: the word 'not' is precisely the thing that changes, while 'changed' is an instruction rather than part of the description.

19 down PRIVET

Shrub split in part (6)

Definition: Shrub · container

Privet is the dense-leaved shrub grown for suburban hedging, so 'Shrub' defines it by the plain category it belongs to.

  • part → PT pt is the everyday shortening of part, as in Pt 1 of a series
  • split → RIVE to rive something is to split it apart, the verb behind 'riven'
  • in — The little word puts one piece inside the other rather than beside it, and that placement is the whole instruction.

A garden shrub that has split partway down.

RIVE is worth banking: it means to tear or split, and it survives mainly in crosswords, old poetry and the word riven.

20 down NOVA

Increasingly bright star Victor wears angora, off and on (4)

Definition: Increasingly bright star · container + alternate letters

A nova is a star that suddenly brightens by many magnitudes, so the definition describes the flare-up astronomers named it for.

  • angora, off and on → NOA every second letter of the wool, counting from the second
  • Victor → V v = Victor in the phonetic alphabet used over the radio
  • wears — Somebody wearing a garment is inside it, so the letter for the man goes within the letters taken from the wool.
  • off and on — Something happening off and on happens at every other turn, so every second letter is taken and the rest dropped.

A rising star called Victor who wears angora from time to time.

'Star' points at Hollywood in the picture and at astronomy in the definition — a star that flares up in brightness rather than one who is famous.

21 down GASP

Finally evading snake, breathe with effort (4)

Definition: breathe with effort · charade + last letter

To gasp is to draw breath sharply and with difficulty, which is the laboured breathing the definition describes — verb for verb.

  • Finally evading → G the closing letter of the second word
  • snake → ASP an asp is the small viper of Cleopatra's story
  • Finally — Taken finally, a word gives up only its closing letter, and the rest of it plays no part in the build.

Somebody struggling for breath after narrowly dodging a snake.

'Finally evading snake' reads as a narrow escape, and the comma is the only sign that 'finally' is an instruction rather than part of the story.
