Across

9 across AGREE

Comply with one hooked on avarice without end? (5)

Definition: Comply · charade + deletion

To agree to something is to comply with it: both verbs mean consenting to do as you have been asked.

  • one → A The indefinite article standing in for the number one.
  • avarice → GREED Avarice is greed, the word the deletion then goes to work on.
  • without end → The final D is dropped.
  • hooked on — Something hooked on is fastened to what comes next, so the two pieces are joined end to end.
  • without end — A word without its end stops one letter short, losing the one it would have finished on.

Somebody with an addiction to greed that shows no sign of letting up.

'Comply with' looks like one phrase, but the 'with' is only joinery and the definition is the first word alone. 'Without end' is a deletion, not a claim that the avarice goes on for ever.

10 across ATONEMENT

Reparation that one mentioned in passing (9)

Definition: Reparation · hidden word

Atonement is making amends for a wrong, so 'reparation' fits in the moral sense rather than the financial one.

  • that one mentioned → ATONEMENT Nine consecutive letters run through these three words, beginning two letters into the first.
  • in passing — Something said in passing runs straight through the sentence without stopping, and so do the letters wanted.

A remark about compensation, dropped casually into somebody else's conversation.

'Mentioned' reads as a flag for how a word sounds, and it is really part of the hiding place. Nine letters lying in the open are harder to see than four, because nobody expects a span that long.

11 across HIGHLIGHT

Hair colour of intoxicated bully at core ‘not starting conflict’ (9)

Definition: Hair colour · charade + middle letter + deletion

A highlight is a lightened streak put into hair at the salon, so 'hair colour' names the treatment rather than a shade.

  • intoxicated → HIGH Somebody high on a drug is intoxicated.
  • bully → L The middle letter of this five-letter word.
  • conflict → IGHT Conflict is a FIGHT, deprived here of its opening F.
  • at core — The core of a word is its middle, so a five-letter word gives up its third letter and nothing else.
  • not starting — A word not starting has lost the letter it would have begun with, and picks up one along.

The hair colour of a drunk bully who insists he is not the one starting a fight.

The quotation marks look like reported speech from the bully and are really wrapping an instruction. 'Hair colour' is the definition, and it wants a colourist's word rather than a shade such as auburn.

12 across PAINS

Displacing the leader of a country causes agonies (5)

Definition: agonies · cycling

Pains are agonies: the plural noun for bodily suffering, as in growing pains rather than the effort of taking pains.

  • a country → SPAIN The country on the Iberian peninsula, whose opening letter is about to be moved.
  • Displacing the leader — Displacing a leader turns the first letter out of its post, and it goes round to the back of the queue rather than away.

Toppling the head of a country brings nothing but suffering.

'Displacing the leader' is the news story and the instruction at once, and the letter pushed out of the front is not lost — it rejoins at the back. 18 across takes a leader off a country too, and does throw it away.

13 across STARE AT

Boatman in base for watch (5,2)

Definition: watch · container

To stare at something is to watch it fixedly, so the phrase substitutes for the verb 'watch' and not for the noun.

  • base → SEAT A seat is a base: the seat of government, the seat of power.
  • Boatman → TAR A tar is an old word for a sailor, and Boatman is also the name printed above this puzzle.
  • in — In places the piece before it inside the word named next, rather than alongside it.

A boatman back at his base, taking his turn on watch.

Boatman is the setter signing his own work, and he doubles here as the sailor's nickname. 'Watch' asks for a verb, which is why it means to look and not a thing worn on the wrist.

15 across ABUSERS

Aggressors led out of rules-based order in chaos (7)

Definition: Aggressors · anagram + deletion

Abusers treat others cruelly or use them wrongly, so 'aggressors' names them by what they do, and both nouns are plural.

  • rules-based → RULESBASED The ten letters the wordplay starts from.
  • led out of → An L, an E and a D are taken away, leaving seven.
  • order → 'Rules-based order' is one phrase in the surface; only the first half of it supplies any letters.
  • led out of — 'Led' names three letters and 'out of' takes them away from the word that follows.
  • in chaos — Letters in chaos have lost their arrangement, so they may come back in any order at all.

Aggressors being shown out of a rules-based order that has collapsed into chaos.

'Rules-based order' is such a fixed phrase that the eye refuses to break it, and the clue needs it broken: the letters come from the first half, and the second is scenery.

17 across PUT-UP

Falsely planned from both sides (3-2)

Definition: Falsely planned · palindrome

A put-up job is one arranged beforehand in order to fool somebody, so 'falsely planned' is the adjective's exact sense.

  • Falsely planned → The definition end of the clue: arranged in advance so as to deceive, the way a fraud is.
  • from both sides → The wordplay end: five letters that read the same starting from either end, which is all the help on offer.
  • from both sides — Reading from both sides means starting at either end, which only ever works when the letters are the same either way.

A scheme cooked up dishonestly by two parties at once.

'From both sides' reads as two parties conspiring and is the whole of the wordplay: nothing is turned into anything else, so no fragment hands over letters.

18 across RAN

Stood by elimination of a country’s leader (3)

Definition: Stood · deletion

To stand for office and to run for it are the same act, one British and one American, and both are here in the past tense.

  • a country → IRAN The country this puzzle keeps circling back to.
  • leader → Its first letter, I, is the one eliminated.
  • elimination of — To eliminate something is to get rid of it, so the letter named is struck out of the word.
  • leader — A leader stands at the head, so the letter wanted is the one the word begins with.

A regime toppled by taking out its head of state.

'Stood' is the political sense — standing for election — and that is what makes the past tense fair. 12 across takes a leader off a country too, but sends it round to the back instead of binning it.

20 across POWER

Might is right? Captive enemy’s leader taken at the front (5)

Definition: Might · charade + first letter

Might is power in the plainest sense: the capacity to make things happen, or to make other people do as you say.

  • Captive enemy → POW A POW is a prisoner of war, a captured enemy soldier.
  • enemy’s leader → E The same word is used a second time, now for its opening letter only.
  • right → R R is the standard abbreviation for right.
  • leader — The leader of a word is the letter at its head, and that letter alone is taken.
  • taken at the front — Taken at the front says where these pieces go rather than which letters they are: ahead of the one already named.

The old maxim about might and right, tried out on a captured enemy commander.

'Enemy' does double duty, first as the captive and then as the word whose first letter is wanted — setters are allowed that, and it is easy to read past. The question mark queries the maxim, not the wordplay.

22 across RECKONS

Playing conkers with judges (7)

Definition: judges · anagram

To reckon something is to judge it — 'I reckon he's right' delivers a verdict — and the verb matches in the third person.

  • conkers → RECKONS The seven letters of the game's name, rearranged.
  • Playing — Playing means at play rather than at rest, so the letters beside it are loose and free to move about.

Children playing conkers while the grown-ups judge the contest.

Conkers is the British playground game of swinging a horse chestnut on a string at an opponent's. 'Playing' sits at the very front, which is where an anagram indicator is easiest to walk past.

25 across WREATHE

Honour as peace finally follows devastation of the war (7)

Definition: Honour · anagram + last letter

To wreathe is to encircle with a wreath, so honouring the dead at a memorial is the verb's ordinary use, both in the infinitive.

  • the war → WREATH Six letters devastated into a new arrangement.
  • peace finally → E The last letter of peace.
  • devastation of — Devastation leaves a place with its parts strewn about, which is what happens to the letters named after it.
  • finally — Finally points at the letter a word ends on, and nothing else of that word is used.
  • follows — What follows comes after, so this letter is written at the end of the assembly.

Honouring the dead once peace has come at last, after the destruction of a war.

'Honour' is a verb here, which is what makes the answer's ending fair. The joke is that the letters of 'the war', wrecked, come out as the thing that gets laid at the memorial afterwards.

26 across MEANT

Signified Boatman by symbol of industry (5)

Definition: Signified · charade

Signified and meant are the same past-tense verb: what a word, a sign or a gesture conveyed to whoever read it.

  • Boatman → ME The setter is pointing at himself, and ME is how a setter writes that.
  • symbol of industry → ANT The ant is the proverbial tireless worker, and the crossword's fixed answer to 'industry'.

The setter indicating himself with an emblem of hard work.

Boatman turns up twice in this grid and means something different each time; here he is the setter rather than the sailor of 13 across. 'Symbol of industry' is a pairing worth learning whole.

27 across DIPLOMACY

Order: comply with aid talks (9)

Definition: talks · anagram

Talks between governments are diplomacy, the word for the whole practice of negotiating rather than for any single meeting.

  • comply with aid → COMPLYAID Nine letters from the two words either side of the joining 'with'.
  • Order — To order things is to put them into an arrangement, and any arrangement of these letters is allowed.

An instruction to fall in with a relief programme and start negotiating.

'Order' opens the clue looking like a command being issued, colon and all, and it is the instruction to rearrange. 9 across used 'comply' as its definition; here the same word is raw material.

30 across LOGORRHEA

Content of blogs: sorry, cheap, using too many words (9)

Definition: using too many words · charade + middle letters

Logorrhea is an uncontrollable flood of words, so the phrase names as an activity exactly what the noun names as a condition.

  • blogs → LOG The three letters left when the outer two are taken off.
  • sorry → ORR Same again: the middle three of a five-letter word.
  • cheap → HEA And once more, the inside of a five-letter word.
  • Content of — The content of a word is what lies inside it, so the letter at each end is thrown away and the middle kept.

A verdict on what blogs actually contain: apologetic, cheap and far too long.

One instruction governs three separate words here, which is the thing to spot — 'Content of' is not just about the blogs. The answer is the medical name for an unstoppable flood of speech.

31 across SONAR

Cycling round raising fire as a defence against submarines (5)

Definition: a defence against submarines · cycling

Sonar is the echo-sounding gear ships use to find submarines, so it is a defence in the sense of a detector rather than a weapon.

  • raising fire → ARSON Fire-raising is the British name for the crime of deliberately setting a blaze.
  • Cycling round — Letters that cycle round travel from the front to the back keeping their order, like beads pushed round a loop.

Somebody cycling round and stirring up a blaze, apparently to keep submarines away.

'Cycling round' reads as a bike ride and is the instruction. Fire-raising is the British term for arson, rewritten here as 'raising fire' so that it passes for an ordinary verb phrase.

Down

1 down RASH

Ill-considered result of conflagration after 7th of October (4)

Definition: Ill-considered · charade + last letter

A rash decision is one taken too fast to have been thought through, so the adjective is a plain synonym for ill-considered.

  • October → R The letter standing seventh, and last, in the month's name.
  • result of conflagration → ASH What a fire leaves behind once it has burnt itself out.
  • 7th of — October is a seven-letter word, so asking for its seventh picks out the single letter standing at the very end.
  • after — What comes after something is written behind it, so the piece named first ends up second in the assembly.

Something reckless done in the wake of a fire, dated to the seventh of October.

The date is the trap: it reads as a day on the calendar, and 7 October is a date this puzzle's theme keeps circling. Nothing about the month matters here except how it is spelt.

2 down PREGNANT

Expecting quiet fury to rise over holy books (8)

Definition: Expecting · charade + reversal

Expecting, used on its own, is the polite English for carrying a child, which is what the answer says plainly.

  • quiet → P P is the musician's mark for piano, meaning played softly.
  • fury → REGNA Fury is ANGER, and the down entry takes those five letters upwards.
  • holy books → NT NT is the New Testament, the second half of the Bible.
  • to rise — In a down entry a thing that rises is read from the bottom upwards, so those letters arrive in the opposite order.
  • over — One thing over another sits above it, so this half is written before the letters it covers.

Bracing for a hushed outrage to break out over some sacred texts.

'Expecting' reads as the opening of a sentence about anticipation and is the definition standing alone. NT is worth owning: 'holy books', 'testament' and 'scripture' all point at those two letters.

3 down HEEL

With the end of truth, he’s evasive: a despicable type (4)

Definition: a despicable type · charade + last letter

A heel is a contemptible person, a sense borrowed from American slang of the 1920s and still the crossword's standard despicable man.

  • truth → H The final letter of the word, the one the instruction points at.
  • evasive → EEL An eel is the byword for a creature that slips out of your grasp.
  • the end of — The end of a word is the letter it finishes on, so only the last of these five is taken.

A slippery man, caught out at the last, turning out to be thoroughly nasty.

'Evasive' has to be read as a noun — the creature that is proverbially impossible to hold — rather than as an adjective describing the man in the surface. The colon does the work of an equals sign.

4 down LAUGHTER

Paroxysm of pointless killing? (8)

Definition: Paroxysm · deletion

A paroxysm is an uncontrollable fit or convulsion, and the phrase it turns up in most often is 'a paroxysm of laughter'.

  • killing → SLAUGHTER Killing on a large scale is slaughter.
  • pointless → The S of south — a point of the compass — comes off the front.
  • pointless — A point is a compass point, so a word made pointless has to give up the S that stands for south.

A fit brought on by senseless mass killing.

'Pointless' pretends to be a judgement on the killing and is an instruction to drop a compass point. The question mark covers the leap from carnage to a fit of the giggles.

5 down PORTIA

Apparently a lawyer, she is one that moves quickly in hearing (6)

Definition: Apparently a lawyer, she · homophone

Portia is no lawyer but pleads as one in disguise in The Merchant of Venice, which is exactly what 'apparently' concedes.

  • one that moves quickly → PORTIA A Porsche is a fast car, and the name said aloud is written out here a different way.
  • in hearing — In hearing asks for what the words sound like when spoken, not for how anybody writes them down.

A woman who looks like a barrister and is quick on her feet in court.

'In hearing' does double duty: a court hearing in the surface, and the ear in the wordplay. The sound wanted is a German sports car, so the trick is hearing a make of vehicle in 'moves quickly'.

6 down DEEP PURPLE

Band initially denied piss-up over unmixed disc set up inside (4,6)

Definition: Band · charade + reversal + container

Definition by example: 'Band' names any band, and this one is the English hard-rock group behind 'Smoke on the Water'.

  • initially denied → D The opening letter of the word the instruction stands beside.
  • piss-up → EEP Piss is PEE, and the 'up' of piss-up lifts those three letters in a down entry.
  • unmixed → PURE Something unmixed has nothing else in it.
  • disc → PL A disc is an LP, the vinyl album, stood on its end.
  • initially — Initially asks for nothing of a word but the letter it opens with.
  • over — In a down entry what is over something is written above it, so this half goes on top of the rest.
  • set up — Something set up has been stood on its end, so in a down entry its letters are read from the bottom.
  • inside — Inside says the piece is buried within another word rather than added at either end of it.

A group denying there was ever a drinking session, with an untampered record slipped in among the evidence.

'Band' on its own is the definition, though it reads as the subject of the sentence that follows it. A piss-up is British for a heavy drinking session, and LP is the crossword's standard disc.

7 down REGIME CHANGE

Clue for emigré in coup (6,6)

Definition: coup · anagram

A coup is the forcible replacement of one government by another, which is what the phrase names in the language of foreign policy.

  • emigré → REGIME The six letters of emigré, set down in a different order.
  • Clue for → CHANGE For those six letters to work as a clue they need an instruction telling you to rearrange them, and supplying it is the job of the second half.

Read the answer as an instruction and it shuffles a word for you: apply it to its own first half and 'emigré' falls out. Setters call that backwards arrangement a reverse anagram.

8 down OTIS

Toti Soler covers King of Soul (4)

Definition: King of Soul · hidden word

Definition by example: Otis Redding was billed as the King of Soul, and the first name alone is enough to point at him.

  • Toti Soler → OTIS Four letters run consecutively across the gap between these two names.
  • covers — To cover something is to lie over the top of it, so the letters wanted are concealed beneath the words named.

A guitarist recording cover versions of a soul singer's back catalogue.

'Covers' works twice over: recording somebody else's song in the surface, lying on top of the letters in the wordplay. Toti Soler is a Catalan guitarist, and his name is here only for the letters it carries.

13 down SUPER

Society to raise former ruler to be glorious (5)

Definition: glorious · charade

Super means splendid or first-rate — the everyday British 'super!' of approval, which stands in for glorious well enough.

  • Society → S S is the standard abbreviation for society.
  • to raise → UP To raise something is to put it up.
  • former ruler → ER ER is Elizabeth Regina, the late Queen's cipher, and she is a former ruler now.

A society raising a monument to a dead monarch, to glorious effect.

'To raise' in a down clue looks like an instruction to turn a piece upside down; here it is only a synonym for a two-letter word. ER is worth owning, since any crossword's 'ruler' tends to be her.

14 down EXPLOITERS

Assembly of Experts with grip on oil wrongly supply profiteers (10)

Definition: profiteers · anagram + container

Exploiters use people or resources for their own gain, which is what profiteers do with a shortage — and both words are plural.

  • Experts → EXPTERS The seven letters of Experts, rebuilt in a new order and then split open.
  • oil → LOI Three more letters, also reordered, dropped into that gap.
  • Assembly of — An assembly is a putting together, so the letters that follow are to be built up in a fresh order.
  • grip on — Something with a grip on another thing holds it fast, so these letters close around the ones named next.
  • wrongly — Letters set down wrongly are not in the order they were handed over in.

A clerical assembly with its hands on the oil, enriching the people who profit from it.

The Assembly of Experts is the Iranian clerical body that chooses the Supreme Leader, which is why the surface hangs together so well. 'Supply' reads as a verb in that sentence and is only the join between wordplay and definition.

16 down SURGE

Powerful movement, certain to embrace an end to fighting (5)

Definition: Powerful movement · container + last letter

A surge is a sudden forward rush — of water, of electricity, or of troops sent in — so 'powerful movement' covers it directly.

  • certain → SURE Certain and sure are the same adjective.
  • fighting → G The last letter of the word.
  • to embrace — To embrace something is to put your arms right round it, so this word closes over the letter named next.
  • an end to — The end of a word is the letter it finishes on, and that single letter is all that is wanted.

A powerful movement that is certain to welcome the end of a war.

'An end to fighting' reads as a ceasefire, which is what the whole sentence is about, and it asks only for the letter fighting ends on. The definition has a military sense too: a sudden reinforcement of troops.

19 down NEWSPEAK

Fox etc an extreme instance of Orwellian communication (8)

Definition: Orwellian communication · charade

Newspeak is the invented state language of Nineteen Eighty-Four, so the definition names it by its author rather than by its content.

  • Fox etc → NEWS Fox is a channel of that kind, and 'etc' widens it to the category the channel belongs to.
  • an extreme instance → PEAK The extreme instance of anything is its peak.

A television network held up as the worst example of political doublespeak.

'Fox etc' asks you to climb a level: not the broadcaster itself but the kind of thing it is. Orwell invented the language to make dissent literally unthinkable, by removing the words for it.

21 down WET PAINT

Where Trump, lacking guts, is not to touch this: would be unwise (3,5)

Definition: to touch this: would be unwise · charade + outer letters

The answer is the wording on the sign, and the reason anybody puts it up is precisely that touching would be unwise.

  • Where → WE The outside of the word, with HER taken out of the middle.
  • Trump → TP The same treatment again: RUM comes out and the two ends close up.
  • is not → AINT Ain't is the colloquial contraction of 'is not', written here without its apostrophe.
  • lacking guts — The guts of a word are its innards, so a word lacking them keeps only the letters standing at either end.

A gutless Trump keeping well clear of somewhere, with a warning that meddling would be unwise.

Two words in a row are gutted the same way, which is the pattern to spot: a pair of two-letter scraps taken off the outsides. The definition is a notice on a bench, not a comment on politics.

24 down SADDHU

Strictly austere, doubly devout Hindu, unshod primarily (6)

Definition: Strictly austere, doubly devout Hindu, unshod primarily · &lit + first letters

The whole clue reads straight: a saddhu is a Hindu ascetic who renounces possessions, goes barefoot and lives by strict devotion.

  • Strictly austere, doubly devout Hindu, unshod → SADDHU Six words in order, each handing over the letter it starts with.
  • primarily — Primarily asks for what comes first, so each word in the list gives up only the letter it opens with.

A barefoot Hindu holy man, twice as devout as anybody else and owning nothing at all.

Every word does both jobs at once: the sentence describes the man, and taken from the front letter by letter it also spells him. 'Doubly' quietly warns that a letter is about to arrive twice.

26 down MALE

Man using computer virus etc: battle is lost (4)

Definition: Man · deletion

A male is a man, the word used when the sex rather than the person is the point, and both are singular nouns.

  • computer virus etc → MALWARE Viruses, worms and trojans are all malware, and 'etc' asks for that category.
  • battle → A battle is a WAR, and those three letters are the ones that disappear.
  • is lost — What is lost has gone from what remains, so the piece named drops out of the longer word.

A man losing the battle against the viruses on his computer.

'Etc' is the signal to climb from an example to the class it belongs to, exactly as 'Fox etc' does at 19 down. What comes out sits in the middle of the longer word, not at either end.

28 down OUST

Depose leader missing in combat (4)

Definition: Depose · deletion

To oust somebody is to force them out of their position, which is precisely what deposing a ruler means — both plain verbs.

  • combat → JOUST A joust is single combat between two knights on horseback.
  • leader missing → The J at its head is the letter absent.
  • leader — The leader of a word is the letter at its head, and that is the one singled out here.
  • missing — Something missing is simply not there, so the letter named never appears in the answer.

A leader vanishing in the middle of a fight.

'Leader missing' reads as a news bulletin — a head of state gone — and is an instruction about a first letter. A joust is the tournament combat of the Middle Ages, fought with lances.

29 down YARD

Where to hang sheets in back garden (4)

Definition: Where to hang sheets · double definition

Both senses are everyday: the yard of a sailing ship is the spar its canvas hangs from, and a yard is also the plot behind a house.

  • Where to hang sheets → The nautical reading: the timber crossing a mast, from which the canvas is spread.
  • back garden → The domestic reading: the paved or grassed patch behind a house.

Pegging out the washing on a line in the back garden.

'Sheets' is the trap. The surface shows bedlinen on a line, and the first definition is at sea, where sheets are ropes and sails are spread from a spar. No letters move at all.
