Across

8 across SHOOTOUT

Leave pitch for final battle (8)

Definition: final battle · charade

A shootout is the climactic gunfight of a western, and a penalty shootout is the battle that finally settles a drawn tie.

  • Leave → SHOO 'shoo' is what you say to drive a cat or a pigeon away
  • pitch → TOUT a tout pitches tickets to passers-by outside the ground

A player walking off the pitch just as the decider begins.

'Leave pitch' looks like one football instruction and is two words clued separately — an order to be off, and the patter of a man selling tickets.

9 across APRIL THE FIRST

When filthier traps set? (5,3,5)

Definition: When · anagram

'When?' is answered by a date, and this is the date on which the tricks the surface describes are traditionally sprung.

  • filthier traps → FILTHIERTRAPS thirteen letters of fodder
  • set — 'set' is the printer's word for type arranged on a page, and type arranged again comes out in a different order.

Somebody asking when the nastiest snares get laid.

Every 10 in this grid is a fool, and this is the day they own — the puzzle was published on it. 'Filthier traps' reads as the snares of the surface and is thirteen letters waiting to be moved.

10 across FOOL

Bottom, for example, sweet (4)

Definition: Bottom, for example · double definition

Bottom, the ass-headed weaver, is one instance of the word; the other sense is the pudding of fruit whipped into cream.

  • Bottom, for example → the weaver in A Midsummer Night's Dream who is given an ass's head, offered as one instance rather than a synonym
  • sweet → 'sweet' is the British name for the dessert course, and this one is fruit crushed into whipped cream

A bottom described, oddly enough, as something sweet.

Seven other clues in this grid point back at this one, because the answer is the puzzle's theme. A gooseberry fool is the pudding; Bottom is only one of Shakespeare's many candidates.

11 across IN TRAINING

Moderation ultimately cutting it, wet acquiring skills (2,8)

Definition: acquiring skills · charade + container + last letter

Someone in training is acquiring skills — the phrase covers athletes and apprentices alike, both still mid-process rather than finished.

  • it → IT the clue's own word, taken as it stands, and about to be split open
  • Moderation ultimately → N the closing letter of moderation
  • wet → RAINING when it is wet outside, it is raining
  • ultimately — 'ultimately' means at the very end, and the end of the word beside it is where the single letter comes from.
  • cutting — someone cutting in pushes into the middle of something already under way, so the letter lands inside rather than after.

Restraint at last proving good enough, while a soggy sort picks up new skills.

'Cutting it' reads as the idiom for being good enough, and the two words are not even in the same piece of the wordplay: one is an instruction, the other is material.

12 across SHTICK

Cane has whipped hard in routine (6)

Definition: routine · container

A performer's shtick is the routine they are known for — the same gag or turn wheeled out at every appearance.

  • Cane → STICK a cane is a stick
  • hard → H H = hard, the grade stamped on a pencil
  • has whipped — to whip something, in British slang, is to pinch it, and what the cane pinches it keeps inside itself.

A cane coming down hard, apparently as part of somebody's regular practice.

Shtick is Yiddish for a piece or slice, and a comic's shtick is the bit of business they do every time out. 'Whipped' is the pickpocket's sense here, not the beating the surface shows.

15 across COSTARD

Hoax has taken in famous 10 (7)

Definition: 10 · container

10 is FOOL: Costard is the rustic clown of Love's Labour's Lost, the comic simpleton the play sends about with letters.

  • Hoax → COD a cod is a hoax, and cod as an adjective means fake
  • famous → STAR a famous name is a star
  • has taken in — 'has taken in' is what a household does with a lodger: the next piece is given a room inside.

A hoax that has swallowed a celebrity whole.

'Cod' for a hoax survives in codswallop and cod Latin. A costard is also a big old English apple, which is where the character got his name.

17 across CATHODE

Rat traps however ending in pipe where current flows (7)

Definition: where current flows · container + last letter

A cathode is an electrode: the terminal in a valve, tube or cell that the current passes through.

  • Rat → CAD a rat is a cad, an untrustworthy sort
  • however → THO tho' is the poetic short form of though
  • pipe → E the last letter of pipe
  • traps — a trap holds on to whatever walks into it, so the word before it shuts around the next piece.
  • ending in — 'ending in' points at the final letter of the word that follows it.

Rat traps laid at the end of a pipe with water running through it.

The surface is plumbing and vermin and the answer is electrical. Tho' is the sort of poetic contraction setters keep on hand whenever three awkward letters are wanted.

20 across SPRAY-GUN

Aerosol device possibly empty, gas having run out (5-3)

Definition: Aerosol device · anagram + outer letters

A spray gun is the hand tool that atomises paint or pesticide into an aerosol, so the definition names it by what it produces.

  • possibly empty → PY 'possibly' with its insides gone leaves the first and last letters
  • gas → GAS three letters of fodder, taken as they stand
  • run → RUN three more letters of fodder, again straight off the page
  • empty — 'empty' hollows a word out: the middle goes and only the letters standing at each end are left.
  • out — 'out' here is out of order, so the letters gathered up do not stay in the sequence the clue lists them in.

An aerosol can that may be finished, its propellant all used up.

'Having run out' reads as one phrase about an empty canister and is doing two jobs: the first word is material and the second is the instruction.

22 across INDIGO

One of seven bands that’s popular enjoy love (6)

Definition: One of seven bands · charade

Indigo is one of the seven colour bands Newton listed in the rainbow, sitting between blue and violet.

  • popular → IN in = popular, as in what is in this season
  • enjoy → DIG to dig something is to like it, in the slang of the sixties
  • love → O in tennis love is a score of nothing, written as a zero

A well-liked band, one of seven, basking in adoration.

The bands are the rainbow's rather than music's: Newton named seven colours so that the spectrum would match the notes of a scale, which is how a narrow blue-violet got a place of its own.

23 across TOUCHSTONE

10 bit something hard (10)

Definition: 10 · charade

10 is FOOL: Touchstone is the jester who follows Rosalind and Celia into the Forest of Arden in As You Like It.

  • bit → TOUCH a touch of something is a small bit of it
  • something hard → STONE a stone is the hard thing you stub a toe on

Somebody biting down on something hard.

A touchstone proper is the dark stone jewellers rub gold against to judge its quality, which is where the everyday sense of a standard to measure things by comes from.

25 across FESTE

Interminable rot for 10 (5)

Definition: 10 · deletion

10 is FOOL: Feste is the licensed jester of Twelfth Night, employed at Olivia's house to be witty for a living.

  • rot → FESTE the six-letter verb for what rot does to fruit, docked of the letter it ends on
  • Interminable — 'Interminable' means without end, so the word it describes gives up the letter it ends on.

Endless nonsense, served up to an idiot.

'Rot' is the British word for nonsense in the surface and a verb in the wordplay. Feste is the jester who sings Twelfth Night out with the wind and the rain.

26 across EMISSARY

Agent exhausted having killed leader and rescued girl (8)

Definition: Agent · deletion + container

An emissary is somebody sent on a mission on another's behalf — an agent in the diplomatic sense rather than the spying one.

  • exhausted having killed leader → EARY WEARY is exhausted, and its leading W is killed off
  • girl → MISS a miss is a girl — the form of address for an unmarried woman
  • killed leader — the leader of a word is its first letter, and killing it takes that letter off the front.
  • rescued — anyone rescued is taken in and sheltered, so the girl ends up housed inside the other word.

An exhausted spy who has killed the boss and saved a girl.

The thriller in the surface does its killing to a letter: 'leader' is the first character of a word. An emissary carries a message rather than a gun.

Down

1 down CHOO-CHOO

Soften tobacco, say, doubly loudly – for puffer? (4-4)

Definition: puffer · homophone + charade

A puffer is a steam locomotive, and the nursery name for one imitates the noise it makes going along.

  • Soften tobacco, say → CHOO a plug of tobacco is softened between the teeth, and the word for doing that, said out loud, is written this way in the nursery
  • doubly → CHOO the same noise is called for a second time
  • doubly — 'doubly' asks for the same piece over again, so whatever the first half hands you gets written out twice.
  • loudly — 'loudly' is about saying a word rather than spelling it, so what goes in the grid is what the ear hears.

Tobacco worked soft and a shout repeated twice over.

'Puffer' looks like a fish or an inhaler and is the nursery word for a steam engine. A chew is the wad of tobacco you soften in your cheek.

2 down BOWL OVER

Astound Cockney Casanova? (4,4)

Definition: Astound · charade

To bowl someone over is to knock them flat, and the figurative sense — leave them astonished — is the one in everyday use.

  • Cockney → BOW a true Cockney is born within the sound of Bow Bells, the church bells of Bow in London's East End
  • Casanova → LOVER the eighteenth-century Venetian's name is the byword for a great seducer

An East End ladies' man, held up as something to take your breath away.

The break in the wordplay is not the break in the answer: the two pieces divide one letter earlier than the two words that go in the grid.

3 down YORICK

10, barely sick, housebound? (6)

Definition: 10 · container + middle letters

10 is FOOL: Yorick was the king's jester in Hamlet, remembered as the skull Hamlet holds in the graveyard.

  • housebound → YORK the House of York, the royal house of the white rose in the Wars of the Roses
  • sick → IC the outer S and K are stripped off, leaving the two letters in the middle
  • barely — 'barely' is stripped bare, and a word stripped bare gives up the letters standing on its outside.
  • housebound — the '-bound' half is the instruction: anyone bound to a house cannot get out of it, so the house closes round what follows.

A sickly figure who can barely get out of the house.

'House' is the royal House of York rather than a home, so the invalid stuck indoors is a picture the surface paints and the wordplay never uses.

4 down STATUTE

Rule with old king in new seat (7)

Definition: Rule · anagram + container

A statute is a law as written down by a legislature, which is the formal sense of a rule imposed rather than a rule of thumb.

  • seat → SEAT four letters of fodder
  • old king → TUT Tutankhamun, the boy pharaoh, is the old king crosswords keep on the shelf
  • in — 'in' places one piece within another instead of alongside it, so the king lands in the middle rather than at the end.
  • new — 'new' means made afresh, and letters made afresh come out in an order the clue has not printed.

A coronation: an old monarch installed on a newly made throne.

Every word of the coronation picture is doing letter work, and Tut is the standard crossword king — three letters, permanently available, no other pharaoh so short.

5 down HABANERA

A taboo embraced by goddess in aria (8)

Definition: aria · container

A habanera is a Cuban dance song, and the famous one is Carmen's, so 'aria' names it by its best-known example.

  • goddess → HERA Hera, queen of the Greek gods and wife of Zeus
  • A taboo → ABAN a taboo is a ban, and the clue's own A comes along with it
  • embraced by — 'embraced by' is a hug: whatever does the embracing opens its arms and takes the other piece inside.

A goddess folding something forbidden into her arms.

The clue's opening 'A' belongs to the wordplay rather than to the grammar, which is the hardest thing to see here. Hera is the four-letter goddess setters reach for first.

6 down BRANDISHED

Design I dropped flourished (10)

Definition: flourished · charade

Brandished and flourished are the same act in the past tense: a sword or a document waved about for show.

  • Design → BRAND a brand is the design a company stamps on what it sells
  • I → I the clue's own letter, taken as it stands
  • dropped → SHED to shed something is to drop it, the way a tree sheds leaves

A design its creator abandoned went on to thrive.

'Flourished' is the waving sense, not the thriving one the surface offers, and 'dropped' is a synonym at work rather than the fate of the design.

7 down PLANKS

10s in wood? (6)

Definition: 10s · double definition

10 is FOOL: in British usage a plank is a blockhead, and planks are also the sawn boards cut out of a tree.

  • 10s → the British insult for the very dim, as in 'thick as two short ones'
  • wood → sawn timber, the boards a mill turns a trunk into

Idiots blundering about in a wood.

British slang makes a plank a dolt — 'thick as two short planks' is the phrase behind it — so both halves of the clue are talking about the same timber.

13 down IN THAT CASE

The satanic works, atheist can too then! (2,4,4)

Definition: then · anagram

'Then' in the sense of 'if so': 'then I'll walk' and 'in that case I'll walk' are the same sentence.

  • The satanic → THESATANIC ten letters of fodder
  • atheist can too → these three words hold the very same ten letters over again, which is the setter's joke rather than extra material
  • works — 'works' claims the phrase functions as raw material: put to work, its letters take up new positions.

A claim that satanic writings work, and that an atheist could manage as much.

Both phrases carry the same ten letters: 'the satanic' and 'atheist can' each rearrange into IN THAT CASE, and the exclamation mark is the setter enjoying the coincidence.

16 down REGISTER

Record for example in brief, bar lifted? (8)

Definition: Record · deletion + container

A register is a record kept in a book — the roll a school calls or a church writes marriages into.

  • brief, bar lifted → RISTER in British slang your brief is your barrister, the lawyer arguing your case, and BAR is lifted off the front of him
  • for example → EG the everyday two-letter shorthand for 'for example'
  • in — 'in' is a placement word: what comes before it is dropped inside what comes after.
  • lifted — 'lifted' is the shoplifter's sense — the bar is pinched from the word and carried off.

Something noted down in a lawyer's summary once the bar has been raised.

In British slang a brief is a barrister, so the bar comes off the man and not off the document. Reading 'lifted' as a raising, which a down clue would license, is the false trail: it means stolen.

18 down DOGBERRY

Stalk with fruit 10 (8)

Definition: 10 · charade

10 is FOOL: Dogberry is the blundering constable of Much Ado About Nothing, the play's self-important idiot.

  • Stalk → DOG to dog someone is to follow them doggedly about
  • fruit → BERRY a berry is the small soft fruit on a bush

A plant stalk with its fruit still attached.

'Stalk' is the verb here while the surface keeps it botanical alongside the fruit. Dogberry's mangled words — 'Comparisons are odorous' — are what Shakespeare built the character out of.

19 down SNOOKER

10’s game (7)

Definition: 10 · double definition

10 is FOOL: to snooker someone is to outwit them into a hopeless position, and snooker is also the baize game itself.

  • 10 → the verb sense: to leave somebody thwarted and out of options, which is what a trickster does to a mark
  • game → the one played on green baize with fifteen reds, six colours and a rest

The apostrophe reads as a possessive and is standing in for 'is', with a definition either side of it. The verb comes from the table game: leave your opponent no shot and you have snookered them.

21 down PROOFS

Page and covers – those to check? (6)

Definition: those to check? · charade

Proofs are the trial copies read for mistakes before printing, so 'those to check' names them by the only job they exist for.

  • Page → P p. is the standard short form of page
  • covers → ROOFS a roof covers a building, and the verb is doing the work here

A page and its covers, waiting for somebody to look them over.

'Covers' looks like the boards of a book, keeping company with the page in the surface. Proofs are the trial pages a printer sends out so that errors get caught before the run.

22 down IRENIC

Wrath appealing? Not quite for pacifist (6)

Definition: pacifist · charade + deletion

Irenic means aiming at peace, which is the outlook a pacifist holds — the adjective to their noun.

  • Wrath → IRE ire is wrath
  • appealing → NIC NICE means appealing, and it is cut short of its last letter
  • Not quite — 'Not quite' stops the word just short of its full length, so the final letter never gets written down.

Wondering whether anger has its attractions, and deciding not quite.

Irenic comes from Eirene, the Greek goddess of peace, and describes anything meant to make peace. The question mark after 'appealing' warns that the synonym is about to be docked.

24 down OAST

Top off, beach drier (4)

Definition: drier · deletion

An oast is a hop-drying kiln, so 'drier' names it by the single job the building was put up to do.

  • beach → OAST the five-letter word for the shoreline, with its opening C taken off
  • Top off — in a down entry the top is the first letter, and 'off' is what takes it away.

Somebody stripped to the waist on the beach, drying off.

An oast is the kiln with the white cowl on its roof that dries hops for brewing, a familiar shape in the Kent countryside.
