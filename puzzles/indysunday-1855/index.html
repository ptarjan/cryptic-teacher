Across

1 across GOVERNESS

Old childminder shot French writer's son (9)

Definition: Old childminder · charade

A governess was the woman a household employed to live in and teach its children, a post that has all but died out, which is what 'Old' flags.

  • shot → GO a shot at something is a go at it
  • French writer's → VERNES Jules Verne, the French novelist, with the possessive s the clue prints after him
  • son → S s is the standard abbreviation for son

An elderly nanny photographing the son of a French novelist.

'Shot' looks like a photograph and means an attempt, and the apostrophe s that reads as ordinary possession in the sentence is letters the wordplay needs.

6 across DEMIT

Scottish give up bad diet around start of May (5)

Definition: Scottish give up · anagram + container + first letter

Demit is the Scots and legal word for giving up an office — resigning a post, rather than abandoning a habit.

  • diet → DIET anagram fodder, four letters
  • start of May → M the first letter of May
  • bad — Food gone bad has gone off, and letters that have gone off will not stay in the order the clue prints them.
  • around — Something placed around another thing encloses it, so the shuffled letters close over the single letter named next.

A Scot swearing off a poor diet as May begins.

'Give up' reads as abandoning a habit and means resigning an office, and 'Scottish' is the label a dictionary puts on this verb rather than a piece of the picture.

9 across INCOG

Using secret identity, male avoids coming out (5)

Definition: Using secret identity · deletion + anagram

Incog is the clipped form of incognito, the state of going about under an assumed name so that nobody knows you.

  • coming → COMING the fodder, shuffled once a letter has left it
  • male → M m is the standard abbreviation for male, and it is the letter kept out
  • avoids — What a word avoids it keeps clear of, so the letter named just before it never joins the mix.
  • out — A thing that is out is out of order, and letters out of order have to be put back in some other arrangement.

A man with something to hide keeping quiet about who he really is.

'Coming out' is one phrase in the surface and two separate instructions in the wordplay, which is exactly where the clue does its hiding.

10 across GO BANANAS

When catching granny on backed-up toilet, get potty (2,7)

Definition: get potty · charade + container + reversal

Going bananas is losing your head, and 'potty' is the British word for mildly mad, so the two idioms match sense for sense.

  • toilet → BOG the bog is British slang for the lavatory
  • When → AS as = when, in the sense of 'as I came in'
  • granny → NANA nana is one of the family names for a grandmother
  • catching — Catching something is closing round it, so the word in front takes the word named next inside itself.
  • on — In an across clue a thing on another has been laid on top of it, so the piece named first is written after the one it rests on.
  • backed-up — Backed-up traffic has come back along its own route, and in an across entry that means the letters run right to left.

Walking in on your grandmother at a blocked lavatory, and reaching for the potty.

'Potty' is the chamber pot in the surface and the British word for barmy in the definition, and the plumbing keeps the whole picture pointed the wrong way.

11 across TINTAGEL CASTLE

Stain and stick together shed in Beer, busy SW seaside spot (8,6)

Definition: busy SW seaside spot · charade + container

Tintagel Castle is the cliff-top ruin on the north Cornish coast, tied to the Arthur legend and packed with visitors all summer.

  • Stain → TINT to tint something is to stain it with colour
  • Beer → ALE the capital letter makes it a Devon village on the surface; the drink is what the letters want
  • stick together → GEL when things gel they set and stick together
  • shed → CAST an animal that sheds its skin casts it
  • in — A thing described as in another sits inside it, so the two pieces just named are dropped between the letters of the word that follows.

Painting and gluing a beach hut back together in the crowded seaside village of Beer.

Beer really is a small seaside village in Devon, which is what makes the capital letter honest and the definition so hard to prise away from the wordplay next to it.

13 across ROADBED

Splintering boarded base for rail (7)

Definition: base for rail · anagram

A roadbed is the prepared foundation of earth and ballast that railway track is laid on, so the rails literally sit on it.

  • boarded → BOARDED anagram fodder, seven letters
  • Splintering — Wood that is splintering is breaking into pieces, and the word beside it breaks into loose letters to be put back another way.

Boards splintering under the footing of a handrail.

'Splintering boarded' reads straight past as carpentry, which is what hides the shuffle, and 'rail' is the railway rather than a banister.

15 across THERMAL

After cycling that lady's whisky is warming (7)

Definition: warming · charade + cycling

Thermal underwear is the warming kind, and the adjective covers anything that holds or gives off heat.

  • that lady's → HER that lady's = her, the possessive
  • whisky → MALT a malt is a Scotch whisky
  • After cycling — Cycling carries the letters round like a bicycle chain: their order never changes, but the read starts at a different point.

After a bike ride, the woman's whisky is doing its work against the cold.

The cyclist in the surface is the joke: the letters go round in exactly the way she does. Malt is the crossword's standard whisky, worth keeping.

17 across HEELING

"Getting health back" read out on a list (7)

Definition: on a list · homophone

A ship heeling is leaning over to one side, and that tilt is what sailors call a list.

  • "Getting health back" → HEELING getting your health back is healing, and that sound has a twin with another spelling
  • read out — Reading something out turns it into a sound, so the quoted phrase is wanted for how it is said and not for how it is spelt.

A get-well message being read aloud from a list of names.

The quotation marks are the setter flagging that the phrase inside is to be heard rather than read, and the list at the end is a boat's lean, nothing to do with names.

19 across PASS OUT

Dad's heading for Antarctica shortly to complete army training (4,3)

Definition: complete army training · charade + deletion

To pass out is to graduate from a military academy — the parade at the end of training, not fainting on it.

  • Dad's → PAS pa is dad, and the clue's apostrophe s comes along with him
  • heading for Antarctica → SOUTH the direction you head in to reach Antarctica
  • shortly — Something put shortly is cut short, so the word in front of it gives up its final letter.

Dad about to set off for the Antarctic just as the army training ends.

Everywhere but the parade ground the answer means fainting; here it is what cadets do on the day they finish, and the polar trip hides a plain compass point.

20 across MARCHING ORDERS

Doctor beginning to doubt Roger's charm in the sack (8,6)

Definition: the sack · anagram + first letter

Marching orders are the instruction to leave, which is what getting the sack amounts to — dismissal issued as a command.

  • beginning to doubt → D the first letter of doubt
  • Roger's charm in → ROGERSCHARMIN anagram fodder, thirteen letters, the apostrophe ignored
  • Doctor — To doctor something is to tamper with it, and letters tampered with come back in a different order.

A physician beginning to suspect that Roger is charming his way into bed.

'In the sack' is bed in the surface and dismissal in the definition, and the little word 'in' belongs to the letters rather than to the phrase it appears to start.

24 across IMPRECATE

Curse that is extended by politician dealing with whip (9)

Definition: Curse · container + charade

To imprecate is to call down a curse on somebody, the formal verb for wishing them harm out loud.

  • that is → IE i.e. is the Latin id est, printed where you would say 'that is'
  • politician → MP MP is a Member of Parliament
  • dealing with → RE re means concerning, the word at the head of a memo
  • whip → CAT the cat-o'-nine-tails, the lash once used in the navy
  • extended by — Something extended is stretched out, so the two letters named first are pulled apart to make room for everything that follows.

A swear word drawn out by an MP handling the party whip.

The whip is the party official in the surface and the sailor's lash in the wordplay — the same shortening the Royal Navy has given English for two hundred years.

25 across TAKEN

You shouldn't have everything you know taught (5)

Definition: taught · charade

In British schools a teacher takes a class — 'who takes you for maths?' — so a lesson taken by somebody is one they taught.

  • You shouldn't have → TA 'ta' is the casual thank-you, and 'you shouldn't have' is what goes with it
  • everything you know → KEN your ken is the whole range of what you know

A complaint that nobody should be spoon-fed everything they know.

One word at the very end is doing the defining, which is easy to miss in a sentence that reads all the way through as a single grumble about education.

26 across LINKS

Chain left marks on skin (5)

Definition: Chain · charade

Links are the connected rings a chain consists of, so naming them names the chain.

  • left → L l is the standard abbreviation for left
  • marks on skin → INKS to ink somebody is to tattoo them, marking the skin

A chain that has left its marks pressed into somebody's skin.

The whole thing reads as one sentence about a chafed wrist, so nothing shows where the definition stops — and 'left' is the side, not the past tense of leave.

27 across FORM HORSE

Best runner probably in class runs in socks (4,5)

Definition: Best runner probably · charade + container

The form horse is the one whose recent results make it the likeliest winner, which is what 'probably' is conceding.

  • class → FORM a form is a class at school
  • socks → HOSE hose is the old word for stockings and socks
  • runs → R r is the cricket scoreboard's abbreviation for runs
  • in — One thing in another is inside it, so the single letter named goes between the letters of the word for socks.

The likeliest winner of the school race, running in nothing but his socks.

'Runs' is the cricket abbreviation rather than the verb the racing surface wants, and a horse's form is its record of recent results, which is where the phrase comes from.

Down

1 down GRIP

Almost all grouse in bag (4)

Definition: bag · deletion

A grip is a soft travelling bag, the holdall sense of the word rather than the handshake one.

  • grouse → GRIPE to grouse is to gripe — both mean to complain
  • Almost all → the instruction that costs the complaint its last letter, the E
  • Almost all — Taking almost all of a word means stopping one letter short of its end, so the final letter never gets written down.

Nearly all the shot grouse fitting into one bag.

'Grouse' is the moorland bird in the surface and the verb 'complain' in the wordplay, and the shooting party's game bag is the picture hiding the luggage sense.

2 down VACCINATE

Hearing of hols in narrow boat, prepare to cope with awful conditions (9)

Definition: prepare to cope with awful conditions · charade + homophone

To vaccinate is to prime somebody's immune system in advance so that it can meet a disease already ready for it.

  • hols → VAC the vac is what British students call the university holidays
  • narrow boat → EIGHT an eight is the long thin racing boat pulled by eight oarsmen
  • hols in narrow boat → VACCINATE say the three little words straight through and the joins between them vanish
  • Hearing of — Hearing of something reaches you as sound, so the phrase that follows is wanted for the noise it makes rather than its spelling.

Told that the holiday will be spent on a canal boat, you brace yourself for grim weather.

The narrow boat of the surface is a canal barge and the wordplay wants the racing sort, the rowing eight; 'hols' is schoolboy shorthand the setter expects you to carry.

3 down RIGHT

Stand back up in ceremony when told (5)

Definition: Stand back up · homophone

To right a capsized dinghy is to set it upright again, which is standing it back up.

  • ceremony → RIGHT a ceremony is a rite, and that syllable has several spellings in English
  • when told — Something told is spoken rather than written, so the word before it is wanted for its sound and comes out spelt another way.

Getting back on your feet in the middle of a ceremony.

'Stand back up' reads as an instruction to a person and is what you do to a capsized boat, while the vertical entry makes 'back up' feel like a reversal it never is.

4 down ENGAGED

Promised to stop talking English in terminus (7)

Definition: Promised · charade + container

Engaged means promised in marriage — a pledge given, not a diary that is full.

  • terminus → END a terminus is the end of the line
  • stop talking → GAG to gag somebody is to stop them talking
  • English → E e is the standard abbreviation for English
  • in — One thing said to be in another sits inside it, so the letters gathered first are dropped between the two ends of the word that follows.

Somebody who has promised to stop speaking English by the time the train reaches the last station.

'Terminus' drags the whole surface towards railways, where the answer would be a signal or a telephone line; the definition wants the betrothal sense and stands alone at the front.

5 down SUBPLOT

Group beneath lead us on the climb in novel feature (7)

Definition: novel feature · charade + reversal

A subplot is the secondary story running alongside the main one, so it is a feature of the form rather than of any single book.

  • lead → PB Pb is the chemical symbol for lead, from the Latin plumbum
  • us → US the clue's own word, taken at face value
  • Group → LOT a lot is a group of people or things
  • beneath — In a down entry the letters run from the top down, so the piece said to be beneath the rest is written last.
  • on the climb — Something on the climb is heading upwards, and upwards in a down entry is back towards the start, so those letters are read from their far end.

A party roped in below the leader as they take us up the mountain.

'Lead' looks like the climber at the front of the rope and is the metal, which is where the mountaineering surface does its work; the novel of the definition is a book, not something new.

6 down DUNGAREES

Overalls brown with smeared grease (9)

Definition: Overalls · charade + anagram

Dungarees are the bib-and-brace trousers pulled on over your clothes for dirty work, which is the class 'overalls' names.

  • brown → DUN dun is a dull greyish brown, the colour of a dun horse
  • grease → GREASE anagram fodder, six letters
  • smeared — Smearing something spreads it about so that nothing stays where it was put, and the letters beside it are spread about the same way.

A mechanic's brown work clothes with oil smudged all over them.

The greasy brown workwear the surface describes is the very thing being defined, so there is no misdirection to see through — only the shuffle, and a colour word that survives mostly in horses.

7 down MANET

Painter turned up one hour before morning tea-break (5)

Definition: Painter · reversal

Manet was a nineteenth-century French artist, so the definition simply names his trade.

  • one hour before morning tea-break → TENAM the British mid-morning tea-break is elevenses, so an hour earlier is ten am
  • turned up — Turning something up in a down entry sends it back towards the top, so the letters are read from the bottom end first.

The decorator showed up an hour before the mid-morning tea-break.

'Painter' is a decorator in the surface and an artist in the definition, and 'turned up' does the same double duty — arriving, and being written out backwards.

8 down TESSELLATE

Tile Lesley positioned upside down at the last minute (10)

Definition: Tile · charade + reversal

To tessellate is to cover a surface with shapes that meet edge to edge with no gaps, which is what tiling a floor does.

  • Lesley → LES the name docked to its everyday short form
  • positioned → SET something positioned has been set in place
  • at the last minute → LATE doing a thing at the last minute is leaving it late
  • upside down — Something upside down has its ends swapped, so the two short pieces in front of it are written out from the back.

Lesley putting a tile up the wrong way round with no time to spare.

'Tile' is a verb here rather than a noun, which is the whole disguise: the clue reads as somebody sticking one on a wall in a hurry.

12 down GRAHAM HILL

Racing driver I see eating meat in steak restaurant (6,4)

Definition: Racing driver · container

Graham Hill won the Formula One world championship twice in the 1960s, so the definition names him by the class he belongs to.

  • steak restaurant → GRILL a grill is the kind of restaurant that cooks its steaks over heat
  • I see → AH 'ah' is the noise you make on seeing what somebody means
  • meat → HAM ham is meat, cured from a pig's leg
  • eating — Something eating another thing has it inside, so the piece just named swallows the one that follows.
  • in — A piece said to be in something goes between its letters, and here the whole assembly drops inside the word at the end.

A diner nodding in recognition over a plate of meat at a steakhouse.

'I see' is not somebody looking but the noise of understanding, which is the piece that stays hidden longest. The champion here fathered another one, Damon.

14 down BLIGHTERS

Empty bottles filled by blonder people in shower? (9)

Definition: people in shower · container + outer letters

Blighters are contemptible fellows, and a shower is British slang for a hopeless bunch of them, so the definition carries the disapproval too.

  • Empty bottles → BS bottles with everything between the outer letters poured away
  • blonder → LIGHTER hair that is blonder is lighter
  • Empty — An empty container has had its middle taken out, so only the outside letters of the word that follows are left standing.
  • filled by — Something filled by another thing takes it inside, so the next word goes into the gap the emptying has just made.

A crowd of fair-haired drinkers refilling the bottles they have emptied.

'Shower' is not rain but the British insult for a useless bunch, and the question mark is the setter owning up to how loose that is.

16 down MOONRAKER

One creating secret character for Spooner in spy film (9)

Definition: spy film · spoonerism

Moonraker is the 1979 James Bond picture, so the definition works by example — one spy film standing in for the class.

  • secret character → MOON a rune is a letter of the old Germanic alphabet, long used for writing in secret
  • One creating → RAKER one who creates a thing is its maker
  • for Spooner — The Reverend Spooner was famous for swapping the opening sounds of two words, so the pair described here trade their first noises.

Somebody writing a mystery part for the Reverend Spooner in a spy movie.

Say 'rune maker' and trade the opening noises. The vowel slides a little on the way — 'roon' becomes 'moon' — which is the sort of drift a spoonerism is allowed.

18 down GANDALF

McKellen's magic role also in Blue Peter coming up? (7)

Definition: McKellen's magic role · container + reversal

Ian McKellen played the wizard Gandalf in the Lord of the Rings films, so the definition points at the actor's part rather than naming the character.

  • Blue Peter → FLAG the Blue Peter is the blue-and-white flag a ship flies when about to sail; the children's programme was named after it
  • also → AND and = also, tacking one thing onto another
  • in — One thing in another goes between its letters, so the short word named first is dropped inside the piece that follows.
  • coming up — A down entry is read from the top downwards, so anything coming up is written out from its bottom end back to its top.

The actor's wizard turning up on the children's programme Blue Peter.

The italics dress Blue Peter up as the television title, while the wordplay wants the sailing flag the programme borrowed its name from.

19 down PIONEER

Innovator going east during long walk on stilts (7)

Definition: Innovator · charade + container

A pioneer is the first into new ground, so 'innovator' names the same person by what they are first to do.

  • long walk on stilts → PIER a seaside pier is a long walkway standing on legs above the water
  • going → ON a machine that is going is on
  • east → E e is the compass abbreviation for east
  • during — Something happening during another thing falls inside it, so the letters named first go between the letters of the walk.

An inventor heading east on a long walk on stilts.

'On stilts' describes the structure, not the walker, which is the reading the surface works hardest to prevent; 'going' is the everyday sense of a machine left running.

21 down RIPEN

Fully develop small state prison (5)

Definition: Fully develop · charade

To ripen is to come to full maturity, which is what fully developing means of fruit and of plans alike.

  • small state → RI Rhode Island, the smallest state in the US, whose postal code is RI
  • prison → PEN the pen is American slang for the penitentiary

A plan to build out the little state's jail.

'Develop' looks like the property developer's word that the rest of the surface sets up, and means maturing instead; American geography and American slang supply the two halves.

22 down DITCH

Land where there isn't any? (5)

Definition: Land where there isn't any? · cryptic definition

To ditch is to bring an aircraft down on water, so the answer is a landing made where no land exists — and the same word names the water-filled trench.

  • Land → the surface offers this as a noun, a stretch of ground; the setter wants the verb a pilot uses
  • where there isn't any → nothing to come down on but water, which is the emergency the word describes

A riddle about setting down somewhere that has no ground at all.

Pilots use this word for putting a plane down on the sea, and the same word names a trench with water in it, so 'where there isn't any' is doing the whole job.

23 down ANTE

Grecian tenderly holds the beginnings of a pot (4)

Definition: the beginnings of a pot · hidden word

The ante is the opening stake each player puts up before the cards are dealt, so it is literally what a pot starts as.

  • Grecian tenderly → ANTE run the two words together and four consecutive letters straddle the join
  • holds — What a phrase holds is already sitting inside it, so the letters are there in the clue and only need picking out.

Somebody Greek cradling the makings of a pot.

'The beginnings of' looks like an instruction to take first letters and is part of the definition instead, where the pot is the poker one and not the Grecian urn.
