Guardian Cryptic Crossword No 30,000

Across

7 across PERIMETER

Edges of empire sacked by Egypt’s last queen (9)

Definition: Edges · anagram + last letter

A perimeter is the line running right round the outside of an area, which is what all the edges of it come to.

  • empire → EMPIRE six letters of fodder
  • Egypt’s last → T the closing letter of Egypt, thrown in with the fodder
  • queen → ER ER is Elizabeth Regina, the monogram a British queen signs with
  • sacked — A sacked city has been ransacked and left in disorder, and letters left in disorder no longer sit in the order they were handed over.
  • last — The last of a word is the letter it finishes on, so the name in front of this gives up one character and keeps the rest.

Cleopatra's army burning its way along the outlying provinces of a neighbouring empire.

Egypt's last queen was Cleopatra, and that is the picture only: the wordplay wants nothing of her, just the letter her country ends on and the crossword's standard two for a queen.

8 across TODAY

Told ally to lose three pounds before tomorrow (5)

Definition: before tomorrow · deletion

Anything that has to happen before tomorrow has to happen today, so the definition fixes the day by the one that comes after it.

  • Told ally → TOLDALLY eight letters to start from, the two words run together
  • three pounds → LLL a pound is written L, from the Latin libra, and three of them go
  • to lose — Losing something means it is no longer there, so what is named after this phrase comes out of the letters named before it.

Telling a friend to shed three pounds before the day is out.

The dieting surface is what hides the pounds: they are money rather than weight, and the L they give up is the same L drawn as the pound sign.

9 across BIRTH SIGN

Bring this novel for Leo, perhaps (5,4)

Definition: Leo, perhaps · anagram

Leo is one of the twelve signs of the zodiac people are born under, so it defines the answer by being an instance of it.

  • Bring this → BRINGTHIS nine letters of fodder
  • novel — Something novel has not been seen in that shape before, so the letters are wanted in an arrangement the clue has not printed.

Asking somebody to bring a new book along for a friend called Leo.

'Novel' sits between the fodder and the definition passing as the book in the surface, and 'perhaps' is the standard flag that Leo is one example of the answer rather than a synonym for it.

10 across FLOOR

Reportedly failing, fell (5)

Definition: fell · homophone

To floor somebody is to knock them to the ground, which is what a boxer does when he fells an opponent — verb for verb.

  • failing → FLOOR a failing is a flaw, and British speech leaves the r unsounded, so the two words come out identical aloud
  • Reportedly — A report passes on what somebody said, so the word wanted is reached by ear and then written down some other way.

Word reaching you that somebody in decline has gone over.

Both words read as talk about a person's health, and the last one is the definition: 'fell' here is the verb for knocking a man down, not the past tense of falling.

12 across TSONGA

Some thoughts on Gabonese Bantu language (6)

Definition: Bantu language · hidden word

Tsonga is the Bantu language of southern Mozambique and the South African lowveld, so the definition names it by the family it belongs to.

  • thoughts on Gabonese → TSONGA six letters in a row, beginning inside the first word and finishing inside the last
  • Some — Some of a thing is a part of it, so what is wanted is a run of letters taken from inside the words that follow rather than all of them.

A few opinions offered about the languages spoken in Gabon.

The country is the misdirection: this language belongs to southern Africa, thousands of miles from Gabon, and the letters run straight across the gaps between the words.

13 across ACROSTIC

Always, come rain or shine, tongue in cheek? (8)

Definition: Always, come rain or shine, tongue in cheek? · &lit + first letters

An acrostic is a piece of writing whose initial letters spell something out, and this clue is one, which is what the question mark flags.

  • Always, come rain or shine, → ACROS the letter each of these five words begins with, taken in the order printed
  • tongue in cheek? → TIC the last three words keep the run going, the little joining word included

Somebody promising to turn up whatever the weather, and not entirely meaning it.

The sentence performs what it names: read the letter each word starts with and the clue has written the answer out along its own front edge. 'Tongue in cheek' is the setter admitting as much.

14 across ASUNDER

In bits, heads to America (see below) (7)

Definition: In bits · first letters + charade

Something in bits has been broken apart, and asunder is the adverb English keeps for exactly that state — put asunder, torn asunder.

  • America → A the letter the country's name opens with
  • see → S the first letter of the little cross-reference word inside the bracket
  • below → UNDER below = under, the preposition at face value
  • heads — A head is the front of a thing, so what each word named here gives up is the letter it starts with and nothing more.

A line in a guidebook sending you off to America and pointing further down the page.

The bracket is doing the hiding: '(see below)' reads as a typesetter's cross-reference, and both words inside it are wordplay. Asunder survives in modern English almost only in 'torn asunder'.

17 across BLASTED

Earliest of begonias continued blooming (7)

Definition: blooming · first letter + charade

Both words are polite stand-ins for a curse — a blasted nuisance, a blooming nuisance — so the fit is the exasperated sense, not the floral one.

  • Earliest of begonias → B the letter begonias opens with
  • continued → LASTED a downpour that continued lasted
  • Earliest of — The earliest part of a word is where it starts, so nothing but the opening letter of the flower named next is taken.

The first begonias of the season carrying on flowering.

Definition and answer are both mild British swearing dressed up as gardening. Nothing in the clue is really about flowers: the begonias are there for their first letter and for the surface.

20 across BUCKSHEE

Free money for Americans, with short notice (8)

Definition: Free · charade + deletion

Buckshee means had for nothing, so 'Free' is being used in the costing-nothing sense rather than the one about liberty.

  • money for Americans → BUCKS a buck is a dollar
  • short notice → HEE to take notice of something is to heed it, and this heed stops a letter early
  • short — A word cut short stops before it is finished, so the final letter of the one named beside it never arrives.

Cash handed to Americans at very short notice.

Buckshee is army slang out of the Persian baksheesh, a tip or a handout, and it settled into British English meaning got for nothing. The surface never leaves the world of money.

22 across ROASTS

Goes off round centre of Damascus and swelters (6)

Definition: swelters · container + middle letters

Something that roasts is unbearably hot, so 'swelters' matches the weather sense of the word — a roasting afternoon — rather than the cooking one.

  • Goes off → ROTS milk that goes off rots
  • centre of Damascus → AS the middle pair of the city's eight letters, with three standing on either side
  • round — Going round something means closing on both sides of it, so the first word wraps the letters named after it.
  • centre of — The centre of a word is what has an equal count either side of it, and an eight-letter name has a pair sitting there.

Food going off in the heat around the middle of Damascus.

'Goes off' is milk turning, not a bomb or a departure. The Syrian heat in the surface is there so the definition can pass as part of the same picture.

24 across WISPY

Delicate international agent put on weight (5)

Definition: Delicate · charade

Wispy describes something thin and insubstantial — a wispy beard, a wispy cloud — so 'Delicate' means fineness here rather than fragility.

  • weight → W w = weight, the standard abbreviation on any chart
  • international → I i = international, the usual short form in sport and politics
  • agent → SPY an agent in the espionage sense is a spy
  • put on — Something put on a thing lands on top of it, so the pieces named before this phrase follow the letter named after it.

A frail secret agent who has been eating rather too well.

The joke is the contradiction: putting on weight is the last thing anyone delicate does. In an across clue a piece put 'on' another follows it, which is why the weight comes first of all.

25 across ETYMOLOGY

Exciting yet gloomy study of roots (9)

Definition: study of roots · anagram

Etymology is the study of where words come from — their roots — so the definition uses 'roots' in the linguistic sense the surface keeps hidden.

  • yet gloomy → YETGLOOMY nine letters of fodder
  • Exciting — To excite something is to stir it up, and letters stirred up do not settle back into the order they were given in.

A book about plant roots that manages to be gripping and depressing at once.

'Roots' is the word-origin kind rather than the garden kind, and 'Exciting' opens the clue as an adjective while doing a verb's work on the two words beside it.

26 across QUICK

Brief question for bachelor in automobile (5)

Definition: Brief · substitution

A quick word is a brief one, so the definition is the sense of short duration rather than the one about speed of movement.

  • question → Q q = question, as in a Q and A session
  • bachelor → B b = bachelor, as in a BA degree; this is the letter being shown the door
  • automobile → BUICK Buick is one of the oldest American car marques, now part of General Motors
  • for — X for Y is an exchange at a counter: what follows this word steps out and what comes before it steps into the space.
  • in — What happens in a thing happens inside it, so the exchange is made within the word named after it.

A short question put to the single man sitting in the car.

'Brief question' reads as one phrase and splits between definition and wordplay. 'Automobile' is the American word for a car, which is the nudge towards an American make.

27 across CROSSWORD

Oblique signal in setter’s work (9)

Definition: setter’s work · charade

A crossword is what a setter produces, so the definition points at the answer through the job of the person who makes it.

  • Oblique → CROSS cross means lying athwart something, as in a crosswind or a cross-beam
  • signal → WORD the word is the signal to go, as in 'just say the word'

A sideways hint slipped into the puzzle-maker's own handiwork.

The setter is the person who writes the puzzle, not a dog and not somebody laying a table. This being the Guardian's 30,000th, the final across answer names the thing you are holding.

Down

1 down GENIUS

Einstein retrospectively taking legal action over equation’s origin (6)

Definition: Einstein · container + reversal

Einstein is the name English reaches for when it wants an example of a genius, so the definition names the most famous instance rather than a synonym.

  • taking legal action → SUING to sue somebody is to take legal action against them
  • equation’s origin → E the letter equation begins with, its origin
  • retrospectively — To look at something retrospectively is to look back over ground already covered, so the word beside it is read from its last letter to its first.
  • over — One thing laid over another covers it, so what has just been turned round closes about the single letter named next.

A physicist being taken to court over where his famous formula came from.

The name at the front is the definition rather than the wordplay: it stands for the quality, not the man. E=mc² is the equation the surface is pointing at, and only its opening letter is wanted.

2 down LISTEN IN

Lustrous, wingless bug (6,2)

Definition: bug · deletion

To bug a room is to listen in on what is said inside it, so the definition is the eavesdropping verb and not the beetle.

  • Lustrous → GLISTENING a wet pavement or a polished shoe is glistening, which is lustrous
  • wingless — Wings are the parts that stick out at either side, so a wingless word has given up the letter standing at each of its two ends.

A shiny beetle that has somehow lost its wings.

'Bug' is a verb here, not an insect: to bug a room is to eavesdrop on it. The comma after the first word is the setter's only hint that it is fodder rather than description.

3 down RED SEA

Parched after cycling in area south of Suez (3,3)

Definition: area south of Suez · cycling

The Red Sea is the long water running south from the Gulf of Suez between Egypt and Arabia, so the definition places it on the map instead of naming it.

  • Parched → SEARED ground seared by the sun is parched
  • after cycling — A cycle brings you back round to where you began, so the letters roll to a new starting point and keep their order all the way.

A cyclist arriving thirsty in the desert country below Suez.

Cycling looks like an anagram and is far stricter: no letter changes places with another, the run simply starts three letters further along.

4 down VERGLAS

Loose gravel’s slippery stuff (7)

Definition: slippery stuff · anagram

Verglas is the film of clear ice that forms on rock or road, so 'slippery stuff' names it by what makes climbers dread it.

  • gravel’s → GRAVELS seven letters of fodder, the apostrophe counting for nothing
  • Loose — Something loose is not fixed in place, and letters that are not fixed in place will not stay in the order the clue prints them.

Loose gravel on a mountain road and the slithery mess it makes underfoot.

The apostrophe does the hiding: 'gravel's' reads as a possessive introducing the definition, and the S it carries is the seventh letter of the fodder.

5 down OODLES

See you doffing cap a lot (6)

Definition: a lot · deletion

Oodles is the informal noun for a great quantity, which is what 'a lot' says when nobody is counting.

  • See you → TOODLES toodles, from toodle-oo, is a breezy British way of saying goodbye
  • doffing cap — To doff a cap is to lift it off, and in a down entry the cap of a word is the letter sitting at the top of it.

Somebody raising their hat as they say goodbye.

The last two words read as part of the farewell — see you a lot — and they are the definition standing alone. Down clues can treat the top letter of a word as its hat.

6 down GASOLINE

Fossil fuel left dons suffering agonies (8)

Definition: Fossil fuel · anagram + container

Gasoline is refined from crude oil, so it belongs to the fossil-fuel family the definition names rather than being a synonym for one.

  • agonies → AGONIES seven letters of fodder
  • left → L l = left, as stamped inside a pair of boots
  • dons — To don a coat is to climb inside it, so the letter doing the donning ends up within the letters it puts on.
  • suffering — Anything suffering is being put through the mill, and letters put through it come out in an order they did not go in.

A fuel shortage leaving university dons in agony.

'Dons' reads as a plural noun, the college teachers the fuel has abandoned, and works as the verb for putting a garment on. Gasoline is what most of the English-speaking world calls petrol.

11 down ORAL

Offer and appraisal both turned out to be unwritten (4)

Definition: unwritten · outer letters

An oral agreement is made in speech alone, so 'unwritten' picks the sense of the word that contrasts with paper rather than the one about mouths.

  • Offer → OR the letters at the two ends of the word, with everything between them gone
  • appraisal → AL the same treatment for the longer word: what it opens with and what it closes on
  • both turned out — Turned out means facing outwards, so from each of the two words named you keep only the letters standing at its ends.

A bid and a valuation that were never actually put down on paper.

'Turned out to be' reads as a discovery about a deal and hides an instruction about letters. An oral offer is precisely the kind that leaves nothing on paper, so surface and answer describe one situation.

15 down SPURIOUS

False promise goads nurses (8)

Definition: False · container

Something spurious is not what it claims to be, so 'False' is the plain synonym in the counterfeit sense rather than the merely mistaken one.

  • goads → SPURS a rider's spurs are what goad a horse forward
  • promise → IOU an IOU is a scribbled promise to pay, the letters standing for 'I owe you'
  • nurses — To nurse a thing is to hold it in your arms, so the word doing the nursing closes round the letters named before it.

A pay pledge that turns out to be empty, and the nursing staff it has infuriated.

'Nurses' is the profession the surface is angry on behalf of and a verb underneath, since to nurse something is to cradle it. IOU is worth owning: three letters that spell out what you say when you owe money.

16 down ECHT

Authentic beach huts oddly disappearing (4)

Definition: Authentic · alternate letters

Echt is the German word for real, used in English of the true article as opposed to a copy of it.

  • beach huts → ECHT run the two words together and keep only what sits in the even positions
  • oddly disappearing — The odd ones are the first, the third, the fifth and so on, and here they are the ones that go, leaving whatever stands in the even places.

Beach huts vanishing one by one from a seafront.

'Oddly' is doing arithmetic rather than describing how strange the disappearance is. Echt is German for genuine, borrowed whole into English and used mostly of art and antiques.

18 down SWALLOWS

Buys barrier to stop litter droppers (8)

Definition: Buys · container

To swallow a story is to accept it whole, which is exactly the sense of 'buys' in 'I'm not buying that'.

  • litter droppers → SOWS a sow drops a litter of piglets
  • barrier → WALL a wall is the plainest barrier there is
  • to stop — To stop a gap is to plug it, so the barrier is packed into the middle of the other word instead of standing beside it.

Buying a fence to keep people from dumping rubbish.

'Litter droppers' is people tossing crisp packets about in the surface and pigs in the wordplay. 'Buys' is the sense in 'I don't buy it' — accepting a story rather than paying for anything.

19 down PERTURB

Familiar with your sib’s butt trouble (7)

Definition: trouble · charade + last letter

To perturb somebody is to trouble them, in the sense of unsettling a person's mind rather than putting them to any inconvenience.

  • Familiar → PERT pert means forward and cheeky, familiar in the way that annoys people
  • your → UR the way the word gets typed on a phone by somebody in a hurry
  • sib’s → B the end of sib, the short form of sibling
  • butt — The butt of a thing is its blunt back end, so what is taken from the word in front is the letter it finishes on.

Knowing rather too much about your brother's or sister's backside complaint.

The lavatorial surface is a decoy: 'butt' is not the body part but the blunt end of a word, and text-message spelling is fair game for a Guardian setter.

21 down KOPECK

Roger backing strike for change in Russia (6)

Definition: change in Russia · reversal + charade

A kopeck is the hundredth part of a rouble, so the definition means coinage of small value rather than anything that alters.

  • Roger → OK 'Roger' on the radio means message received, which is to say OK
  • strike → PECK a bird's peck is a short sharp strike with the beak
  • backing — Backing a thing is getting behind it, and a word read from behind hands over its letters in the opposite order.

Roger throwing his support behind a strike for reform in Russia.

'Roger' opens the clue looking like a man's name and is radio code for 'received'. A kopeck is a hundredth of a rouble, so it really is Russian small change.

22 down REMISS

Lacking vigour in harem is shameful (6)

Definition: Lacking vigour · hidden word

Remiss means slack, and the word covers a want of energy quite as much as neglect of a duty.

  • harem is shameful → REMISS six letters in a row that start partway through one word and run on across the joins
  • in — What is in something sits inside it, so the letters wanted are already sitting inside the words that come after it.

Feebleness in a harem being held up as a disgrace.

The run crosses two word joins, which is what keeps it out of sight, and 'in' is the only word telling you to look — it reads as an ordinary preposition.

23 down TIGERS

Chasing game, regularly dispatching zebras and big cats (6)

Definition: big cats · charade + alternate letters

Tigers are the largest of the big cats, so the definition names the group they belong to — plural for plural.

  • Chasing game → TIG tig is the British playground game of chase, called tag in most other places
  • zebras → ERS what is left of the word once the odd-numbered letters have gone
  • regularly dispatching — Dispatching is killing off, and doing it regularly means at every other letter, so the ones in the odd places never arrive.

Hunters chasing game across the plains and picking off zebras as they go.

'Chasing game' reads as the pursuit of prey, which is what the definition's animals do all day, and it is the name of a children's game. Zebras and big cats sit in one picture, so the join never shows.
