Observer Everyman Crossword No 4,144

Setter: Everyman · Published: Sunday 22 March 2026 · Difficulty: Moderate (harder than 31% of the puzzles here) · Grid: 15×15

Across

1 across ARCS

Cockney listens - and bows (4)

Definition: bows · homophone

  • listens → HARKS To hark is to listen — 'hark at him', 'hark the herald angels'.
  • Cockney listens → ARCS With the aitch gone, what is left is said aloud and comes out spelt a different way.

'Bows' first reads as the ones you tie, or the ones you take on stage. The archer's is the one meant, and it is the shape of it that matters.

3 across PROCEDURES

Reproduces dodgy systems (10)

Definition: systems · anagram

  • Reproduces → REPRODUCES The opening word is the raw material, and its ten letters match the enumeration exactly.

A three-word clue leaves nowhere to hide, and the trap is taking the first word as the definition. Count its letters against the enumeration and it is plainly the fodder.

9 across OINK

Sound from pen - why pen no use? (4)

Definition: Sound from pen · charade

  • why pen no use? → The riddle framing: a pen is no use when it has nothing in it. Its two pieces are taken below.
  • no → O Nothing is written as a round zero, which doubles as the letter.
  • pen → INK The second one is the writing kind, and what it runs on is ink.

Two different pens sit in this clue: the first is a sty, the second writes. Everyman reuses the word on purpose, so it belongs to the definition and to the wordplay both.

10 across CLAMOURING

Clutch, clutching loved one, making racket (10)

Definition: making racket · container

  • Clutch → CLING To clutch at a rail is to cling to it.
  • loved one → AMOUR Amour is the French for love and is used in English for a lover — 'a secret amour'.

'Clutch' and 'clutching' sit side by side doing different jobs: the first is the word to be opened up, the second is the instruction to open it.

12 across RAFAEL NADAL

Playing Lendl: a four-time French tennis ace (6,5)

Definition: tennis ace · anagram

  • Lendl → LENDL Ivan Lendl, eight times a grand slam champion, is wanted only for his five letters.
  • a four-time → AAAA The letter A, taken four times over, exactly as the words say.
  • French → FR Fr. is the usual abbreviation for French, the one a dictionary uses to label a borrowed word.

The hard step is reading 'a four-time' literally. It is not describing a champion; it is asking for one letter written out four times over.

15 across PROMPTS

Spurs very quietly storm off ... (7)

Definition: Spurs · anagram

  • very quietly → PP Pianissimo, the score's marking for very quiet, is written as a doubled p.
  • storm → STORM The five letters of the word itself join the two above.

'Spurs' opens the clue looking like Tottenham Hotspur, the London football club, and 'storm off' looks like what a beaten team does. Both readings belong to the surface.

16 across CASH COW

... decrepit coach, exhausted, wants steady source of income (4,3)

Definition: steady source of income · anagram + outer letters

  • coach → COACH The five letters of the word itself, before they are shaken up.
  • exhausted, wants → WS Empty the middle out of the second word and its first and last letters are what remain.

The commas are load-bearing. 'Exhausted' looks like it describes the coach, and the sentence reads perfectly that way — but the word it hollows is the one after it.

17 across ARMENIA

Beggar me! Niagara's surrounding country (7)

Definition: country · hidden word

  • Beggar me! Niagara's → ARMENIA Seven letters run on without a break from the end of the first word, through the second and into the third; the punctuation is ignored.

'Beggar me!' is an old exclamation of astonishment, thrown in purely to smuggle its last two letters into the run. Hidden words often begin mid-word like this.

19 across PROVOKE

In favour of 'very average'!? Everyman's beginning to annoy (7)

Definition: annoy · charade + first letter

  • In favour of → PRO Pro means in favour of — pro-reform, the pros and cons.
  • very → V v. is the everyday abbreviation for very, as in 'v. good'.
  • average → OK Something merely OK is average: neither good nor bad.
  • Everyman's beginning → E Everyman is this setter's own pen name, and what it begins with is its first letter.

Everyman signs his clues, so his name inside one is nearly always letters rather than a person. The quotation marks are the hint that 'very average' is two pieces, not one phrase.

20 across WAKE-UP CALLS

Warnings as Spooner relays outcome of birthday-party food fight (4-2,5)

Definition: Warnings · spoonerism

  • outcome of birthday-party food fight → CAKE UP WALLS The food at the centre of a birthday party is the cake, and a fight ends with it up the walls.
  • Spooner relays → WAKE-UP CALLS Say that aloud with the opening sounds of the outer words traded, C for W.

A spoonerism always names its speaker, so the clue tells you which two sounds to swap before you have any idea what you are swapping them in.

23 across CHARTREUSE

Once again, apply following diagram depicting something intoxicating (10)

Definition: something intoxicating · charade

  • diagram → CHART A chart is a diagram of figures — a bar chart, a flow chart.
  • Once again, apply → REUSE To apply something once again is to put it to use a second time.

Chartreuse is a fiercely strong herbal liqueur made by Carthusian monks in the French Alps, and the yellow-green colour is named after the drink rather than the other way round.

24 across CODA

End-piece: a physician taken aback (4)

Definition: End-piece · reversal

  • a physician → A DOC Doc is the familiar short form for a doctor, with the article kept in front of it.

'Taken aback' reads as the doctor being startled, which is exactly what a good reversal indicator does: it describes a person while instructing the letters.

25 across MURPHY'S LAW

It says you'll go wrong adding potato to cabbage salad (7,3)

Definition: It says you'll go wrong · charade

  • potato → MURPHY Murphy is an old informal British word for a potato, borrowed from the Irish surname.
  • cabbage salad → SLAW Slaw, as in coleslaw, is shredded cabbage dressed as a salad.

The apostrophe is invisible in the wordplay: the two chunks run straight together and it is the solver who puts the punctuation back in.

26 across SKYE

Island on vacation - Sark - yippee! (4)

Definition: Island · charade + outer letters

  • Sark → SK Sark is one of the Channel Islands; emptied out, its first and last letters are left.
  • yippee! → YE The shout of delight, hollowed the same way, leaves the letters at its ends.

One indicator serving two words is the economy that makes a four-letter clue work, and the surface — an island, a holiday, a cheer — never lets on.

Down

1 down APOCRYPHAL

Silly Carol, happy to be uncertain (10)

Definition: uncertain · anagram

  • Carol, happy → CAROLHAPPY A woman's name and the adjective after it supply all ten letters between them.

Everyman's fodder is often a first name plus an adjective, and the step that costs solvers time is seeing that Carol is wanted for her letters, not as a person.

2 down CAN OF WORMS

Tricky situation: farm with no cows to flounder (3,2,5)

Definition: Tricky situation · anagram

  • farm with no cows → FARMNOCOWS 'with' only joins them: farm, no and cows between them supply the ten letters.

A farm with no cows sounds like a subtraction, and it is not: nothing is taken away. All three words stay and hand over every letter they have.

4 down RULINGS

Decrees - when you've repeatedly drawn a line? (7)

Definition: Decrees · double definition

  • Decrees → The legal sense: what a judge or a referee formally hands down.
  • when you've repeatedly drawn a line → The other sense of the verb: to rule is to draw a line against a straight edge, and doing it more than once makes it plural.

The question mark is the setter owning up to the second reading. One word covers a courtroom and a pencil case, and only the plural joins them.

5 down COMEDIC

Some welcome Dickensian characters being amusing (7)

Definition: amusing · hidden word

  • welcome Dickensian → COMEDIC Seven consecutive letters run from the middle of the first word straight on into the second, needing no rearrangement.

Dickens wrote characters, so the surface gives nothing away — which is exactly what a hidden word needs. The join between the two words is where to look.

6 down DOUBLE SCOTCH

Twin to put lid on some whisky (6,6)

Definition: some whisky · charade

  • Twin → DOUBLE A twin is a double — one of an identical pair.
  • put lid on → SCOTCH To scotch a rumour is to put a lid on it and stop it dead.

The second half is doing two jobs at once: the verb that kills a rumour builds the word, while the drink is what the finished phrase orders at a bar.

7 down RAID

Most of logbook picked up in police swoop (4)

Definition: police swoop · deletion + reversal

  • logbook → DIARY A logbook is filled in day by day, which is what a diary is for.
  • Most of logbook → DIAR Take all but the final letter of those five and four are left to turn round.

'Picked up' reads as gossip overheard, which is how it slips past as an instruction — and in a down clue it can only mean read the letters upwards.

8 down SIGN

Notice function making noise (4)

Definition: Notice · homophone

  • function → SIGN The trigonometric function that sits beside cosine and tangent; said aloud it is written another way entirely.

'Function' looks like it wants to be a party or a job, and it is neither — it is the one from school trigonometry, and only its sound is used.

11 down KEEPING WATCH

What guard's doing: not selling retirement gift? (7,5)

Definition: What guard's doing · charade

  • not selling → KEEPING If you are not selling something, you are keeping it.
  • retirement gift → WATCH The traditional present for a long-serving employee on retirement is a gold watch.

The question mark flags a definition by tradition rather than by dictionary: not every leaver gets one, but every crossword leaver does.

13 down SCHOOLWORK

Assignments making reformed crooks howl (10)

Definition: Assignments · anagram

  • crooks howl → CROOKSHOWL The two words after the indicator give up all ten of their letters.

The surface is a police story — reformed criminals, howling — and it hangs together so well that the ten letters sitting in plain sight take a while to notice.

14 down SWEEPSTAKE

Chimney-cleaners draw in drawing of lots (10)

Definition: drawing of lots · charade

  • Chimney-cleaners → SWEEPS A chimney sweep cleans the flue; put in the plural the job title gives six letters.
  • draw in → TAKE A stall that draws in a hundred pounds a day takes a hundred pounds a day.

'Draw' appears twice and means two different things: money coming in for the wordplay, and a name pulled out of a hat for the definition.

18 down ANKLETS

Bling: grotesque slanket (7)

Definition: Bling · anagram

  • slanket → SLANKET A slanket is a blanket with sleeves, sold as loungewear; here it is simply seven letters.

A colon after a one-word definition is Everyman's favourite joinery, and everything on the far side of it is indicator plus fodder and nothing else.

19 down PRUSSIA

Initially powerful realm; ultimately subservient state ignobly abolished! (7)

Definition: Initially powerful realm; ultimately subservient state ignobly abolished! · &lit + first letters

  • powerful realm; ultimately → PRU The first three words of the run hand over the letters they begin with, semicolon and all.
  • subservient state ignobly abolished → SSIA The last four do the same, and the letters arrive in the order the clue reads.

Nothing here is left over for a definition, because the sentence is the definition: seven words of history, each one also giving up its first letter.

21 down SCUM

Mucky film showing Special Constable with hesitant expression (4)

Definition: Mucky film · charade

  • Special Constable → SC A special constable is a volunteer part-time police officer in Britain, and SC is the rank's short form.
  • hesitant expression → UM The noise you make out loud while you hunt for the next word.

'Mucky film' sets up a seedy movie, and the answer turns out to be nothing so interesting: it is the layer that forms on top of standing water.

22 down FAIR

Decent trade show (4)

Definition: Decent · double definition

  • Decent → The sense of adequate rather than admirable: a decent effort, a middling grade.
  • trade show → The industry gathering where firms take a stand and show off what they sell.

Three words and no wordplay, so the whole trick is the join: 'Decent trade' reads as one phrase, which is what stops you splitting the clue in the right place.
