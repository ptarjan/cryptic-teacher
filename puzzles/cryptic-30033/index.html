Across

8 across MISSPENT YOUTH

Long for captive juvenile’s frivolous past (8,5)

Definition: frivolous past · charade

frivolous past → MISSPENT YOUTH: the set phrase is the standard one for early years thrown away on fun, and no single word of it carries the sense alone.

  • Long for → MISS to miss somebody is to long for them
  • captive → PENT pent means shut in, as a captive is
  • juvenile → YOUTH a juvenile is a youth, a young person not yet grown

Yearning for the wild early years of a young prisoner.

'Long for' sits at the front where a definition usually goes and it is wordplay. 'Pent' survives now mostly inside 'pent-up', but on its own it means shut in.

10 across MERE

Just water (4)

Definition: Just · double definition

Just → MERE: mere means nothing more than, as in a mere child; and a mere is a lake, water lying still in the old sense.

  • Just → in the sense of no more than that, as in 'just a scratch'
  • water → a lake or pond, the old word for standing water that survives in English place names

A drink turning out to be nothing but water.

The belittling adjective and a northern word for a lake share a spelling and nothing else, which is how two words manage to be an entire clue.

11 across YOURSELVES

Solvers being solvers with every other beauty product (10)

Definition: Solvers · anagram + alternate letters

Solvers → YOURSELVES: the puzzle is talking to the people solving it, so the plural pronoun stands in for the very readers the clue is naming.

  • solvers → SOLVERS anagram fodder, seven letters — the clue's second use of the word
  • beauty → EUY the second, fourth and sixth letters of bEaUtY
  • product → anagram indicator
  • product — A product is what comes out once things have been worked together, so the letters arrive combined into something new instead of in their own order.
  • every other — 'Every other' means every second one, so only alternate letters of the word beside it are picked up.

Crossword solvers behaving exactly as solvers do, with cosmetics from the counter thrown in.

One word does both jobs: 'Solvers' is the definition, and its second appearance is the fodder. 'Beauty product' splits too, so only the first of those two words hands over letters.

12 across KERNEL

Officer sounding a bit of a nut (6)

Definition: a bit of a nut · homophone

a bit of a nut → KERNEL: the kernel is the soft part inside the shell, which is exactly the bit of a nut anybody eats.

  • Officer → KERNEL an army officer is a colonel, and British speech puts an R in the middle of that word and no L at all
  • sounding — 'Sounding' asks for the noise a word makes rather than its spelling, which is the only place these two words meet.

An officer who strikes everyone as slightly mad.

'A bit of a nut' looks like an instruction to take a few letters out of 'a nut', and it is the definition read literally.

14 across CLOBBERS

Last users promoting the ultimate in rock’n’roll hits (8)

Definition: hits · anagram + last letter

hits → CLOBBERS: to clobber somebody is to hit them a heavy blow, and both words are given here in the same third-person form.

  • Last users → COBBLERS a last is the foot-shaped block a shoemaker builds a shoe on, so the people who use lasts are cobblers
  • rock’n’roll → its final letter, the L, is the one singled out to move; the word hands over nothing of its own
  • promoting — To promote a thing is to move it up the order, so the letter picked out travels nearer the front of the word holding it.
  • the ultimate in — The ultimate item in anything is its last, so this points at the final letter of the word that follows.

The last few listeners still pushing the greatest rock and roll records up the charts.

A last is the shoemaker's foot-shaped block, so its users are cobblers, and only one letter of that word travels: the L that the end of rock’n’roll points at.

16 across FLAT CAP

Tom’s entry for Spooner’s headgear (4,3)

Definition: headgear · spoonerism

headgear → FLAT CAP: the cloth cap with a small stiff peak, the working man's hat of northern England, is one particular piece of headgear.

  • Tom’s → FLAT a tom is a male cat, said "kat"; it gives away its opening K and takes the FL coming back the other way
  • entry → CAP the door a cat lets itself in by is a flap; it hands over its FL and collects the K that has just been let go
  • Spooner’s — Spooner was the Oxford don famous for swapping the opening sounds of two words, so those two openings change places.

A man called Tom submitting an entry to Spooner's hat competition.

A tom is a male cat and its entry is the flap in the door — say those two words, trade the noises at their fronts, and a hat comes out.

18 across STALKER

Skinhead speaker’s undesirable presence (7)

Definition: undesirable presence · charade + first letter

undesirable presence → STALKER: a stalker is the person nobody wants near them, defined here by the effect of being followed rather than by the following.

  • Skinhead → S the head of SKIN is its first letter, so only the S survives the operation
  • speaker → TALKER the speaker at a meeting is the one doing the talking

A shaven-headed orator with somebody unwelcome loitering at the back.

'Skinhead' is one word doing two jobs, the word SKIN and the instruction to keep only its head, while the surface's shaven orator is there to be looked at instead.

21 across MASTODON

Big beast that was unsettling to nomads (8)

Definition: Big beast · anagram

Big beast → MASTODON: the mastodon was the elephant-sized browser of the Ice Age, so the plain phrase names the animal by its size and kind.

  • to nomads → TONOMADS anagram fodder, eight letters
  • unsettling → anagram indicator
  • unsettling — Something unsettling refuses to settle, and letters that will not settle do not stay in the order they were handed over in.

Wandering herders unnerved by something enormous out on the plain.

Nomads and a big beast belong in the same sentence, which is what hides the letters: the fodder is the very people the beast is upsetting.

23 across BLOTTO

Bravo, gambling drunk (6)

Definition: drunk · charade

drunk → BLOTTO: blotto is the slang adjective for being completely drunk, well past merry, and it has no other sense to confuse it with.

  • Bravo → B Bravo is B in the radio alphabet, the one spelt out over the airwaves
  • gambling → LOTTO lotto is the numbers game people gamble on

A shout of approval for a gambler who is thoroughly drunk.

Bravo is B in the radio alphabet, worth owning along with its twenty-five companions, and the comma after it turns the rest of the clue into a cheer.

24 across BRUSCHETTA

Toast giving thanks to Schubert composition (10)

Definition: Toast · anagram

Toast → BRUSCHETTA: the Italian starter is grilled bread rubbed with garlic and oil, so it is toast served as a dish in its own right.

  • thanks → TA ta is the everyday British way of saying thanks
  • Schubert → SCHUBERT anagram fodder, eight letters
  • composition → anagram indicator
  • composition — A composition is something put together out of parts, so the letters beside it are being set in a new arrangement.

A dinner toast raised in thanks to Schubert and one of his pieces.

'Toast' is the food rather than the speech the surface promises, and TA supplies the two letters the composer's name is short of.

26 across HEAR HEAR

They care when warm clothing not provided and we fully support that (4,4)

Definition: we fully support that · middle letters

we fully support that → HEAR HEAR: the cry is what a chamber calls when it agrees with a speaker, so the shout means exactly the sentence given.

  • They → HE THEY undressed of its outer T and Y
  • care → AR CARE with the C and E at its ends taken off
  • when → HE WHEN stripped of its opening W and closing N
  • warm → AR WARM without the W and M that cover it
  • clothing not provided — Clothing is what covers a thing on the outside, so a word given none of it loses the letters at each of its two ends.

People who mind that others are left without warm clothes, and a voice backing them completely.

Four four-letter words in a row, each with two letters buried at its centre; the sentence reads as a plea about the cold and hands over the same shout twice, once across and once down.

27 across FOUND

Discovered love in money (5)

Definition: Discovered · container

Discovered → FOUND: found is the past tense of find, so it matches the definition's tense as well as its sense of coming across something.

  • money → FUND a fund is a pot of money set aside
  • love → O love is the tennis score of nothing, written as a nought
  • in — 'In' places the letter before it inside the word that follows, not beside it.

Romance turning up where the money is.

Love is the tennis nought, no score at all, and the little story about finding romance in wealth is what keeps a single letter out of sight.

28 across OLIGARCH

Crude oil charge mostly makes wealthy and powerful individual (8)

Definition: wealthy and powerful individual · anagram + deletion

wealthy and powerful individual → OLIGARCH: an oligarch is one of the few who hold power, and in current usage a very rich businessman with political weight.

  • oil → OIL anagram fodder, the three letters of the word itself
  • charge → CHARG CHARGE with its final letter dropped
  • Crude — Crude oil is oil not yet refined, and letters handed over crude have not been put in their finished order either.
  • mostly — Most of a word is all of it bar the last letter, so the final one is left behind.

The price of crude oil making somebody enormously rich.

'Crude' does two jobs at once, describing the oil in the surface and ordering the shuffle in the wordplay, so nothing in the sentence looks like an anagram.

1 down SIDEREAL

Stellar team, one from Spain (8)

Definition: Stellar · charade

Stellar → SIDEREAL: sidereal means measured by the stars, as a sidereal day is one turn of the Earth against them, so both are adjectives of the stars.

  • team → SIDE a side is a team, the word every match report uses
  • one from Spain → REAL a real was a Spanish silver coin, so one of them is one from Spain

A brilliant team with a Spaniard among them.

'Team' beside 'one from Spain' looks like a nod at Real Madrid, which is the picture the setter wants standing in front of the old coin the wordplay is really after.

2 down ISLE

Man possibly barring a passage (4)

Definition: Man · deletion

Man → ISLE: the Isle of Man is one island among many, so this is definition by example — 'possibly' is the setter saying so.

  • passage → AISLE the passage between rows of seats in a church or a plane is an aisle
  • a → A the single letter A, the one thing barred from that word
  • possibly — 'Possibly' admits the name given is only one instance of the kind wanted, not a synonym for it.
  • barring — To bar a thing is to shut it out, so the letter named beside it is kept out of the word it came from.

A man planted in a corridor and refusing to let anybody past.

'Man' is the island in the Irish Sea rather than a person, and the opening capital gives nothing away because every clue starts with one.

3 down JEKYLL AND HYDE

Different types of KY jelly handed out (6,3,4)

Definition: Different types · anagram

Different types → JEKYLL AND HYDE: Stevenson's kindly doctor and his monstrous other self give the phrase for two utterly opposed characters in one person.

  • KY jelly handed → KYJELLYHANDED anagram fodder, thirteen letters
  • out → anagram indicator
  • out — Letters put out are out of the order they arrived in, which is the sense being borrowed from the phrase around it.

Assorted lubricants being given away at some promotional stand.

'Handed out' looks like the whole instruction and only 'out' is: 'handed' is fodder, which is why the letters of 'KY jelly' on their own leave you six short.

4 down STAUNCH

Firm stem (7)

Definition: Firm · double definition

Firm → STAUNCH: a staunch ally is unwavering, and to staunch a wound is to stem the flow — one spelling holding an adjective and a verb.

  • Firm → the adjective for an ally who does not waver, loyal through everything
  • stem → the verb for stopping a flow, what a bandage does to bleeding

A plant with a good sturdy stalk.

The two senses land in different parts of speech, an adjective for the loyal friend and a verb for stopping blood, which is how two words make a whole clue.

5 down AYES

One expression of approval or several (4)

Definition: several · charade

several → AYES: the ayes are the votes in favour called in a parliamentary division, so several approvals is precisely what the plural names.

  • One → A A is the crossword's one, the article standing in for a single thing
  • expression of approval → YES the plainest word of agreement there is

Somebody counting approval, one voice or a roomful of them.

The definition borrows the words in front of it: 'or several' means several of the approvals just described. In the Commons the ayes are the votes cast in favour of a motion.

6 down HULLABALOO

Commotion when a bear seen near city (10)

Definition: Commotion · charade

Commotion → HULLABALOO: the word is the noisy fuss a crowd makes, an uproar of voices rather than any disturbance in general.

  • city → HULL Hull is the port city on the Humber in the north-east of England
  • a → A the word is lifted from the surface exactly as it stands
  • bear → BALOO Baloo is the bear who teaches Mowgli in The Jungle Book
  • seen near — Things seen near one another are side by side, so these pieces are written one against the next with nothing between them.

A city in uproar the moment a bear is spotted on the edge of town.

'Bear' wants a name rather than a species — Baloo, of The Jungle Book — and the city is a real one, Hull, sitting in the clue as though it were scenery.

7 down OH DEAR

We’re disappointed when ostrich skin gets expensive (2,4)

Definition: We’re disappointed · charade + outer letters

We’re disappointed → OH DEAR: the exclamation is the mild noise of dismay English makes at bad news, so it says what the definition states flatly.

  • ostrich → OH the first and last letters of OSTRICH, the two the skin holds
  • expensive → DEAR dear means costly, still the everyday British word for a high price
  • skin — A skin is the covering on the outside, so what is kept here is only the letters at the two ends of the word.

The price of ostrich leather climbing, and somebody's face falling at the news.

The surface sells a report on the leather trade, and 'gets expensive' is not describing any skin at all — it hands over an ordinary word for costly.

13 down NOT AT ISSUE

Perhaps silk handkerchief is irrelevant (3,2,5)

Definition: irrelevant · charade

irrelevant → NOT AT ISSUE: a matter not at issue is one the argument does not turn on, so it has no bearing — both sit as adjectives after 'is'.

  • Perhaps silk → NOT A a hanky that might be silk is not the paper kind, and the wordplay opens with that denial
  • handkerchief → TISSUE the paper sort of handkerchief is a tissue

Somebody ruling that whether the handkerchief happens to be silk makes no difference.

Say what a silk hanky is not, then move the gaps between the words along one letter, and a phrase for something beside the point comes out.

15 down OPT

Choose Pinot? (3)

Definition: Choose · container

Choose → OPT: to opt is to pick between the things on offer, the verb that takes 'for' and means settling on one of them.

  • Pinot → OT the grape's name breaks into three scraps, P / in / ot, and this is the last of them
  • P → P the first of those scraps, one letter standing alone
  • Pinot — Read as an instruction instead of as a wine, this word is P in OT: the IN at its centre drops the letter before it inside the pair that follows.

A wine list, and somebody plumping for the Pinot.

The wine is not a synonym for anything here: its name reads as an instruction, P in OT. Setters call this a reverse clue, and the question mark is the warning.

19 down ENTRANCE

Cast a spell on the way in (8)

Definition: Cast a spell on · double definition

Cast a spell on → ENTRANCE: the verb means to delight as though by magic, and the noun, said with the stress moved, is the door you come in by.

  • Cast a spell on → the verb for holding somebody spellbound, stressed on its second syllable
  • the way in → the door or gateway you come in by, stressed on its first syllable

A magician bewitching the guests as they come through the door.

One set of letters said two ways — EN-trance the doorway, en-TRANCE the enchantment — which is why the clue can lay the two meanings side by side with no joint between them.

20 down UNCTION

Juliet exits crossing to get ointment (7)

Definition: ointment · deletion

ointment → UNCTION: unction is the oil used in anointing, as in the last rites' extreme unction, so it names the salve itself.

  • crossing → JUNCTION a crossing of roads is a junction
  • Juliet → J Juliet is J in the radio alphabet, and it is the letter that walks out
  • exits — Whatever exits leaves the place it was in, so the letter named goes out of the word around it.

Juliet stepping off the crossroads to fetch some ointment.

Juliet is the J of the radio alphabet, Alpha to Zulu, and 'crossing' is a meeting of roads rather than anything to do with a ship.

22 down ADRIFT

Commercial division is off course (6)

Definition: off course · charade

off course → ADRIFT: a boat adrift has no steerage and goes wherever the current takes it, which is precisely not where it was headed.

  • Commercial → AD the commercial between programmes is an ad, short for advertisement
  • division → RIFT a rift is a split, whether in a rock face or in a party

A company's sales department wandering away from the plan.

'Commercial division' reads as one department of a firm, and the wordplay wants the two words apart, the first of them in its television sense.

23 down BEAT IT

Dine in part and leave (4,2)

Definition: leave · container

leave → BEAT IT: the slang imperative means clear off, and both are verbs for going away, though only one of them is something you shout.

  • part → BIT a bit is a part of something, a piece broken off
  • Dine → EAT to dine is to eat, the plain word for it
  • in — 'In' puts the word before it inside the word after it rather than alongside, so one splits to let the other sit at its centre.

Told to eat a little of the meal and then go.

'Dine in' reads as one phrase about eating at home, and the wordplay takes the words apart, with the middle one saying where the first of them goes.
