Observer Everyman Crossword No 4,147

Across

1 across RASH

Unguarded hives? (4)

Definition: Unguarded · double definition

Unguarded is the reckless sense, acting before thinking; hives are nettle welts, so that half is definition by example of a skin eruption.

  • Unguarded → of a person or a remark: made without a thought for the consequences, with nothing held back in reserve
  • hives? → the itchy raised welts of urticaria, one particular outbreak on the skin — which is what the question mark admits

Two words, no joinery at all, and the two senses are miles apart: one is about somebody's character, the other about their skin.

4 across BALE

Announced legal collateral package (4)

Definition: package · homophone

package → BALE: a large bundle of hay, wool or cotton bound up for storage or transport, so it is one particular shape of parcel.

  • legal collateral → BALE the money a court holds as security for a defendant's return is bail
  • Announced — To announce a thing is to say it out loud, so the clue describes a sound and leaves the spelling for you to settle.

One sound, two spellings, and nothing connects them: the security a court takes and the bundle a farmer stacks share only their pronunciation.

8 across PELICAN CROSSING

Safe arrangement in which breeding comes with large bill? (7,8)

Definition: Safe arrangement · charade

Safe arrangement → PELICAN CROSSING: a light-controlled place to walk across a busy road, so the definition describes it by what it is there for.

  • large bill? → PELICAN the bird whose pouched beak is big enough to hold its own catch; the question mark allows that other birds have large bills too
  • breeding → CROSSING breeding one strain with another is crossing them, the word a stockman or a horticulturist uses

A pelican crossing is the British road crossing whose lights a pedestrian presses for, named from PEdestrian LIght CONtrolled — while the surface reads as a zoo's breeding programme.

11 across REGARDS

Compliments looks (7)

Definition: Compliments · double definition

Compliments are the good wishes sent at the end of a letter; looks is the verb for viewing or considering — one word covering two parts of speech.

  • Compliments → the plural noun signed at the foot of a letter — good wishes sent to somebody
  • looks → the verb instead: to look at a thing steadily, or to consider it in a certain light

Two words and no joinery, the hardest shape a clue can take: one half is a noun, the other a verb, and nothing whatever says where the definition stops.

12 across TORPEDO

Put an end to fuss about Republican games (7)

Definition: Put an end to · container

Put an end to → TORPEDO: the figurative sense of sinking a plan or a negotiation outright, borrowed from what the weapon does to a ship.

  • fuss → TODO a to-do is a fuss or a commotion, as in 'what a to-do'
  • Republican → R R is the letter printed beside a candidate's name on an American ballot
  • games → PE games at a British school is PE, the physical education lesson
  • about — About means round the outside of a thing, so the word beside it wraps itself round the two pieces that come after.

The wrapper opens after only two letters, which is why 'fuss' looks far too short to hold anything at all.

13 across AIR PISTOL

I will dance with pilots, mostly arm in arm (3,6)

Definition: arm · anagram + deletion

arm → AIR PISTOL: an arm is a weapon, so this is definition by example — one particular target-shooting sidearm standing for the general word.

  • I → I the pronoun taken as a single letter, the first thing thrown into the mix
  • pilots → PILOTS six more letters lifted straight from the clue
  • arm → AR the first of the clue's two limbs, with its last letter dropped
  • will dance — Dancing is movement that will not hold a line, so the letters gathered beside it end up somewhere other than where they started.
  • mostly — Mostly means all of it bar a little, and the little dropped is the final letter of the word standing next to it.

'Arm in arm' is one warm phrase in the surface and two separate jobs in the parse: the first arm supplies two letters, the second is the definition.

14 across GO RED

Stuck flush (2,3)

Definition: Stuck · double definition

Flush is the blushing sense, colour rising to the cheeks; stuck fits the same letters read as one word, what a bull's horn does to a matador.

  • Stuck → close the gap between the two words and you have the past tense of what a bull does with its horns — pierced, and so stuck
  • flush → to flush is what a face does when its owner is embarrassed, the colour rising into the cheeks

The enumeration promises two words and the second definition needs them; the first needs the space closed up. The letters are fixed, the gaps are the setter's to move.

15 across LET ON

Allow to board - and squeal (3,2)

Definition: Allow to board · double definition

One sense is letting a passenger climb aboard; the other is giving a secret away, which English keeps alive in the phrase 'don't let on'.

  • Allow to board → the conductor's sense: permitting a passenger up onto a bus or a train
  • squeal → to squeal is to give the game away, telling what you were meant to keep quiet about

The dash makes it look like a sentence interrupted, and the 'and' is the only joinery in the clue: two unrelated senses of the same two small words.

16 across GREAT DANE

Maybe Hans Christian Andersen, the dog! (5,4)

Definition: Maybe Hans Christian Andersen · double definition

One half is definition by example, a famous Dane of the first rank; the other is the breed of very large hound that goes by the same name.

  • Maybe Hans Christian Andersen → the writer of the fairy tales was born in Odense and is about as distinguished as his countrymen come; 'maybe' allows that others qualify
  • the dog! → the second reading is the breed: the huge smooth-coated hound that stands waist-high to its owner

The comma invites you to read the whole thing as one exclamation about a man, and the two words after it are quietly doing a job of their own.

19 across MOMBASA

A mambo's choreographed somewhere in Kenya (7)

Definition: somewhere in Kenya · anagram

somewhere in Kenya → MOMBASA: the country's second city and its main seaport, so a deliberately vague pointer is answered by naming one place.

  • A mambo's → AMAMBOS seven letters from the clue; the mambo is the Cuban ballroom dance, and the apostrophe-s gives the last of them
  • choreographed — A choreographer sets dancers moving into new positions, and the letters beside the word are moved about in exactly that way.

Mombasa is Kenya's old port on the Indian Ocean rather than its capital, and the clue says 'somewhere' precisely because it will not narrow the country down for you.

21 across ROE DEER

Uncapped pederero wounded woodland creatures (3,4)

Definition: woodland creatures · deletion + anagram

woodland creatures → ROE DEER: the small native deer of British woods, a name that does not change between one animal and a herd of them.

  • pederero → EDERERO a pederero was a small ship's cannon; the P at its front is the letter the cap takes away
  • Uncapped — A cap sits on top of a thing, so a word uncapped has lost the letter at its front; the sporting sense, never having played for one's country, belongs to the surface.
  • wounded — A wounded animal moves crookedly, and letters made to move crookedly do not arrive in the order they set off in.

Nobody knows the word 'pederero', and that is the point: an unfamiliar string of letters sitting in a clue is nearly always there to be rearranged.

22 across DUCKING THE ISSUE

Stuck inside - huge blunder - avoiding reality (7,3,5)

Definition: avoiding reality · anagram

avoiding reality → DUCKING THE ISSUE: to duck is to dodge, and the idiom means side-stepping the question that actually matters instead of facing it.

  • Stuck inside - huge → STUCKINSIDEHUGE fifteen letters for a fifteen-letter entry, three ordinary words picked for what they contain
  • blunder — To blunder about is to stumble around with no sense of direction, so the letters named before it finish up somewhere other than where they began.

'Blunder' wants to be the noun in a sentence about a mistake, and it is the verb. The dashes chop the clue into three so the fodder never looks like one block of letters.

23 across MEOW

Primarily, mog's exclamation (or wail!) (4)

Definition: Primarily, mog's exclamation (or wail!) · &lit + first letters

The whole clue also reads straight: a mog is a cat and this is the noise one makes, while the very same words hand over their initials.

  • mog's → M the opening letter; a mog is British slang for a cat
  • exclamation → E the second initial, from a word that says what sort of noise is coming
  • or → O even the little joining word gives up its initial
  • wail! → W the last initial, from a word for a long complaining cry
  • Primarily — Primarily means in the first place, so it asks for the opening letter of each word that follows, taken in the order they are printed.

Read the sentence again once the four letters are down: a cat's exclamation is exactly what you have written, which is what makes this wordplay and definition at once.

24 across TIDE

Season limited, did you say? (4)

Definition: Season · homophone

Season → TIDE: the archaic word for a season or a festival time, alive now only in Yuletide, Eastertide and the phrase 'time and tide'.

  • limited → TIDE something limited is tied — restricted, kept within bounds
  • did you say? — The question asks what was heard rather than what was written, so the entry is spelled one way and sounds like another word entirely.

'Season' looks like a red herring until you remember Yuletide and Whitsuntide: the old sense survives almost nowhere else in modern English.

2 down ACCORDION

Agreement: Everyman never to pick up musical instrument (9)

Definition: musical instrument · charade + reversal

musical instrument → ACCORDION: definition by example, the bellows-and-buttons squeezebox standing in for the whole category.

  • Agreement → ACCORD an accord is a formal agreement between parties, the word used of treaties
  • Everyman → I the setter signs these puzzles Everyman, and a setter speaking of himself is I
  • never → ON never is a flat refusal and so is no, which is the pair of letters that then travels upward
  • to pick up — In a down entry, picking a thing up carries it towards the top of the grid, so the short word beside it is written from its far end.

The colon makes 'Agreement' look like a heading announcing what follows. It is the first piece of the build, and the definition is the two words at the very end.

3 down HANDSET

Had sent off for phone component (7)

Definition: phone component · anagram

phone component → HANDSET: the part you lift and hold to your ear, so the definition names it as one piece of the telephone.

  • Had sent → HADSENT seven letters handed over whole, the past-perfect verb chosen only for what it contains
  • off — Milk that has gone off has turned, and letters told they have turned will not stay in the order the clue prints them.

'Had sent off' reads as posting a parcel, and that ordinary picture is what stops anybody counting the seven letters sitting in front of the definition.

4 down BORSTAL

Muscle collective commit crime - picked up here? (7)

Definition: here? · charade + reversal

here? → BORSTAL: the question mark owns up to a bare pointer, and what the sentence points at is the place a young offender was sent after arrest.

  • Muscle collective → LATS the lats are the latissimus dorsi, the broad pair of back muscles gym-goers name as a group
  • commit crime → ROB to rob is to commit one particular crime, taking property by stealth or by force
  • picked up — A down entry runs from the top, so anything picked up is read from the bottom upward — and in the surface the same phrase means arrested.

A borstal was the British institution for young offenders, abolished in 1982 and still the word for where a teenage criminal was sent. Arrest in the surface, a turnaround in the parse.

5 down LOSER

No-hoper, charlie abandoning one sealing deal (5)

Definition: No-hoper · deletion

No-hoper → LOSER: a person with no chance of winning, the noun for somebody who fails at things rather than one who has mislaid something.

  • one sealing deal → CLOSER the closer is the salesman brought in at the end to get the contract signed
  • charlie → C Charlie is C in the NATO alphabet, and it is the letter that goes
  • abandoning — To abandon a thing is to walk away leaving it behind, and what gets left behind here is the single letter named just before the word.

'Charlie' looks like a name in a story about a business deal, and the small c is the only sign the setter gives that it is one letter instead.

6 down SPARTA

A sneaky trick's backfiring somewhere in ancient Greece (6)

Definition: somewhere in ancient Greece · charade + reversal

somewhere in ancient Greece → SPARTA: the militaristic city-state that fought Athens in the Peloponnesian War, so a vague location is answered by naming one.

  • A → A the indefinite article taken as itself, one letter before anything else is done
  • sneaky trick's → TRAPS a trap is the sneaky trick you lay for somebody, and the apostrophe-s supplies a fifth letter
  • backfiring — A plan that backfires comes back on the person who laid it, so the pieces assembled so far are read from the far end instead.

The apostrophe does two jobs: in the surface it is 'is', holding the sentence together, and in the parse it is a plain S that the assembly cannot do without.

7 down AGE-OLD

Ancient earl sporting a medal (3-3)

Definition: Ancient · container

Ancient → AGE-OLD: both mean having existed for a very long time, though this one attaches to customs, questions and traditions rather than to ruins.

  • a medal → A GOLD the medal a winner takes is a gold, and the article in front of it is part of the material
  • earl → E E is the standard abbreviation for an earl, as printed in lists of the peerage
  • sporting — To sport a hat or a badge is to wear it where it shows, so the piece named next is worn inside the other one.

Peerage abbreviations are worth learning as a set — D for duke, M marquess, E earl, V viscount, B baron — because a title in a clue is usually just its letter.

9 down LOGARITHMIC

A microlight deployed in concerning operation (11)

Definition: concerning operation · anagram

concerning operation → LOGARITHMIC: taking a logarithm is a mathematical operation, so the adjective means belonging to that one rather than to surgery or war.

  • A microlight → AMICROLIGHT eleven letters straight from the clue; a microlight is the tiny one-person aircraft
  • deployed — Troops deployed are spread out into fresh positions, and letters moved into fresh positions are no longer in the order they were handed over in.

Eleven letters for an eleven-letter entry is the tell, and the aviation surface — a small aircraft sent up on a mission — is doing all the work of hiding them.

10 down ICEBREAKERS

They float topics to start conversation (11)

Definition: topics to start conversation · double definition

One half is the party sense, a topic that gets strangers talking; the other is the strengthened ship that shoulders a way through pack ice.

  • topics to start conversation → the opening question or silly game at a party, there to get strangers talking to each other
  • They float → read the first two words as ships instead: the reinforced vessels that shoulder a channel through frozen sea

Nothing punctuates the join, so the whole thing reads as one remark about a party; the two words at the front are a definition in their own right.

14 down GET BEHIND

Support benighted rogue (3,6)

Definition: Support · anagram

Support → GET BEHIND: to get behind a candidate or a campaign is to back it publicly, the idiom of pushing from the rear rather than leading.

  • benighted → BENIGHTED nine letters handed over whole; benighted means overtaken by darkness, or else ignorant
  • rogue — A rogue elephant has left the herd and does as it pleases, so the letters beside the word will not keep the arrangement they were given.

'Benighted rogue' reads as a description of one shady person, and that is what stops you seeing that one of the two words is only there to shuffle the other.

15 down LAMBDA

Greek character represented by naïve type, US lawyer (6)

Definition: Greek character · charade

Greek character → LAMBDA: the eleventh letter of the Greek alphabet, so 'character' means a written symbol rather than somebody's personality.

  • naïve type → LAMB an innocent led into something is a lamb, as in lamb to the slaughter
  • US lawyer → DA the DA is the district attorney, the state's prosecutor in an American court

'Greek character' is a person in the surface and a letter of the alphabet in the parse — the commonest swap in cryptics, and worth expecting every time.

16 down GLASGOW

City's wing exhausted; goal's unlikely (7)

Definition: City · outer letters + anagram

City → GLASGOW: definition by example, Scotland's largest city standing in for the general word, and the capital letter at the start of a clue signals nothing.

  • wing → WG the first and last letters of the word, everything inside having gone
  • goal's → GOALS five more letters, with the apostrophe counting for nothing
  • exhausted — An exhausted supply has had its middle used up, so only the letters standing at either end of the word are left.
  • unlikely — An unlikely story is one that does not hold together, and letters that do not hold together have to be put back in some other order.

The surface is a match report — a winger running out of steam, a goal that will not come — and every word of it is either letters or an instruction to move them.

17 down EARSHOT

Hearing sign they're talking about you? (7)

Definition: Hearing · charade

Hearing → EARSHOT: within earshot means within the distance at which a thing can be heard, so the word names the range of somebody's hearing.

  • they're talking about you? → EARS the old superstition says the pair on the sides of your head give it away when you are discussed behind your back
  • sign → HOT the sign in that superstition is a sudden burning glow, which in one word is what burning makes a thing

The whole clue leans on a folk belief rather than on any convention, and the question mark is the setter admitting as much.

18 down EARNED

Achieved, somewhat learnedly (6)

Definition: Achieved · hidden word

Achieved → EARNED: the sense of having got something by deserving it — a reputation, a rest, a place — rather than merely having reached it.

  • learnedly → EARNED six consecutive letters inside the long adverb, beginning one letter in from the front
  • somewhat — Somewhat means to a partial degree, so what is wanted is a part of the word that follows rather than the whole of it.

Only two words follow the comma and the hiding place is one of them: an adverb odd enough to have been chosen for its letters rather than its sense.

20 down ANISE

Aromatic slice of flan I served (5)

Definition: Aromatic · hidden word

Aromatic → ANISE: an aromatic is a plant or substance with a strong scent, and this is the liquorice-flavoured seed used in baking and drinks.

  • flan I served → ANISE five consecutive letters that run across both gaps, starting inside the first word
  • slice of — A slice is cut out of the middle of a thing, so a short run of letters is taken from the words that follow and the rest left alone.

Three short words of a dinner-party sentence, and the run crosses two spaces — which is what makes a hidden word laid across a phrase so hard to see.
