Across

1 across ANTELOPE

Animal bound on stake (8)

Definition: Animal · charade

Animal → ANTELOPE: the definition names the class and the answer is one member of it, a hoofed grazer of the African plains.

  • stake → ANTE the ante is the bet every player must put up before the cards are dealt
  • bound → LOPE to lope is to run in long easy bounds, the stride a wolf crosses open ground with
  • on — In an across clue a piece laid 'on' another is tacked against its end, so the word after it supplies the opening letters.

An animal tethered to a post.

'Bound' reads as tied fast, which is what the tethering picture needs, and the wordplay wants the other sense entirely: a long springing stride.

5 across ALASKA

Unfortunately, Kenya borders very cold terrain (6)

Definition: very cold terrain · charade + outer letters

very cold terrain → ALASKA: definition by example — the American far north is a byword for tundra, ice and permafrost.

  • Unfortunately → ALAS alas is the old-fashioned sigh you let out when something is unfortunate
  • Kenya → KA the first and last letters of the country's name, read in that order
  • borders — A country's borders are its outer edges, so what you take is the letter standing at each end of the name.

A lament that Kenya has freezing country next door.

Kenya sits on the equator, so the geography is nonsense the moment you check it — the setter's way of saying the country is here for its spelling.

9 across DEMOCRATS

Politicians cared most after reshuffle (9)

Definition: Politicians · anagram

Politicians → DEMOCRATS: members of one of the two US parties, the plural noun naming them as a class of politician.

  • cared most → CAREDMOST anagram fodder, nine letters, and the enumeration matches it exactly
  • after reshuffle — A reshuffle moves ministers into different jobs, and the letters change places in exactly the same way.

MPs who did the most caring get their reward in a cabinet reshuffle.

'Politicians' and 'reshuffle' pull towards Westminster, and the party the letters spell is American — the misdirection is geographical rather than lexical.

11 across NATAL

Manage intake files at centre dealing with birth (5)

Definition: dealing with birth · middle letters

dealing with birth → NATAL: the adjective from Latin natalis, used of anything to do with being born, as in prenatal and neonatal.

  • Manage → NA maNAge — the middle two of its six letters
  • intake → TA inTAke — the middle two of its six letters
  • files → L fiLes — the middle one of its five letters
  • at centre — It points at the letters standing in the middle of each word, and not at what those words mean.

Handling the admissions paperwork at a maternity unit.

'Centre' reads as a place — a records centre, a birth centre — and it means a position inside each word. The three donor words are pure office vocabulary, so nothing medical shows until the definition.

12 across BLAH BLAH BLAH

Boring language about heritage leaders keep repeating claptrap (4,4,4)

Definition: claptrap · charade + first letters

claptrap → BLAH BLAH BLAH: empty noise nobody needs to hear, the sound you make in place of words not worth saying.

  • Boring language about heritage → BLAH the heads of these four words, read in order
  • Boring language about heritage → BLAH the instruction hands the same four letters back a second time
  • Boring language about heritage → BLAH and a third time, which fills the last of the three four-letter words
  • leaders — The leaders of a run of words are the letters at their heads, taken in the order the words come.
  • keep repeating — It asks for the same short unit over and over, which the three equal words of the enumeration confirm.

Dull talk from heritage bosses who keep saying the same thing.

'About' is not doing its usual container job here — it is one of the four words being decapitated, and it is easy to miss because it reads as pure grammar.

15 across EDAM

Cheese made from 50% of used spam (4)

Definition: Cheese · charade + deletion

Cheese → EDAM: definition by example — the round Dutch cheese in its red wax coat is one cheese among many.

  • used → ED the back half of its four letters
  • spam → AM the back half of its four letters
  • 50% of — Half the letters of each word are wanted, and it is the back half that survives in both of them.

A cheese apparently produced out of leftover tinned meat.

Spam is the tinned pork of wartime rationing, and 'used spam' is deliberately revolting — the definition is the one clean word in the sentence.

16 across FREELOADER

One taking advantage of available heavy machinery (10)

Definition: One taking advantage · charade

One taking advantage → FREELOADER: someone who lives off other people's generosity and never pays their share.

  • available → FREE a free seat is an available one
  • heavy machinery → LOADER a loader is the site vehicle that scoops earth or rubble into a truck

Somebody exploiting the fact that a big digger is going spare.

'Free' in the wordplay means available, while the same four letters in the answer mean not paying — the setter uses the sense you do not need.

18 across UNDERSTATE

Don’t go overboard when reporting to nation (10)

Definition: Don’t go overboard · charade

Don’t go overboard → UNDERSTATE: to understate is to put a thing more mildly than the facts warrant, which is exactly not overdoing it.

  • reporting to → UNDER in a company, whoever reports to a manager sits under them on the chart
  • nation → STATE a sovereign state is a nation

Advice against exaggerating when you brief the country.

'Reporting to nation' reads as a broadcast to the public, and it is two separate pieces: the org-chart sense of reporting to somebody, then a plain synonym.

19 across TWIG

Get a bit of stick (4)

Definition: Get · double definition

'Get' → TWIG in the British slang sense of catching on — did you twig? — and a twig is literally a small piece of a stick.

  • Get → in the sense of catching on — 'now I get it' is a claim to have finally understood
  • a bit of stick → read literally: a small piece broken off a branch, not an instruction to take one letter

Someone on the receiving end of a bit of flak.

'A bit of' looks like the instruction to take a single letter out of 'stick', and it is meant literally: a small piece of one. Getting stick is British for being criticised.

21 across CONTINGENTLY

Military group extremely lively – depending on conditions (12)

Definition: depending on conditions · charade + outer letters

depending on conditions → CONTINGENTLY: the adverb from 'contingent', describing something that holds only if some other thing does.

  • Military group → CONTINGENT a contingent is a body of troops detached for one particular duty
  • lively → LY the outermost letter at each end of the word, in that order
  • extremely — At the extremes of the word beside it: the outermost letter at each end, with everything between them dropped.

A detachment of soldiers in unusually high spirits.

Two letters at the end of a twelve-letter answer are easy to overlook, and 'extremely' is the only word in the clue doing any instructing.

24 across A-GAME

Regular characters in gang war meet to exhibit best performance (1-4)

Definition: best performance · alternate letters

best performance → A-GAME: from sport, where bringing your A-game means playing at the very top of your ability.

  • gang war meet → AGAME start on the second letter and take every other one straight through all three words
  • Regular characters — Characters here are letters on the page, and taking them regularly means every second one, at a fixed step.

Rival gangs turning up for a fight they had arranged.

'Regular characters' reads as the usual crowd, the faces who always show up, and 'characters' is really the printer's sense: marks on a page.

25 across TOUCH WOOD

Seek money from club – and hope for good fortune (5,4)

Definition: hope for good fortune · charade

hope for good fortune → TOUCH WOOD: the words said, hand on something wooden, to stop you jinxing what you have just claimed.

  • Seek money from → TOUCH to touch someone for a tenner is to cadge it off them
  • club → WOOD a wood is a golf club with a bulky head, made of persimmon before metal took over

Cadging cash off a club and trusting to luck.

British speakers say 'touch wood' where Americans say 'knock on wood', both of them a charm against having tempted fate.

26 across ELDEST

Part of Sheffield estate that’s been around the longest (6)

Definition: that’s been around the longest · hidden word

that’s been around the longest → ELDEST: the superlative of old used of people, naming the one born before all the others.

  • Sheffield estate → ELDEST the letters run consecutively across the join between the two words, in the order given
  • Part of — Only a part of what follows is wanted: a run of letters sitting inside it, rather than the sense of the words.

A district of a Sheffield estate that has stood longer than the rest.

Sheffield estates are real places, so the surface reads as local history and never suggests that only six of those letters are wanted.

27 across EDITS OUT

Removes text suited to getting misspelt (5,3)

Definition: Removes text · anagram

Removes text → EDITS OUT: to edit something out is to cut it from a piece of writing or film so it never reaches the audience.

  • suited to → SUITEDTO anagram fodder, eight letters for an eight-letter entry
  • getting misspelt — A word that comes out misspelt has its letters in the wrong places, which is the licence to move them.

Cutting the words that always end up spelled wrong.

'Suited to' looks like a link phrase joining a definition to its wordplay, and it is the raw material itself — the eight letters you need.

Down

1 down AIDA

Opera is a concept lacking energy (4)

Definition: Opera · charade + deletion

Opera → AIDA: definition by example — Verdi's Egyptian tragedy is one opera among hundreds, and the wordplay picks which.

  • a → A the sentence's own indefinite article, taken at face value as a single letter
  • concept → IDEA a concept is an idea — four letters, one of which is about to go
  • energy → E E is the physics symbol for energy, and E is the letter that leaves the word beside it
  • lacking — Something that lacks a thing is simply missing it, so the letter named next is absent from the word before.

An opera dismissed as a listless notion with no drive behind it.

The lone A hides in plain sight as the article of 'a concept', which is what makes the split so hard to see; E for energy is the standard physics symbol.

2 down TOME

Book is closed up as far as I’m concerned (4)

Definition: Book · charade

Book → TOME: a tome is a book, specifically a heavy scholarly one, so the everyday word defines the weightier one.

  • as far as → TO 'as far as' marks how far something extends, and 'to' is the preposition English uses for that limit
  • I’m concerned → ME the speaker in the form used after a preposition — a matter that concerns me
  • closed up — Closing a gap up means the two short words are written with no space left between them.

Someone declaring a matter closed, the book shut so far as they care.

'Book is closed' invites you to read the first three words as one phrase, and that is where the split hides: the definition stops at the first word.

3 down LOCALE

Position that sounds more healthy? (6)

Definition: Position · homophone

Position → LOCALE: a locale is the place where something is set or happens, matching 'position' in its plain noun sense of where a thing sits.

  • more healthy → LOCALE food sold as low-cal has had the calories cut, the healthier option on the shelf
  • that sounds — 'Sounds' hands you the noise instead of the spelling: say the healthier phrase aloud and write down what your ear gets.

A spot being recommended as the healthier choice.

Low-cal is supermarket shorthand for low-calorie, and said quickly it comes out as two syllables with the stress on the second — which is where the spelling parts company with the sound.

4 down PLAY HARD TO GET

Rare drama showing approach to attracting lover (4,4,2,3)

Definition: approach to attracting lover · charade

approach to attracting lover → PLAY HARD TO GET: the courtship tactic of feigning indifference so the other person does the pursuing.

  • drama → PLAY a drama staged in a theatre is a play
  • Rare → HARDTOGET what is rare is hard to get hold of — the phrase is a definition of scarcity

An unusual drama about how to win a lover round.

'Rare drama' reads as one phrase, an unusual piece of theatre, and it is two separate definitions clued in the opposite order to the one the answer runs in.

6 down LUNCHBOX

Food fight shows one’s masculine quality perhaps (8)

Definition: one’s masculine quality perhaps · charade

one’s masculine quality perhaps → LUNCHBOX: British tabloid slang for the visible bulge of a man's genitals.

  • Food → LUNCH the midday meal, food by name
  • fight → BOX to box is to fight with gloves on inside a ring

A food fight that reveals something manly about one of the throwers.

British tabloids adopted LUNCHBOX for a man's bulge after Linford Christie's sprinting Lycra made the front pages in 1993, which is why 'perhaps' is there: it flags slang rather than a dictionary sense.

7 down SETTLE DOWN

Pay low? Try to be calm about it (6,4)

Definition: Try to be calm · charade

Try to be calm → SETTLE DOWN: what you say to someone worked up — to settle down is to stop agitating and go quiet.

  • Pay → SETTLE you settle a bill when you pay off what you owe
  • low → DOWN down is the direction of low, and someone feeling low is feeling down
  • about it → surface only, no letters: it makes the sentence sound like advice to someone stewing over a grievance

One person urging another to stay calm about a poor wage.

The question mark after 'Pay low?' admits those two words define nothing between them: each is a synonym in its own right, laid end to end.

8 down ALL THE RAGE

Sensible to acquire silver – it’s quite fashionable (3,3,4)

Definition: quite fashionable · container

quite fashionable → ALL THE RAGE: what everybody wants at the moment, 'rage' carrying its older sense of a craze.

  • Sensible → ALLTHERE someone all there is in full possession of their wits — the phrase survives mostly in the negative, 'not all there'
  • silver → AG Ag is the chemical symbol for silver, from the Latin argentum
  • acquire — To acquire something is to take it in and keep it, so the phrase in front closes around the metal.

A tip on the metals market: buying silver is the smart move just now.

The clue reads as investment advice, with the last three words as the reason to buy. 'Rage' is the old sense of a passing craze, not anger.

10 down SILVER-TONGUED

Posh cutlery licked? That’s charming! (6-7)

Definition: charming · charade

charming → SILVER-TONGUED: a silver-tongued talker persuades by smooth, flattering speech, which is 'charming' in the sense of winning people round.

  • Posh cutlery → SILVER the good cutlery kept for guests, named after the metal it is made of
  • licked → TONGUED to tongue something is to put your tongue to it, which is what licking is

Someone has licked the best cutlery, which is hardly delightful.

'That's charming!' is what a British speaker says about something disgusting, so the sarcastic reading and the straight one sit on top of each other; the clue means the straight one.

13 down DENUNCIATE

Condemn deceit a nun disseminated (10)

Definition: Condemn · anagram

Condemn → DENUNCIATE: a rare verb meaning to denounce publicly, so to denunciate a thing is to condemn it out loud.

  • deceit a nun → DECEITANUN anagram fodder, ten letters, the full length of the entry
  • disseminated — To disseminate is to scatter seed broadly, and letters scattered that way come down in new places.

A nun caught spreading a lie, and somebody condemning her for it.

'A nun' looks like part of the story rather than part of the fodder, which is what keeps ten letters sitting in plain sight. The answer is the rarer sibling of 'denounce'.

14 down HAND IN HAND

Two workers around home being co-operative (4,2,4)

Definition: co-operative · container

co-operative → HAND IN HAND: two people going hand in hand are working in close partnership, the sense of co-operative that means acting together.

  • Two workers → HAND a hand is a worker — a farm hand, all hands on deck; this is the first of the two
  • home → IN someone at home is in, as in 'is she in?'
  • Two workers → HAND the second of the pair the clue asked for
  • around — It sets the pair on either side of what follows, so the short word ends up enclosed between them.

Two labourers getting on with a job around the house.

'Worker' is one of the crossword's stock words — usually ANT, sometimes HAND — and here it is wanted twice, which the enumeration's matching outer fours confirm.

17 down TRAILERS

Do they get dragged into showing what’s in store? (8)

Definition: Do they get dragged into showing what’s in store? · cryptic definition

Both senses land on TRAILERS: a cinema trailer shows the films in store, and a road trailer is a thing dragged along behind.

  • Do they get dragged → the surface asks about being hauled along behind a vehicle, which one sense of these things is exactly
  • showing what’s in store → 'in store' is what is coming rather than what is on the shelves: these are shown before the main feature to advertise the next one

Objects being hauled about to advertise what a shop has in stock.

Both senses are towed along together: the box on a tow bar and the two minutes of clips before the film. 'In store' is the pivot, reading as shop stock and meaning what is coming up.

20 down OLD HAT

Hot lad running amok – it’s somewhat passé (3,3)

Definition: somewhat passé · anagram

somewhat passé → OLD HAT: something old hat has been around so long it has gone stale, which is what passé says of a fashion.

  • Hot lad → HOTLAD anagram fodder, six letters for a six-letter entry
  • running amok — Someone running amok charges about with no order at all, and the letters end up wherever that leaves them.

A good-looking young man rampaging, which is nothing new.

The dash does the work of a full stop: everything before it is letters, everything after it is meaning.

22 down LOCO

Strange facilities storing carbon (4)

Definition: Strange · container

Strange → LOCO: Spanish for mad, borrowed into English slang, so 'strange' in the sense of behaving unhinged.

  • facilities → LOO 'the facilities' is the polite British way of asking where the lavatory is — the loo
  • carbon → C C is the chemical symbol for carbon
  • storing — To store something is to put it away inside something else, so the letter goes within the word in front.

Peculiar washrooms being used to keep carbon in.

'Facilities' is hotel-brochure politeness for the toilet, and the plural is there for the surface alone: the word it stands for is singular.

23 down ADIT

Passage in financial review union dismissed (4)

Definition: Passage · deletion

Passage → ADIT: the horizontal tunnel driven into a hillside to reach a mine, a passage in the most literal sense.

  • financial review → AUDIT an audit is the formal inspection of a company's accounts
  • union → U U is the standing abbreviation for union, as in trade union, and it is the letter sent away
  • dismissed — A worker who is dismissed is sent away, so the letter it points at leaves the word it was in.

A corridor mentioned in an audit once the union had been shown the door.

'Passage in financial review' looks like the opening of a hidden word, which is the wrong path: the review is a synonym, and the union is one letter coming out of it.
