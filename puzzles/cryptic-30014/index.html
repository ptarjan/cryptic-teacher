1 across JACKDAW

Jewel thief raised feeling of dread, did you say? (7)

Definition: Jewel thief · homophone

Jewel thief → JACKDAW: the bird has a reputation for stealing glittering objects for its nest, so the human crime is pinned on it.

  • raised → JACKED to jack something up is to raise it
  • feeling of dread → AWE awe is the mixture of wonder and dread that something overwhelming produces
  • did you say? → JACKDAW said as one, the two words fall across a different join and come out as seven letters
  • did you say? — Asking whether you said it puts the wordplay in your mouth: what counts is the noise two words make run together, not how either is spelt.

Somebody checking they heard right about a jewel thief who left everyone frightened.

The bird is famous for carrying off anything bright and shiny, which is what lets a species answer to a charge of theft. Nothing is spelt out here; the whole clue is a noise.

5 across STUFFED

Taken to the cleaners, as are duvets and mattresses (7)

Definition: Taken to the cleaners · double definition

Taken to the cleaners means thoroughly beaten or robbed; duvets and mattresses are stuffed in the literal sense, packed full of filling.

  • Taken to the cleaners → the idiom for being comprehensively beaten, cheated or fleeced
  • as are duvets and mattresses → both are packed full of filling, which is the literal sense of the word

Bedding on its way to the laundry.

'Taken to the cleaners' is idiom rather than laundry — it means comprehensively beaten or fleeced — and the duvets are there to make the phrase sound like a delivery note.

9 across REINSTATE

Take back control, say (9)

Definition: Take back · charade

Take back → REINSTATE: taking back a sacked employee or a scrapped rule is putting it in place again, the verb for restoring someone to a post.

  • control → REIN to rein something is to control it, from the strap that steers a horse
  • say → STATE to state something is to say it

A political slogan about taking back control.

'Take back control' was a referendum slogan, which is what makes the three words read as one phrase; the definition stops after two of them and the rest is wordplay.

10 across DROID

Robot managed corners by contrast in reverse (5)

Definition: Robot · container + reversal

Robot → DROID: the Star Wars clipping of android, now the general word for a humanoid robot in and out of the films.

  • managed → DID managed to do it comes to the same thing as did it
  • by contrast → OR the word that offers the alternative, the one you reach for when setting one thing against another
  • corners — To corner someone is to trap them with no way past, so this word closes round what it has caught.
  • in reverse — 'In reverse' means read the other way about, and this is an across entry, so those letters arrive right to left.

A machine that took the bends better going backwards.

'In reverse' looks as though it belongs to the driving, and it is an instruction; what gets turned round is a two-letter word, which is why so little seems to happen.

11 across YANG

Force of a male sovereign a youth repelled to some extent (4)

Definition: Force of a male · hidden word + reversal

Force of a male → YANG: in Chinese philosophy this is the masculine, active principle, set against the feminine yin.

  • sovereign a youth → GNAY four letters in a row straddle the two gaps between these words
  • repelled → reversal indicator: that run is read back the other way
  • repelled — Something repelled has been driven back the way it came, so the letters run right to left along this across entry.
  • to some extent — 'To some extent' says you want only part of what is in front of you, and the part wanted is a run of letters inside the words themselves.

A young man fending off the power of a king.

Yin's opposite is the male, bright and active principle of Chinese thought, which is what the definition is describing rather than any king's authority.

12 across ESPADRILLE

Primate in Renault kicking out first of churchgoers, one of those going on foot (10)

Definition: one of those going on foot · container + deletion + first letter

one of those going on foot → ESPADRILLE: the canvas shoe with a plaited rope sole, so what goes on foot is a shoe rather than a walker.

  • Renault → ESPACE the Espace is Renault's people carrier, a name the marque has used for forty years
  • first of churchgoers → the C that word opens with is the letter kicked out, leaving five behind
  • Primate → DRILL the drill is a short-tailed West African monkey, a close relative of the mandrill
  • in — 'In' is the placement instruction: the primate is not beside the car but inside it, and so are its letters.
  • kicking out — Kicking something out throws it off the premises, and the letter named next is thrown out of the word it was sitting in.
  • first of — 'First of' takes the single letter a word opens with and leaves the whole of the rest of it behind.

A monkey riding in a car and shoving the first of the congregation out of it.

'Primate' points hard at an archbishop in a clue already full of churchgoers, and the wordplay wants the monkey instead.

14 across VIRAGO

Maiden visited by a dragon (6)

Definition: dragon · container

dragon → VIRAGO: both name a fierce, overbearing woman, the answer being the formal literary word where 'dragon' is the everyday insult.

  • Maiden → VIRGO the Maiden is the zodiac sign, the constellation drawn as a young woman
  • a → A the article itself, dropped in whole
  • visited by — A visitor comes inside the house rather than standing at the gate, so what visits goes in among the letters instead of beside them.

A young woman receiving a visit from a dragon.

'Dragon' is the definition and it is not the beast: a dragon is a fierce and domineering woman, which is what lets the fairy-tale surface run so smoothly.

15 across LEANT ON

Modest weight needed for support (5,2)

Definition: support · charade

support → LEANT ON: to lean on a thing is to rest your weight against it and let it hold you up, which is taking support from it.

  • Modest → LEAN lean means spare or meagre, as in lean times — a modest amount of something
  • weight → TON a ton is a unit of weight

A small weight is all it takes to prop the thing up.

The enumeration is the whole difficulty: two ordinary words go in and the answer splits the same letters one place further along, into two quite different ones.

16 across POINTER

Clue for dog (7)

Definition: Clue · double definition

A pointer is a hint that points you towards the answer, and a pointer is the gun dog that freezes with its nose aimed at the game.

  • Clue → a hint that points you towards the thing you are hunting for
  • dog → the gun dog that stands rigid with its nose aimed at hidden game

A hint towards which breed is meant.

Three clues in this grid read 'clue for dog' with a different word in front, and each answer is a breed that doubles as somebody's part in the crossword.

18 across SETTER

Writer of clue for dog (6)

Definition: Writer of clue · double definition

The setter is the person who writes the clues in a crossword, and a setter is also a breed of gun dog trained to freeze when it finds birds.

  • Writer of clue → the trade name for whoever composes a crossword, used by the papers themselves
  • dog → the long-haired gun dog that drops to a crouch when it scents birds

Whoever wrote a clue about a dog.

Paul is naming himself here: the word for whoever composes a crossword is also the long-haired gun dog, Irish or English or Gordon, that stands stock still on a scent.

20 across BLOODHOUND

Solver of clue for dog (10)

Definition: Solver of clue · double definition

A bloodhound is the tracking dog with the celebrated nose, and the word is used of any dogged investigator who follows a trail to the end.

  • Solver of clue → the dogged tracker of a person, who follows a trail until it gives out
  • dog → the breed with the drooping ears and the celebrated nose, used to follow a scent

Whoever finally works a clue about a dog out.

Third of the trio — clue, writer of clue, solver of clue — and the longest breed of the three, the one police forces use to follow a scent across country.

21 across PAWN

Poodle, smut by one’s ears? (4)

Definition: Poodle · homophone

Poodle → PAWN: a poodle is somebody's obedient creature and a pawn is the piece a stronger player pushes about, so both are another's instrument.

  • smut → PAWN smut is porn, and in an English accent that drops its r that word is exactly the noise wanted here
  • by one’s ears? — Taking a word by the ears is judging it by the noise it makes, so what gets written down matches the sound and not the spelling.

A poodle with something filthy right by its ears.

'Poodle' is the dog theme's decoy: it means somebody's lapdog in the political sense, which is what got Tony Blair called George Bush's poodle.

24 across IAMBI

What giant might say, though shorter – a few feet? (5)

Definition: a few feet? · deletion

a few feet? → IAMBI: an iamb is a metrical foot in poetry, so a few of them are feet in the prosodic sense rather than the bodily one.

  • What giant might say → IAMBIG the plainest boast a giant could make about himself, run together as six letters
  • though shorter → one letter comes off the end of that boast
  • though shorter — Being shorter than what the giant actually said means the boast arrives with its final letter clipped off.

A giant announcing his size, only not at full length.

Feet are the units a line of verse is measured in, and the answer is the Latin plural of the commonest one — the te-TUM beat of 'again' or 'delay'.

25 across SEDITIOUS

Rabble-rousing idiots use bombs (9)

Definition: Rabble-rousing · anagram

Rabble-rousing → SEDITIOUS: both are adjectives for speech that stirs people up against the authorities, the answer being the word the law uses.

  • idiots use → IDIOTSUSE anagram fodder, nine letters
  • bombs → anagram indicator
  • bombs — A show that bombs falls apart in front of the audience, and letters that fall apart do not land in the order they started in.

Fools with explosives, stirring up trouble.

'Bombs' sits at the end of the clue where a solver expects the definition, and it is the instruction; the definition is the very first word instead.

26 across HONITON

On passing through, come across Devon town (7)

Definition: Devon town · container

Devon town → HONITON: a real market town in east Devon, so the definition names the class and the answer supplies one instance of it.

  • come across → HITON to hit on an idea is to come across it
  • On → ON the clue's first word goes in as it stands, both its letters
  • passing through — Whatever passes through a place goes in one side and out the other, so this piece ends up inside the letters rather than alongside them.

Stopping off in a Devon town while travelling through.

Honiton is the small market town in east Devon on the old road to Exeter, known for two centuries for its handmade lace.

27 across ELDERLY

Wrinkled – in the manner of a tree? (7)

Definition: Wrinkled · charade

Wrinkled → ELDERLY: not a synonym but the sign of the thing, since lined skin is what the polite adjective for old is pointing at here.

  • a tree → ELDER the elder is the hedgerow tree that gives us elderflower and elderberry
  • in the manner of → LY -ly is the ending English tacks on to mean in the manner of, as in slowly or kindly

Something creased like the bark of a tree.

The clue reads back to front: the ending is named first and the tree second, so the assembly runs the opposite way to the sentence you are given.

1 down JERKY

Marked by convulsions, that must be cured (5)

Definition: Marked by convulsions · double definition

Jerky movement is a series of jerks, and jerky the food is meat that has to be cured and dried before it will keep.

  • Marked by convulsions → the adjective for movement that arrives in sudden uncontrolled tugs
  • that must be cured → strips of meat salted and dried until they will keep without a fridge

A twitchy patient whose condition needs treating.

'Cured' is the hinge: it means treated by a doctor in the surface and salted and dried in the answer, which is what turns strips of beef into a snack.

2 down CHIANTI

True self critical of drink (7)

Definition: drink · charade

drink → CHIANTI: the dry red wine of Tuscany, so the definition names the class and the answer supplies one example of it.

  • True self → CHI chi is the life force of Chinese philosophy, the energy held to be a person's innermost self
  • critical of → ANTI anti means set against something, which is what being critical of it comes to

Someone being true to themselves and disapproving of the drinking.

Chi is worth banking: crosswords use those three letters for life force, spirit or inner energy whenever a clue turns mystical. The definition is the last word alone.

3 down DASH

Shatter bolt (4)

Definition: Shatter · double definition

To dash something is to smash it — dashed hopes, dashed to pieces — and to dash is also to bolt, to leave at speed.

  • Shatter → to smash a thing to pieces, the verb used of hopes that come to nothing
  • bolt → the verb rather than the door fitting: to run off suddenly

A bolt being smashed apart.

'Bolt' looks like the noun on a door, which is what makes the two words read as one instruction; the answer needs it as a verb, to run for it.

4 down WHAT'S YOUR POISON

How would you like to go – for a drink? (5,4,6)

Definition: How would you like to go – for a drink? · cryptic definition

The phrase is a barman's dated way of asking what to pour, and it takes 'poison' literally enough to double as a question about dying.

  • How would you like to go → The surface offers this as a question about travel arrangements; the setter means it as a question about how you would choose to die.
  • for a drink? → The second reading is the one heard across a bar, where the same question is only asking which bottle to reach for.

One friend asking another how they fancy getting to the pub.

The dash is where the clue changes register: everything before it belongs to an execution and everything after it to a night out, and the phrase serves both.

5 down SEE NAPLES AND DIE

If it’s the last thing you do, visit the home of pizza: need pasta in Leeds? No time for it, unfortunately (3,6,3,3)

Definition: If it’s the last thing you do, visit the home of pizza · anagram + deletion

The proverb tells you to see the bay before you die, and the definition gives the same instruction the other way round, as a last request.

  • need pasta in Leeds → NEEDPASTAINLEEDS anagram fodder, sixteen letters
  • No time for it → time is T, and this one is unwanted, so sixteen letters come down to fifteen
  • unfortunately → anagram indicator
  • No time for it — Having no time for something is refusing to give it house room, and time is T, so that letter is refused a place among the rest.
  • unfortunately — An unfortunate arrangement is one that has gone wrong, so the letters it governs are not lying in the order they were handed over in.

Advice to go to Italy, and a grumble that there is no time to hunt down pasta in Leeds.

The saying is an old traveller's boast about the bay: see it once and nothing afterwards can matter. 'The home of pizza' is the city itself rather than the country.

6 down UNDERPARTS

Errant spud festering in lunchbox, say? (10)

Definition: lunchbox, say? · anagram

lunchbox, say? → UNDERPARTS: the decorous term for the body's lower regions, of which the slang word is one cheerfully rude example.

  • Errant spud → ERRANTSPUD anagram fodder, ten letters
  • festering → anagram indicator
  • festering — Something festering is rotting where it lies, and letters left to rot do not keep the order they were given in.

A stray potato going bad at the bottom of somebody's packed lunch.

'Lunchbox' is British tabloid slang for a man's genitals, coined at the sprinter Linford Christie's expense in the 1990s and never quite retired since.

7 down FROGLET

Small jumper back on top of littl’un, purchase jackets (7)

Definition: Small jumper · charade + container + first letter

Small jumper → FROGLET: the -let ending makes it a young frog, and a frog is the standard example of a creature that gets about by jumping.

  • back → FRO to and fro: fro is the old word for back, and it survives nowhere else
  • purchase → GET to purchase something is to get it
  • littl’un → L the first letter of that word is all 'top of' asks for
  • on top of — In a down entry these words say one piece sits above another, and the very same words hand over the top letter of the word behind them.
  • jackets — To jacket a thing is to put a coat round it, so this word closes over what sits between its two halves.

A small pullover worn back to front over a little one, with coats bought to go over the top.

'Jumper' is a woolly garment all through the surface and a thing that jumps in the definition, and that is the whole distance between the clothes shop and the pond.

8 down DUDGEON

Resentment in chap on bottling up grievances, initially (7)

Definition: Resentment · charade + container + first letter

Resentment → DUDGEON: the offended, indignant sort of resentment, a noun that now travels almost solely in the phrase 'in high dudgeon'.

  • chap → DUDE a dude is a chap
  • on → ON the little word is taken at face value and tacked straight on the end
  • grievances → G the letter this word begins with, nothing looked up
  • bottling up — Bottling something up keeps it shut inside, which is where the letter goes — in among what has already been put together.
  • initially — 'Initially' asks for the state a word is in at the start, which on the page comes down to its opening letter.

A man who swallows his complaints from the very beginning.

'In high dudgeon' is the only phrase this noun survives in, so the definition is a word most solvers have met a hundred times without ever meeting it alone.

13 down CAST ADRIFT

Said craft at sea, lost ultimately – like that? (4,6)

Definition: Said craft at sea, lost ultimately – like that? · &lit + anagram + last letter

The whole clue reads straight — a craft at sea and finally lost is in exactly this condition — so the sentence defines the state it also spells.

  • Said craft → SAIDCRAFT anagram fodder, nine letters
  • lost ultimately → T the last letter of losT joins the nine
  • at sea → anagram indicator
  • at sea — Being at sea is being all over the place with no bearings, which is exactly the state the letters in front of it have been left in.
  • ultimately — 'Ultimately' means in the end, so what is wanted from the word before it is the letter standing at its end.

A boat reported missing somewhere out on the water.

Every word does both jobs: the sentence describes a vessel that has drifted away and the same words hand over the letters, which is what 'like that?' points back at.

16 down PUBLISH

Print error not quite covered up by press (7)

Definition: Print · container + deletion

Print → PUBLISH: both are verbs for putting words out into the world, though a publisher issues the book while a printer only manufactures it.

  • press → PUSH to press a thing is to push it
  • error not quite → BLI a blip is a small error, and it turns up here a letter short
  • not quite — 'Not quite' the whole word stops just short of the end of it, so the final letter never arrives.
  • covered up by — Being covered up by something means having it lie right over you, so the outer word closes round these letters.

A printing mistake hushed up by the newspapers.

'Press' is the newspaper trade all through the surface and the plain verb to push in the wordplay, which is what makes one sentence out of two halves.

17 down IRON MAN

Female film? (4,3)

Definition: film? · charade

film? → IRON MAN: the 2008 Marvel film, so the definition names the class and the question mark admits this is one film among thousands.

  • Female → IRON the first two letters of that word are Fe, the chemical symbol for iron, taken from Latin ferrum
  • Female → MAN what is left of the word once those two letters go is male, and a male is a man

A film with a woman at the heart of it.

The wordplay is one word long. Split 'Female' after two letters and a chemical symbol stands next to an ordinary noun, which is why the enumeration (4,3) looks impossible at first.

19 down ENAMOUR

Cast a spell on an item of ‘ardware, we ‘ear? (7)

Definition: Cast a spell on · homophone

Cast a spell on → ENAMOUR: to enamour someone is to make them fall in love, which is charming them in the older, magical sense of that verb.

  • an → AN the article stays exactly as the clue prints it
  • item of ‘ardware → AMMER hardware runs to hammers, and the setter's apostrophe has already knocked the aitch off this one
  • we ‘ear? → ENAMOUR said as one, the article and the tool run together into seven quite different letters
  • we ‘ear? — Hearing is the whole point, and the dropped aitch tells you which accent to hear it in before you write anything down.

A spell being cast over a piece of hardware, by all accounts.

The dropped aitches are printed rather than left to you: 'ardware is the setter putting the accent on the page, and the sound is the only route from tools to romance.

22 down NASTY

Vile family missing daddy on vacation at first (5)

Definition: Vile · deletion + outer letters

Vile → NASTY: both are plain adjectives for something disgusting or spiteful, the answer being the everyday word where 'vile' is the stronger one.

  • family → DYNASTY a dynasty is a ruling family, one generation handing on to the next
  • daddy on vacation → DY empty that word out and its first and last letters are left holding the ends
  • missing — What is missing has been taken away, so the letters named next are the ones the longer word has to do without.
  • on vacation — A word on vacation has been emptied out: everyone has left the middle and only the letters at the two ends are still standing there.
  • at first — 'At first' says where the loss falls — at the front of the word rather than anywhere else along it.

A vile family away on holiday with the father not there.

'On vacation' is a hard-working crossword idiom — it empties a word rather than sending it away — and what it empties out here is what then has to go missing.

23 down STUD

Beefcake learning to wipe bottom (4)

Definition: Beefcake · deletion

Beefcake → STUD: both are slang for a conspicuously muscular and attractive man, the answer borrowed from the horse kept for breeding.

  • learning → STUDY learning is study, the noun for what a student puts in
  • bottom → the letter at the foot of that word is the Y, and it is the one wiped away
  • to wipe — To wipe something is to rub it out altogether, so the letter pointed at is deleted rather than shifted elsewhere.
  • bottom — This is a down entry, so the bottom of the word is the letter standing at the foot of the column.

A hunk learning to clean up after himself.

'Beefcake' is 1950s magazine slang for photographs of muscular men, the male answer to cheesecake, and it long ago came to mean the man himself.
