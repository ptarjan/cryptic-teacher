Across

1 across RUMBLE

Complain when government’s head departs – it means trouble (6)

Definition: trouble · deletion

trouble → RUMBLE: in American street slang a rumble is a gang fight, so the word names one particular brand of trouble.

  • Complain → GRUMBLE to grumble is to complain, muttering on about it
  • government’s head → G the head of a word is its opening letter, so this is an operation the clue asks for rather than a shortening to look up
  • departs — Someone who departs walks out and is gone, so the letter named just before it goes missing from the word it was standing at the front of.

Somebody gripes the moment the person in charge walks out, and it can only end badly.

A rumble is a pitched gang fight, American street slang from the days of West Side Story, which is what lets a single noun stand for 'trouble'.

5 across FAR RIGHT

Extremists get a republican involved in terror (3,5)

Definition: Extremists · container

Extremists → FAR RIGHT: the far right is the extreme end of the political spectrum, so the phrase names the people holding those positions.

  • terror → FRIGHT a fright is a jolt of terror; the word opens up after its first letter to let two more in
  • a → A the indefinite article, taken straight off the page
  • republican → R R = republican, the letter printed beside the party's candidates on an American ballot
  • involved in — To be involved in something is to be caught up inside it, so the letters named first end up buried within the word named last.

Extremists rope a Republican into a campaign of political violence.

'Terror' is the pivot: a news-page noun on the surface, a plain synonym for fright underneath, and the whole misdirection rests on which of the two you read first.

9 across SOJOURNS

Day in France is part of boy’s brief trips (8)

Definition: brief trips · container

brief trips → SOJOURNS: a sojourn is a short stay somewhere away from home, so the plural covers a series of such visits.

  • boy’s → SONS a boy is a son, and the apostrophe-s counts as a plain fourth letter; the word then splits open after its first two
  • Day in France → JOUR jour is French for day, the second half of bonjour
  • part of — A part of something sits within the whole, so the letters just named are placed inside the word that follows.

A day spent in France counts as one of a lad's short journeys.

'Brief' looks like an instruction to shorten something, which is the trap; it belongs to the definition, and nothing in this clue is abbreviated.

10 across ANNEAL

Toughen up old queen by adding additional edges (6)

Definition: Toughen up · charade + outer letters

Toughen up → ANNEAL: annealing heats metal or glass and cools it slowly so internal stresses relax, leaving the material tougher and less brittle.

  • old queen → ANNE Anne reigned from 1702 to 1714, the last of the Stuart monarchs
  • additional → AL the first and last letters of this word — an operation the clue asks for, not a standing abbreviation
  • edges — The edges of a written word are the two letters standing at either end of it, and everything between them is left behind.

Instructions for strengthening an elderly monarch by fitting her with extra trim.

'Old queen' is one of a small cast the crossword keeps on call — Anne, Bess, Cleo — and the letter count is what picks which of them is wanted.

11 across DINNER JACKET

Clothing for one providing party music initially (6,6)

Definition: Clothing · cryptic definition

Clothing → DINNER JACKET: a dinner jacket is the black formal jacket worn with a bow tie for evening dress, the British name for a tuxedo.

  • one providing party music → On the surface this is a person hired for the evening — the disc jockey, known to everyone by two letters rather than by a job title.
  • initially → The setter means the initials of the answer itself: take the first letter of each of its two words and you have the very same pair the disc jockey goes by.

Formal wear, apparently recommended for whoever is running the music at the party.

DJ is the whole joke: a dinner jacket is a DJ, and so is the person spinning the records, so the garment and the guest share a monogram.

13 across EMIL

Half of them fail to see this fellow (4)

Definition: this fellow · charade + last letters

this fellow → EMIL: the answer is a man's given name, so the definition can only point at a particular male rather than give a synonym.

  • them → EM the closing two letters, which is half of this four-letter word
  • fail → IL the back half again — the final two letters of another four-letter word
  • Half of — Half of a four-letter word is two of its letters, and the halves wanted here are the back ones, taken from the tail of each word rather than the head.

Some of the group cannot manage to spot the man they came for.

Emil is a German and Scandinavian boy's name, best known in Britain from Erich Kästner's Emil and the Detectives. 'Fail to see' is split in two, and only 'fail' hands over letters.

14 across TASTIEST

Wine in sample is most delicious (8)

Definition: most delicious · container

most delicious → TASTIEST: tasty means pleasing on the palate, so its superlative names the single best-tasting thing, superlative answering superlative.

  • sample → TEST to sample something is to test it; the word opens up after its first letter
  • Wine → ASTI Asti is the sparkling white from the Piedmont town of that name
  • in — The little word is pure placement: whatever was named before it drops inside whatever is named after it.

One wine in the tasting flight beats every other bottle on the table.

'Wine' in a cryptic is almost always one particular bottle rather than the drink in general, and the Italian sparkler is the four-letter one worth learning first.

17 across TORTILLA

Wrong when unwell to have a flatbread (8)

Definition: flatbread · charade

flatbread → TORTILLA: a tortilla is the thin unleavened Mexican round of corn or wheat flour, one particular member of the flatbread family.

  • Wrong → TORT a tort is a civil wrong, the lawyer's word for a harm you can sue over
  • unwell → ILL ill is unwell
  • a → A the indefinite article, lifted straight out of the clue

It would be a mistake to eat bread while you are feeling poorly.

'Wrong' is the legal noun, not the adjective the surface offers — a wrong done to a person rather than a wrong answer — and it is the piece a solver reaches for last.

18 across FINE

Subtle film is not exalted initially (4)

Definition: Subtle · first letters

Subtle → FINE: a fine difference is one so delicate it is hard to perceive, which is precisely what subtle means, adjective for adjective.

  • film is not exalted → FINE the opening letters of these four words, read in the order they already stand
  • initially — Taken initially means taken at the very start, so only the opening letter of each word in the run is kept and the rest is dropped.

A picture that nobody thought much of, at least to begin with.

'Subtle' is the distinction sense of the answer — a fine distinction is one drawn almost too delicately to see — rather than the weather or the parking penalty.

20 across PERSONAL BEST

Plaster bones when fractured – you’ve never done it better (8,4)

Definition: you’ve never done it better · anagram

you’ve never done it better → PERSONAL BEST: an athlete's personal best is their own finest recorded performance, so nothing they have done beats it.

  • Plaster bones → PLASTERBONES anagram fodder, 12 letters
  • fractured → anagram indicator
  • fractured — A fractured bone has broken into pieces that no longer line up, so the letters are to be set down again in some other order.

A doctor sets broken bones in plaster, and nobody has ever made a better job of it.

A personal best is athletics jargon for the finest performance you have ever recorded, so the hospital scene the surface paints has nothing at all to do with the answer.

23 across DIGEST

Take in the essence of fading legend Castro (6)

Definition: Take in · charade + middle letters

Take in → DIGEST: to digest a report is to take it in and absorb it, the figurative use of what the stomach does with a meal.

  • fading → DI the pair at the centre of this six-letter word, with two letters shed from each end
  • legend → GE the middle pair again, taken the same way from the same length of word
  • Castro → ST the heart of the name, its two central letters
  • the essence of — The essence of a thing is what sits at its heart, so take the letters standing dead centre and let the outer ones fall away.

Absorb what really mattered about the fading Cuban icon.

Three six-letter words standing in a row is the signal: one instruction covers all three, and the repetition is what tells you so before you have found any letters.

24 across GIVEAWAY

Eventually agree to accept a freebie (8)

Definition: freebie · container

freebie → GIVEAWAY: a giveaway is something handed over at no charge — the free sample, the promotional gift — which is what 'freebie' names informally.

  • Eventually agree → GIVEWAY to give way is to stop holding out and agree in the end; the phrase splits between its two words
  • a → A the indefinite article, dropped into the join
  • to accept — To accept a thing is to take it in and keep it, so the phrase named first opens up and receives the letter named after it.

You hold out for a while and then say yes to something free.

Give way is what a road sign orders and what a person under pressure does, and only the second reading survives once you need the phrase to split neatly in two.

25 across WHODUNIT

Intelligence about destroyed hound identifying culprit? (8)

Definition: identifying culprit? · container + anagram

identifying culprit? → WHODUNIT: a whodunit is a detective story whose entire plot is the identification of the culprit, its name a jokey misspelling of 'who done it'.

  • Intelligence → WIT wit is intelligence, quickness of mind; the word splits open after its first letter
  • hound → HODUN the five letters of this word shaken into a new order
  • about — A scarf worn about the neck goes round the outside, so this word closes the letters before it over the letters after it.
  • destroyed — Something destroyed has been smashed apart, and the fragments can be put back together in any order at all.

Word reaches the police about a savaged dog, and the question is who is to blame.

The question mark earns its place: the answer is a deliberate misspelling of 'who done it', a name for a kind of story rather than a phrase, so a definition can only gesture at it.

26 across THE ICK

Turn off with this dense absorption of energy (3,3)

Definition: Turn off · container

Turn off → THE ICK: 'the ick' is current slang for the rush of distaste that ends an attraction to someone, which is exactly what a turn-off is.

  • dense → THICK a thick person is dense; the word opens after its first two letters
  • energy → E E = energy, the physicist's symbol from E = mc²
  • absorption of — What is absorbed is taken up and swallowed, so the letter named next vanishes into the middle of the word standing before it.

Some dull, power-swallowing appliance is the thing you would want to switch off.

'The ick' is recent slang: the sudden wave of revulsion that kills an attraction stone dead. Read 'turn off' as the noun, a turn-off, and the definition lines up.

2 down UFOS

Strange sightings occur regularly in Gulf Coast (4)

Definition: Strange sightings · alternate letters

Strange sightings → UFOS: unidentified flying objects are by name the things people see and cannot explain, plural for plural.

  • Gulf Coast → UFOS walk through the nine letters of these two words and keep every second one, beginning with the second letter of the first word
  • occur regularly — Something that occurs regularly comes round at a fixed interval, and the interval here is one letter: take one, skip one, all the way through.

Odd things keep being reported in the skies over the Gulf Coast.

'Gulf Coast' is capitalised as a place name, which is the disguise — to the wordplay it is nothing but a row of nine letters to count along.

3 down BLOOD CLOT

Family fool is a potential killer (5,4)

Definition: a potential killer · charade

a potential killer → BLOOD CLOT: a clot that breaks free can block an artery in the heart, lung or brain, so it is something that may kill you.

  • Family → BLOOD your own blood is your family — blood relatives, blood being thicker than water
  • fool → CLOT clot is mild British slang for an idiot, the sort of thing an exasperated parent says

A relative who behaves like an idiot may turn out to be genuinely dangerous.

Both halves are insults on the surface and neither is one underneath: 'family' is the kinship sense of blood, and the medical sense is what does the damage.

4 down ERRANT

Go wrong taking on colony worker wandering around (6)

Definition: wandering around · charade

wandering around → ERRANT: errant keeps its older sense of roaming or straying, the one preserved in 'knight errant', rather than the modern meaning of mistaken.

  • Go wrong → ERR to err is to go wrong — to err is human
  • colony worker → ANT ants live in colonies and it is the workers that do the labour; this is the crossword's standard worker
  • taking on — To take something on is to add it to what you are already carrying, so the second chunk is joined onto the end of the first.

A firm blunders by hiring an insect from the colony, which promptly drifts off the job.

'Worker' is the crossword's most reliable ant and 'colony' settles it. The definition wants the knight-errant sense: straying about, not mistaken.

5 down FASTER THAN LIGHT

Starting the half playing at impossible speed (6,4,5)

Definition: at impossible speed · anagram

at impossible speed → FASTER THAN LIGHT: relativity forbids anything exceeding light speed, so travelling faster than light is the standard shorthand for a speed nothing can reach.

  • Starting the half → STARTINGTHEHALF anagram fodder, 15 letters — exactly the length of the grid entry
  • playing → anagram indicator
  • playing — Loose parts are said to play in their housing when they shift about instead of sitting still, and letters told to play will not stay where they were put.

A substitute comes on at the start of the second half and tears about the pitch.

The fodder hides because 'Starting the half playing' reads as football team talk. Counting is what unmasks it: fifteen letters offered, fifteen letters wanted.

6 down READJUST

Study what’s right and make further changes (8)

Definition: make further changes · charade

make further changes → READJUST: the RE- prefix means doing the adjusting over again, so it is a second round of alterations to something already set.

  • Study → READ to read a subject at university is to study it
  • what’s right → JUST a just verdict is a right one, right in the moral sense

Work out what the correct answer is, then go on tinkering with it.

'Read' for study is British university usage — you read history at Durham — and 'right' is the fairness sense here, not the direction and not the political side.

7 down IONIC

Sort of bond used in classical architecture (5)

Definition: Sort of bond · double definition

An ionic bond is the chemical link between oppositely charged ions; the Ionic order is a classical Greek column style, recognised by its scrolled capitals.

  • Sort of bond → chemistry's reading: the link formed when one atom hands an electron to another and the two opposite charges hold together
  • used in classical architecture → the builder's reading: one of the three Greek orders of column, the one whose capital is carved as a pair of scrolls

A kind of bond familiar from the columns of ancient Greek temples.

Nothing is taken apart here — the two senses simply have nothing to do with each other, one from the chemistry lab and one from the Acropolis, and five letters serve both.

8 down HEAVEN-SENT

An event she worked on is miraculous (6-4)

Definition: miraculous · anagram

miraculous → HEAVEN-SENT: something heaven-sent arrives as if by divine gift at exactly the right moment, the everyday phrase for a small miracle.

  • An event she → ANEVENTSHE anagram fodder, 10 letters
  • worked on → anagram indicator
  • worked on — Dough that has been worked on has been kneaded and pushed into a new shape, so the letters handed over come out rearranged.

Whatever she was organising turned out unbelievably well.

'An' is doing quiet work: the article belongs to the fodder, and a swallowed little word is where long anagrams usually keep their spare letters.

12 down IMPOVERISH

Rogue finished up sort of bankrupt (10)

Definition: bankrupt · charade

bankrupt → IMPOVERISH: 'bankrupt' is the verb here, and to bankrupt someone is to strip them of their money, which is what impoverishing them does.

  • Rogue → IMP an imp is a small mischief-maker, a rogue in miniature
  • finished up → OVER when a thing is finished up it is over
  • sort of → ISH -ish is the suffix English tacks on to mean roughly or approximately, and it has become a word in its own right

A scoundrel ended up more or less broke.

'Up' belongs to 'finished' and to nothing else — in a down clue it looks very much like a turning instruction, and here it is only half of a phrasal verb.

15 down INFATUATE

Fat auntie dancing to inspire passion (9)

Definition: inspire passion · anagram

inspire passion → INFATUATE: to infatuate someone is to fill them with an intense and unreasoning passion, the verb sitting behind 'infatuation'.

  • Fat auntie → FATAUNTIE anagram fodder, 9 letters — the length of the entry
  • dancing → anagram indicator
  • dancing — A dancer is never still, moving from one position to the next, so the letters offered will not be standing where they started.

A large aunt takes to the floor at the party and stirs somebody up.

The joke is the distance travelled: a mildly unkind description of a relative at a wedding turns into the verb for helpless romantic obsession.

16 down PLANKTON

Board not turning up for seafood (8)

Definition: seafood · charade + reversal

seafood → PLANKTON: plankton is the drifting mass of tiny organisms whales and fish live on, so it is food from the sea rather than food from a restaurant.

  • Board → PLANK a board of sawn timber is a plank
  • not → TON the three letters of this word climbing back up the entry the other way
  • turning up — A down entry is written from the top downwards, so letters told to turn up are climbing against the grain and are written back to front.

The board fails to show up on the day the seafood is served.

'Seafood' is doing the loosest sort of work here: this is food found in the sea rather than anything you would find on a menu, which is what makes the definition hard to trust.

19 down VELVET

Soft material added to underground for old group (6)

Definition: Soft material · deletion

Soft material → VELVET: velvet is a woven fabric with a dense short pile, which is what makes it soft to the touch.

  • old group → VELVETUNDERGROUND Lou Reed's New York rock band of the 1960s, a name two words long
  • underground → UNDERGROUND the second of those two words, handed to you in the clue and therefore the part that has to come off
  • added to — Whatever has been added to something can be taken off it again, so the word named next comes away from the longer name at the end of the clue.

Soft padding is being laid down in the tube tunnels for the benefit of a veteran band.

The clue works backwards: it gives you the tail of a famous name and a definition of its head, so the move is subtraction rather than addition.

21 down SPEND

Lay out odds before finale (5)

Definition: Lay out · charade

Lay out → SPEND: to lay out money is to hand it over in payment, a slightly old-fashioned way of saying spend, verb for verb.

  • odds → SP SP is starting price, the odds a horse goes off at when the race begins — British betting-shop shorthand
  • finale → END the finale of anything is its end
  • before — What comes before something is written in front of it, so the chunk named first is placed ahead of the chunk named second.

The odds are chalked up in good time ahead of the final race.

'Lay out' is the money sense — to lay out fifty pounds on a horse — which is exactly the reading the racing surface is trying to make you skip past.

22 down TALC

Drying material crystal clear to some extent (4)

Definition: Drying material · hidden word

Drying material → TALC: talcum powder is ground talc, dusted on to absorb moisture, so the mineral is named for the job it does.

  • crystal clear → TALC four consecutive letters run across the gap between these two words, ignoring the space
  • to some extent — Only part of what is written is wanted, so a run of letters is taken from somewhere inside the phrase instead of using all of it.

A drying powder is being described as almost perfectly see-through.

Talc is talcum powder, the mineral milled fine and dusted on damp skin. It genuinely is a soft crystal, which is why 'crystal clear' can hide it without looking odd.
