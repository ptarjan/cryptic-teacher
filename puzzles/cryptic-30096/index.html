Across

1 across PROFITS

Professional adds up money (7)

Definition: money · charade

money -> PROFITS: what a business clears once costs are paid, so the general word points at one particular sort of takings.

  • Professional → PRO PRO is the short form a professional golfer or footballer goes by
  • adds up → FITS when a story adds up it fits — the two phrases mean the same

The surface is an accountant at work, which is exactly where 'adds up' belongs; read as wordplay it is a plain synonym instead.

5 across EXAMPLE

Model paper plane’s not one (7)

Definition: Model · charade + deletion

Model -> EXAMPLE: the specimen held up to be copied — 'a model of restraint' is someone being offered as one.

  • paper → EXAM the paper you sit in a hall is an exam
  • one → AN 'one' here is the indefinite article, and these are the letters that go missing
  • plane’s → PLE PLANE with that article struck out of its middle
  • not — Not is the plainest removal instruction there is: whatever follows it is missing from the word standing before it.

'One' as the indefinite article is the piece of vocabulary to bank: a setter can write one and mean nothing more than A or AN.

9 across POTPOURRI

Pocket tip and mostly split collection (9)

Definition: collection · charade + deletion

collection -> POTPOURRI: a jar of mixed dried petals, and by extension any miscellany — an assortment of unlike things rather than a matched set.

  • Pocket → POT to pot a ball at snooker is to pocket it
  • tip → POUR to tip a jug is to pour from it
  • split → RI to split cloth is to RIP it, and the last letter is left behind
  • mostly — Most of a word is all of it bar the final letter, so the word beside this one arrives one letter short.

'Tip' is a verb here, as it is nowhere else in this puzzle, and the French original of the answer means, aptly enough, rotten pot.

10 across TOTED

Did carry tons having ends of feet on either side (5)

Definition: Did carry · container

Did carry -> TOTED: to tote is to lug something about, as in 'tote that barge', so the past tense answers 'did carry'.

  • having ends of feet → TOED the ends of feet are toes, so whatever has them is described as toed
  • tons → T T is the standard abbreviation for tons
  • on either side — What has something on either side of it has been split to make room, half the letters landing to the left and half to the right.

The surface has someone hauling a load with toes poking out on both sides; the wordplay is the same picture with one letter wedged in the middle.

11 across SEED

Descendants to get diamonds (4)

Definition: Descendants · charade

Descendants -> SEED: the biblical sense of the word, where a man's seed is his offspring and every generation after him.

  • get → SEE to get a joke is to see it
  • diamonds → D D is diamonds at the bridge table

Cards supply the last letter, as they so often do, and 'get' has to be read as understanding rather than obtaining.

12 across BIG BROTHER

Ubiquitous leader is great alongside fellow union member (3,7)

Definition: Ubiquitous leader · charade

Ubiquitous leader -> BIG BROTHER: Orwell's 1984 gave the ever-watching head of state a name now used for any authority you cannot get away from.

  • great → BIG a great tree and a big tree are the same tree
  • fellow union member → BROTHER trade unionists have addressed one another that way since the movement began
  • alongside — Set alongside means set next to, so the two pieces stand shoulder to shoulder in the order the clue names them.

The union convention is the one to bank here, and it keeps the whole surface at a shop-floor meeting where nobody is watching anybody.

14 across BREECH

Awkward delivery bowled here; front of crease is rough (6)

Definition: Awkward delivery · anagram + first letter

Awkward delivery -> BREECH: a birth in which the baby arrives bottom-first, the hard way round, is a breech presentation.

  • bowled → B b is how a scorecard records a batsman bowled
  • here → HERE four letters of fodder, taken as they stand
  • crease → C the leading letter of crease joins the fodder
  • front of — The front of a word is the letter it starts on, so only that one is taken from what follows.
  • rough — Rough ground is uneven and unsorted, and letters left rough have not been smoothed into the order they came in.

Two kinds of delivery in one word: the ball bowled at the crease, and the birth the definition means. Only one of them is awkward.

15 across RESERVE

Soldiers wait on order (7)

Definition: order · charade

order -> RESERVE: to reserve a table is to order it kept for you, the booking sense of the verb rather than the commanding one.

  • Soldiers → RE RE is the Royal Engineers, the crossword's standing regiment
  • wait on → SERVE to wait on a table is to serve it

Three military-sounding words in a row and only the first is doing army work; the surface reads as soldiers awaiting instructions.

16 across CYPRESS

Conservative leader of yesteryear to hug tree (7)

Definition: tree · charade + first letter

tree -> CYPRESS: the dark evergreen conifer of Mediterranean churchyards, so the general word is pointing at one particular species.

  • Conservative → C C is the Conservative party on a ballot paper
  • yesteryear → Y the head of yesteryear, one letter only
  • hug → PRESS to press someone to you is to hug them
  • leader of — A word's leader is the letter at its head, so the noun after this hands over its first letter and stays out of the rest.

A Tory grandee from the old days is the picture, and two single letters is all the clue wants of him before the embrace.

18 across HEIFER

Perhaps one in stock here if wanting order (6)

Definition: Perhaps one in stock · anagram

Perhaps one in stock -> HEIFER: livestock is stock on a farm, and a young cow that has not calved is one instance of it.

  • here if → HEREIF six letters of fodder, taken from the two words as they stand
  • wanting order — To want something is to lack it, so letters wanting order can be put back in any other arrangement.

'Perhaps' is the setter admitting the definition is only an example, and the shopkeeper's sense of 'in stock' is there to keep you off the farm.

20 across SUPERWOMAN

She flies American back every week to country (10)

Definition: She flies · charade + reversal

She flies -> SUPERWOMAN: the comic-book heroine of the Superman family, whose powers include flight, with 'she' marking out the female one.

  • American → SU the American is US, and it arrives facing the other way
  • every → PER per day means every day
  • week → W W is the calendar's short form of week
  • country → OMAN the sultanate at the eastern end of the Arabian peninsula
  • back — Back sends an across answer's letters the other way along the row, right to left, so the piece it follows arrives turned round.

The capital S of 'She' is fair warning that a named person is wanted, and everything after it is build — four pieces, only one of them a whole word.

21 across AIDS

Bills squeezing current benefits (4)

Definition: benefits · container

benefits -> AIDS: things that help, the way a hearing aid or a visual aid benefits whoever uses it, so both plurals name supports.

  • Bills → ADS a bill pasted up on a hoarding is an advert
  • current → I I is the physicist's symbol for electric current
  • squeezing — Squeezing something in forces it between what is already there, so the single letter ends up inside the longer piece.

Bills are posters here rather than invoices, and the current is the one running down a wire — two everyday words both used in their second sense.

24 across OWLET

Downed one water, drinking litre after round (5)

Definition: Downed one · charade + container

Downed one -> OWLET: a nestling owl still covered in down, so 'downed' describes its plumage and not anything drunk or knocked over.

  • round → O the round shape is the letter O
  • water → WET the wet is water — the stuff you come in out of
  • litre → L L is the litre on a bottle label
  • drinking — Whatever is drinking takes something in, so the longer piece swallows the single letter named beside it.
  • after — After the round means behind it, so that letter leads and the rest of the build follows on.

Downed, drinking, water, litre, round: the surface is a heavy night at the pub, and the definition is about feathers rather than drink.

25 across TITLE ROLE

Lead championship with Ronaldo’s header: that’s great for Real Madrid fans? (5,4)

Definition: Lead · charade + first letter

Lead -> TITLE ROLE: the part a play is named after is its leading part, so whoever acts it has the lead.

  • championship → TITLE the championship a fighter or a club holds is the title
  • Ronaldo’s → R the head of the footballer's name
  • that’s great for Real Madrid fans? → OLE olé is the Spanish shout of approval a Madrid crowd would be roaring
  • header — A header in football is played off the top of the head, so the top letter of the name beside it is what comes off.

Ronaldo and Real Madrid keep the surface on the pitch, and the last three letters are a crowd's shout rather than any English word.

26 across STRIDER

One steps over poo; shoe initially turned over (7)

Definition: One steps · charade + reversal + first letter

One steps -> STRIDER: someone who walks in long steps, the -er ending turning the action into the person performing it.

  • over → RE a row over money is a row re money — over can mean concerning
  • poo → DIRT dirt is what a dog leaves on the pavement
  • shoe → S the first letter of shoe
  • initially — Taken initially, a word gives up only the letter it begins on and none of the rest.
  • turned over — Anything turned over ends up facing the way it came from, so the assembled letters are read from the far end back.

'Over' is the piece to bank: it can mean concerning, the RE of a memo heading, and spotting that is what breaks the pavement scene into letters.

27 across OBSCENE

Tramp without clothes, sight is disgusting (7)

Definition: disgusting · charade + deletion

disgusting -> OBSCENE: what offends decency outright; the word has widened from indecent to grossly excessive, as in obscene wealth.

  • Tramp → OB a tramp is a HOBO, and the two letters at his edges come off
  • sight → SCENE a sight to behold is a scene
  • without clothes — Strip the clothes off a word and its outermost letters go with them, leaving only what they were covering.

Tramp is this setter's own pseudonym, so he opens his last clue by undressing himself — and the whole surface stays in character.

Down

1 down PIPES

Good, cheap cigarette papers, ultimately they’re for smoking tobacco (5)

Definition: they’re for smoking tobacco · charade + last letters

they’re for smoking tobacco -> PIPES: the bowl-and-stem kind a smoker fills and lights, named by the job they do rather than by a synonym.

  • Good → PI PI is short for pious, the crossword's good in the sanctimonious sense
  • cheap cigarette papers → PES the closing letters of the three words, in the order they are given
  • ultimately — Ultimately means at the very end, so each word in the list before it hands over its final letter and nothing else.

PI for 'good' is worth banking — it is pious cut down, and a setter drops it in without warning. The comma list after it is quiet letter-work.

2 down OUTWEAR

Determined to put on exhaust (7)

Definition: exhaust · charade

exhaust -> OUTWEAR: to outwear something is to wear it out, using it until nothing is left — which is what exhausting a supply or a person means.

  • Determined → OUT someone out to win is set on winning, so out carries the determination
  • put on → WEAR clothes you put on are clothes you wear

'Determined to put on' reads as one phrase about getting dressed, and splits into two unrelated senses the moment you stop reading it as English.

3 down IRON

Club batting, accepting run out (4)

Definition: Club · container

Club -> IRON: the metal-headed golf clubs are the irons, so the general word is pointing at one particular kind of club.

  • batting → IN a batsman at the crease is said to be in
  • run out → RO RO is the scorer's shorthand beside a dismissal of that kind
  • accepting — Whatever accepts something takes it inside itself, so these letters open up and let the next pair sit in the middle.

Cricket runs through the whole surface, and the definition has quietly walked off to a different sport where the clubs are numbered.

4 down STRAIGHT SHOOTER

True American siren chasing pure son (8,7)

Definition: True American · charade

True American -> STRAIGHT SHOOTER: US idiom for one who deals honestly and says what they mean, shooting straight rather than crooked.

  • pure → STRAIGHT spirits drunk with nothing added are drunk straight, so straight is undiluted
  • son → S S is son on a family tree
  • siren → HOOTER a hooter is the siren that sounds over a factory
  • chasing — One thing chasing another runs along behind it, so the piece doing the chasing takes up the rear of the assembly.

The siren is a hooter, the noisy sort on a factory roof, and the fifteen letters run across the whole grid because two of the three pieces are ordinary words.

5 down EDINBURGH TATTOO

Forces show terrible drug being cut: heroin put into skin with needle (9, 6)

Definition: Forces show · anagram + deletion + charade

Forces show -> EDINBURGH TATTOO: the massed military display staged on the castle esplanade each August, mounted by the armed services.

  • drug → DRUG four letters of fodder, taken as they stand
  • being → BEIN BEING docked of its last letter gives four more
  • heroin → H H is heroin in the crossword's drug shorthand
  • put into skin with needle → TATTOO ink put into skin with a needle names the second word outright, no letters required
  • terrible — Something terrible is in a bad state, and letters in a bad state are not left in the order the clue handed them over.
  • cut — A word that has been cut has had a piece taken off the end, so its last letter never reaches the assembly.

Half the answer is spelled out of letters and half is simply defined, which is why the clue changes gear at the colon. A military tattoo is a display, not a drug.

6 down AUTHORSHIP

Writing this, hour wasted filling a puzzle at the beginning (10)

Definition: Writing · anagram + container + first letter

Writing -> AUTHORSHIP: the fact of having written something, as in 'the authorship of the letter is disputed' — who did the writing.

  • a → A the clue's own a, taken as a single letter
  • this, hour → THISHOUR eight letters of fodder waiting to be reordered
  • puzzle → P the opening letter of puzzle and no more of it
  • wasted — Time wasted is time laid to ruin, and ruined letters do not keep the order they arrived in.
  • filling — What fills a space sits inside it, so this material beds down between the two pieces on either side.
  • at the beginning — Only what stands at the front is wanted, so the word beside it gives up its opening letter alone.

'Writing this' looks like the setter at his desk complaining about the hour, and 'this' turns out to be raw material rather than a reference to the crossword.

7 down PITCHER

One trying to flog something is a tosser (7)

Definition: One trying to flog something · double definition

Both senses are people who throw — the salesman throws his pitch at a customer, the baseball player throws the ball at the batter.

  • One trying to flog something → PITCHER to flog is to sell, and a salesman's spiel is his sales pitch
  • a tosser → PITCHER in baseball, the player who throws the ball at the batter

The British insult buried in 'tosser' is the trap: the clue means the word literally, and both halves land on someone who throws.

8 down ENDORSE

Back horse with tip for the first from Haydock (7)

Definition: Back · charade + substitution

Back -> ENDORSE: to endorse a candidate is to put your name behind them publicly, which is what backing someone amounts to.

  • tip → END the tip of a thing is its far END
  • horse → ORSE HORSE with the letter it starts on given away
  • the first from Haydock → H the letter that steps aside is the one Haydock opens on
  • for — Putting one thing in for another is a swap, so what precedes it moves into the place the letter named after it was holding.

Haydock is a racecourse, so the horse and the tip belong to the same afternoon — and 'tip' turns out to mean an extremity, not a hot prospect.

13 down CEREBRATED

Thought before entering bet, card getting dealt (10)

Definition: Thought · anagram + container

Thought -> CEREBRATED: to cerebrate is to use the cerebrum, that is to think, and the past tense matches 'thought' as the past of think.

  • bet, card → BETCARD seven letters of fodder, taken from the two words together
  • before → ERE ERE is the poet's word for before, and it drops in once the rest is shuffled
  • entering — To enter a room is to go inside it, so this short word lands within the letters that surround it.
  • getting dealt — Cards being dealt come off the pack in no particular order, which is what happens to these letters.

The card table in the surface is a decoy for a word about brains, and 'before' is the shortest bit of poetic vocabulary a setter can slip past you.

16 down CASTORS

Vehicles over street with old wheels (7)

Definition: wheels · container

wheels -> CASTORS: the little swivelling wheels screwed under furniture so a chair or a trolley rolls wherever it is pushed.

  • Vehicles → CARS the commonest vehicles of all, and they part in the middle
  • street → ST ST is street on any envelope
  • old → O O is old, as in Old English
  • over — One thing laid over another covers it, so these letters close around what follows instead of stacking on top of it.

In a down clue 'over' tempts you to stack the pieces one above the next; here it means covering, the way a coat goes over a shirt.

17 down POPULAR

Common cold going round pub, endlessly (7)

Definition: Common · container + deletion

Common -> POPULAR: both mean of the people at large — popular opinion is common opinion, the Latin populus sense rather than 'well liked'.

  • cold → POLAR the polar regions are the cold ends of the earth
  • pub → PU PUB stripped of its last letter is what goes inside
  • going round — Something going round another thing encircles it, so these letters part and close again on what comes next.
  • endlessly — Without its end, the word beside this hands over everything except its final letter.

The common cold is one illness in the surface and two separate jobs in the wordplay, which is what makes the phrase so easy to read straight past.

19 down EPITOME

Summary in short, great book (7)

Definition: Summary · charade + deletion

Summary -> EPITOME: the older sense of the word, an abstract or condensed version of a longer work, rather than 'the perfect specimen of'.

  • great → EPI an epic is great in scale, and it arrives a letter short
  • book → TOME a tome is a weighty book
  • in short — Something given in short is cut down, so the word governed by this loses its last letter before joining on.

Everyday English uses this word for a perfect specimen; the clue wants the original bookish sense, which is also why a tome turns up in the build.

22 down SUEDE

‘90s band did rock on the radio (5)

Definition: ‘90s band · homophone

‘90s band -> SUEDE: the Britpop group who opened the decade, so the definition names one particular band of those years.

  • did rock → SUEDE to rock back and forth is to sway, so 'did rock' is SWAYED — and that is the noise the answer makes
  • on the radio — What reaches you over the radio is heard and never seen, so the spelling is the solver's to work out from the sound alone.

The material the band named itself after is spelt nothing like the word you say out loud, which is the whole point of hearing it broadcast.

23 down FEES

Spot fine, put up charges (4)

Definition: charges · charade + reversal

charges -> FEES: what a professional charges for services rendered, the money sense of 'charge' rather than the accusation or the cavalry's.

  • Spot → SEE to spot something is to see it
  • fine → F F is fine on the side of a pencil
  • put up — Put up means raised, and in a down answer a raised assembly is read from the bottom of the column upwards.

The surface reads as a police caution — spot fine, charges brought — and every word of it means something else.
