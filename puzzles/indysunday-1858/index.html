Across

1 across APPARENT

Old men with heads together pulled apart, glaring (8)

Definition: glaring · charade + reversal

Something glaring is obvious at a glance, so this is the plainly-visible sense of the answer rather than the 'seeming but not real' one.

  • Old men → APPA a pa is an old man; there are two of them, and the first is turned round so the two Ps touch
  • pulled apart → RENT cloth pulled apart is rent, the past participle of rend
  • with heads together — a word's head is its first letter, and two heads put together means the first word is turned round so that the two initials meet in the middle

Two old men huddled head to head, then pulled apart, glowering.

'Glaring' reads as the old men's angry stare and means blatant instead, as in a glaring error. The instruction to reverse one PA is buried in the picture of two heads leaning together.

6 across COVERT

Cryptic equivalent of temperature into Celsius? (6)

Definition: Cryptic · charade

Cryptic and covert both mean deliberately hidden: a covert operation is kept out of sight, and a cryptic remark keeps its meaning out of sight.

  • Celsius → C C is the abbreviation for Celsius on any thermometer
  • into → OVER 'four into twelve' is the schoolroom way of saying twelve divided by four, and a division is written as one number over another
  • temperature → T T is the standard abbreviation for temperature

A quiz question about converting a temperature into Celsius.

The question mark is the setter owning up: 'into' is not an insertion here but arithmetic, and division is the one operation you write as a word.

9 across BUNG

Chuck's ready to corrupt official, perhaps (4)

Definition: Chuck · double definition

To chuck something is to bung it, informally; and a bung is the wad of ready money slipped to an official to buy a decision.

  • Chuck → the informal verb for throwing something without taking much care over it
  • ready to corrupt official, perhaps → the ready is old slang for cash in hand, and cash pressed on an official to bend a decision has its own name in football reporting

Chuck has the cash to bribe an official.

'Chuck' sits at the front of the sentence looking like a man's name and is a verb. The whole of the rest of the clue is the second definition, resting on 'the ready' meaning money.

10 across RAINFOREST

Artist chosen to replace Parisian is soaking wood (10)

Definition: soaking wood · charade

A rainforest is woodland soaked by constant rain, so 'soaking wood' describes it literally rather than by any figure of speech.

  • Artist → RA an RA is a Royal Academician, the crossword's standard artist
  • chosen → IN a player chosen for the team is in
  • to replace → FOR a substitute comes on for the player going off, which is 'for' meaning in place of
  • Parisian is → EST 'est' is the French for 'is', so a Parisian says it

A painter picked to stand in for a French one.

'To replace' looks like an instruction to swap something over and is only the word 'for'. Nothing in this clue asks you to do anything: four short words are each traded for a shorter one and laid end to end.

11 across MISUNDERSTAND

Take wrong street with east of motorway split (13)

Definition: Take wrong · charade

To take something wrong is to misunderstand it, 'take' being the sense in which you take a remark badly.

  • motorway → MI the M1 runs north out of London, and in a grid its number does duty as the letter I
  • split → SUNDER to sunder something is to split it in two
  • street → ST ST is the standard abbreviation for street
  • with → AND bread with butter, bread and butter: the two words join things in the same way
  • east of — east is to the right on any map, and in an across entry the letters to the right are the ones written later

Taking the wrong street east of where the motorway divides.

'Take wrong' is a verb phrase, not a driving error, and that is the whole misdirection, since every other word is doing map work. Road numbers become letters freely in grids, which have no squares for digits.

12 across STUCCO

Plaster commanding officers put on wounds in retreat (6)

Definition: Plaster · charade + reversal

Stucco is the fine plaster spread onto walls and mouldings, so 'plaster' names the material by its general kind.

  • wounds → STUC wounds are cuts, and those four letters are then read backwards
  • commanding officers → CO the CO is the commanding officer of a unit
  • put on — plaster is put on a wall, and here one piece is laid onto the end of the other
  • in retreat — an army in retreat goes back the way it came, and in an across entry going back means the letters run right to left

Commanding officers dressing wounds while the army falls back.

'In retreat' does two jobs at once, keeping the army picture going and turning four letters round. Plaster here is the render on a wall, not the dressing the surface offers you.

14 across DEPRIVED

Stripped, dive deeper at sea using half the energy (8)

Definition: Stripped · anagram + deletion

To be deprived of something is to have been stripped of it, and both are past participles for having had a thing taken away.

  • dive deeper → DIVEDEPR the ten letters of the two words with two of their four Es dropped, leaving eight
  • at sea — a swimmer at sea is adrift with nothing to hold position by, so the letters lose the order they arrived in
  • using half the energy — energy is E, four of them are handed over, and only half that many are used

Free divers going down further on less air.

'Stripped' looks exactly like an anagram signal and is the definition instead, so the word you would reach for first is the one word not giving an instruction. The Es are counted, not chosen.

16 across STANDS UP

Despite arrangement, doesn't date pay for drink? (6,2)

Definition: Despite arrangement, doesn't date · charade

To arrange a date and then not appear is to stand somebody up, and the definition is that failure to show, put in the third person.

  • pay for → STAND to stand somebody a drink is to pay for it
  • drink → SUP to sup is to drink, the old northern verb still heard in 'supping ale'

Somebody who made an arrangement and then neither turns up nor pays for the drinks.

The definition runs on for five words and the wordplay is only the last three, which is why the join is so hard to find. Both halves of the wordplay are pub verbs, so the sentence never changes subject.

19 across OVERLY

Too old really to snort line (6)

Definition: Too · charade + container

'Too' in the sense of excessively, as in too keen, is exactly what this adverb means, and it is not the first sense that comes to mind.

  • old → O O is the standard abbreviation for old
  • really → VERY really good, very good: the two intensifiers do the same job
  • line → L L is the standard abbreviation for line
  • to snort — a line snorted is drawn up inside the person taking it, so this letter goes inside the word beside it

An elderly person really snorting a line of cocaine.

Every word of the surface is about drugs and every word of the wordplay is about grammar. 'Too' is the adverb of excess, as in too keen, not the one meaning 'as well'.

21 across COUNTERWEIGHT

Opposing force tally with crew on Queen Elizabeth (13)

Definition: Opposing force · charade

A counterweight is the mass hung on the far side to pull against a load, so 'opposing force' names the job it does.

  • tally → COUNT to tally votes is to count them
  • Queen Elizabeth → ER ER is Elizabeth Regina, the royal monogram on British postboxes and stamps
  • with → W W is the standard abbreviation for with
  • crew → EIGHT an eight is the standard racing boat rowed at Henley, so its crew and its name are the same number
  • on — one thing set on another in an across entry is written after it, so the pieces named before this word follow the one named after it

The ship's company mustering aboard the liner Queen Elizabeth.

'Crew' means the number eight because a rowing eight is a boatload, a piece of British sporting shorthand worth keeping. The liner in the surface has nothing to do with the letters.

24 across BLUE RIBAND

Atlantic voyage's record Iand-lubber sabotaged with hidden typo? (4,6)

Definition: Atlantic voyage's record · anagram + substitution

The Blue Riband was the trophy for the fastest Atlantic crossing by a passenger liner, so the definition names that record by its prize.

  • Iand-lubber → IANDLUBBER as printed the word opens with a capital I where an L belongs: ten letters, and that swapped letter is the typo
  • sabotaged — sabotage wrecks the working order of a thing, so what it touches does not survive in the order it was given in
  • with hidden typo? — a typo is one letter standing where another should be, and this is the setter warning that the word as printed is not the word you think you are reading

A sailor's record-breaking Atlantic crossing wrecked by a landlubber.

The typo is real and it is on the page, a capital I standing in for the L, and those printed letters are the ones that work. Read the word as 'landlubber' and the anagram will not come out.

25 across MOOR

Usually high area's right next to low (4)

Definition: Usually high area · charade

A moor is rough open upland, the high ground of the Pennines and Dartmoor, and 'usually' allows for the boggy low-lying sort.

  • low → MOO cattle low, and the noise they make is spelt this way
  • right → R R is the standard abbreviation for right
  • next to — one thing next to another is written alongside it, so the two pieces simply run together

A weather map with an area of high pressure right beside a low.

The surface is a forecast, high pressure beside low, and 'low' is the cow instead. 'Usually' is the setter conceding that not every stretch of this ground is upland.

26 across X-RAYED

Report of attack on unknown shot inside (1-5)

Definition: shot inside · homophone + charade

Something x-rayed has been photographed from the inside out, so 'shot inside' is that picture taken as a verb.

  • unknown → X x is the unknown quantity in algebra
  • Report of attack → RAYED an attack is a raid, and 'report of' asks for the sound of the word rather than its spelling
  • on — in an across entry one thing on another is written after it, so the sounded word follows the single letter

News of an attack on someone unidentified who was shot indoors.

'Report' does two jobs: a news report of an attack, and the signal that a sound is wanted. 'Shot' is a photograph, which is the other half of the trap.

27 across SCRUTINY

Wee behind bushes avoiding bachelor's gaze (8)

Definition: gaze · charade + deletion

Scrutiny is close searching examination, so 'gaze' names it as a noun: the steady looking, not the passing glance.

  • bushes → SCRUB scrub is rough ground covered in low bushes
  • bachelor → bachelor is B, and that is the letter dropped off the end of the word above
  • Wee → TINY wee is the Scottish word for very small
  • behind — what stands behind something comes after it, so this piece is added at the end
  • avoiding — a word avoiding a letter does without it, so that letter is dropped before the rest is used

Somebody slipping behind the bushes for a wee, out of the bachelor's sight.

'Wee' is Scottish for small and the surface wants the other, cruder noun entirely. 'Gaze' has to carry the sense of a long hard look rather than a glance.

Down

2 down PLUMMET

Quickly drop crumbs in empty pot (7)

Definition: Quickly drop · container + deletion

To plummet is to fall fast and straight down, so 'quickly drop' is the same verb with the speed built into it.

  • empty pot → PT the vessel emptied of its insides keeps only its first and last letters
  • crumbs → LUMME 'crumbs!' and 'lumme!' are the same mild British cry of surprise, lumme being a worn-down 'lord love me'
  • empty — a container called empty has had everything between its walls taken out, so only the letters at the two ends are left
  • in — one thing in another sits inside it, so these letters are lodged between the two that survive beside them

Dropping crumbs into a pot with nothing in it.

'Crumbs' is not the food but the exclamation, and it is swapped for another exclamation almost nobody says any more. Both belong to a politely startled kind of British English.

3 down AEGIS

Protection English and American soldier wear equally (5)

Definition: Protection · container + charade

Under someone's aegis you are under their protection or sponsorship, the sense that outlived the shield the word started as.

  • equally → AS as tall as, as good as: 'as' is the little word that says two things measure the same, and it opens up round what it covers
  • English → E E is the standard abbreviation for English
  • American soldier → GI a GI is an American private soldier, from 'government issue'
  • wear — what you wear goes round the outside of the wearer, so the letters named after this word wrap themselves about the ones named before it

English and American troops turning out in identical kit.

'Wear' sits in the sentence as the soldiers' uniform and works as the instruction to wrap. The word itself is Greek: the aegis was the shield Zeus carried, which is why it now means somebody's protection.

4 down ERRONEOUS

Euro Senor tossed bum (9)

Definition: bum · anagram

A bum steer is wrong information, so 'bum' is the informal adjective for mistaken that this nine-letter word states formally.

  • Euro Senor → EUROSENOR anagram fodder, nine letters
  • tossed — something tossed is thrown up and lands however it lands, which is what happens to the letters it is holding

A euro tossed to a Spanish gentleman turns out to be a bad one.

'Bum' is the adjective, not the noun: a bum steer, bum information. Senor keeps a Spanish look right up to the point where it hands over ordinary letters.

5 down TAIL END

Heel pain occupies mind (4,3)

Definition: Heel · container

The heel of a loaf is its very last piece, and the tail end of anything is that same final scrap.

  • mind → TEND to mind a shop or a child is to tend it
  • pain → AIL whatever ails you is giving you pain, and the verb supplies three letters
  • occupies — to occupy a space is to sit inside it, so these letters take up room in the middle of the word beside them

An aching heel filling somebody's thoughts.

'Heel' is the last inch of a loaf, not the back of a foot, which is how a two-word answer comes to define it. 'Mind' and 'tend' are the same verb of looking after.

6 down CUFFS

Copper that takes the biscuit showing restraint? (5)

Definition: showing restraint? · charade

Cuffs are handcuffs, the restraints an officer carries, and the question mark flags the pun: they show restraint by holding somebody still.

  • Copper → CU Cu is the chemical symbol for copper, from the Latin cuprum
  • that takes the biscuit → FFS something that takes the biscuit beats everything, and FFS is the texted expletive of exasperation fired back at it

A policeman who is the absolute limit, yet holding back.

Copper is the metal, not the policeman the surface is selling, and the second half is not a definition at all but a whole exclamation shortened the way a phone would shorten it.

7 down VERSATILE

Rivals tee off, good all round (9)

Definition: good all round · anagram

Someone versatile can turn a hand to anything, which is exactly the claim made by calling a player good all round.

  • Rivals tee → RIVALSTEE anagram fodder, nine letters
  • off — someone whose aim is off has gone astray, and letters sent astray come back in a different order

Golfers driving off the first tee in fine all-round form.

'Tee off' reads as one golfing phrase and has to be split: the tee joins the fodder and 'off' does the shuffling. 'Good all round' sounds like praise for a sportsman and is the definition.

8 down RESIDUE

University dons party after dealing with surplus (7)

Definition: surplus · container + charade

The residue is what is left in the pan once everything else has gone, and a surplus is that same leftover amount.

  • dealing with → RE re is the 'concerning' at the top of a memo: re your letter of the 3rd
  • party → SIDE a party to an argument is one side of it
  • University → U U is the standard abbreviation for university
  • dons — to don a coat is to pull it on over yourself, so what is donned finishes up on the outside of the one wearing it
  • after — one thing after another follows it, so the piece named first here is written down second

College tutors going to a party once the paperwork is done.

'Dons' is the trap: it looks like the university's teaching staff and it is the verb, putting a garment on. 'Party' is a side in a dispute rather than a celebration.

13 down CONTUMELY

Uncle Tom, terrible yankee insult (9)

Definition: insult · anagram

Contumely is insulting language or scornful abuse, so 'insult' names it as a noun, though it survives now mostly in that soliloquy.

  • Uncle Tom → UNCLETOM anagram fodder, eight letters
  • yankee → Y Y is yankee in the phonetic alphabet used over the radio
  • terrible — a terrible mess is a thing in complete disorder, and letters in disorder have to be set down in a new arrangement

Calling somebody an Uncle Tom, which is a dreadful American insult.

Every word of the surface is an insult, which hides the fact that only the letters of two of them matter. Contumely is Hamlet's word, from 'the proud man's contumely', and it means abuse heaped on someone.

15 down PROVENDER

Food for retail earlier cleared out (9)

Definition: Food · charade + outer letters

Provender is fodder, the feed put by for livestock and by extension the provisions people store, so 'food' names the general kind.

  • for → PRO the pros and cons are the fors and againsts
  • retail → VEND to vend something is to retail it, as a vending machine does
  • earlier → ER the first and last letters of the word, everything between them cleared away
  • cleared out — a room cleared out has had everything between its walls carried away, so only the two ends are left standing

A shop that sold food having been emptied earlier.

'Food for retail' reads as one phrase off a wholesaler's invoice, and the definition stops dead after the first word. Provender is the old word for fodder: feed for animals, and by extension supplies for people.

17 down TICKLER

Pedant not starting difficult puzzle (7)

Definition: difficult puzzle · deletion

A tickler is a poser, a problem that keeps niggling at you, so the noun phrase names the thing rather than describing it.

  • Pedant → STICKLER a stickler is somebody who insists on a rule down to the last detail
  • not starting → the S at the front comes off, leaving the other seven letters as they stand
  • not starting — a word not starting has not got its opening letter, so that first one never appears

A pedant who will not begin a hard crossword.

A tickler is a problem that teases you, the clue that sits unsolved half the morning, and the surface pretends the pedant is simply too grand to start one.

18 down PERHAPS

That could be a hot spring rising (7)

Definition: That could be · charade + reversal

Offer a guess, get 'that could be' back, and you have been told perhaps: both are the same non-committal maybe.

  • a → PER five pounds a day is five pounds per day, so the two words swap freely
  • hot → H H is the letter on the hot tap
  • spring → APS a spa is a spring of mineral water, and its three letters are read up the column
  • rising — a down entry read from its foot back to its head runs upwards, which is the direction this word sends the letters

A hot spring welling up out of the ground.

'A' standing for 'per' is the step solvers miss and it is worth banking, because setters use it constantly. The geyser keeps you hunting for something geological.

20 down LETDOWN

Doctor dwelt on disappointing outcome (7)

Definition: disappointing outcome · anagram

A letdown is an outcome that falls short of what was promised, the noun for the flat feeling left behind.

  • dwelt on → DWELTON anagram fodder, seven letters
  • Doctor — to doctor a document is to tamper with it, so what stands beside it comes out altered

A doctor who kept going on about a poor result.

'Doctor' is the very first word and reads as a person, which is where setters most like to hide the instruction. 'Dwelt on' is a phrase in its own right and is only letters here.

22 down TRIAD

Criminal group lasered air traffic from beneath shelters (5)

Definition: Criminal group · hidden word + reversal

A triad is one of those Chinese criminal societies, so 'criminal group' names the class it belongs to rather than defining the word.

  • lasered air traffic → TRIAD five consecutive letters run from the end of the first word across the second, and they are collected from the far end backwards
  • from beneath — a down entry picked up from its foot comes from beneath, so the letters are collected in that upward direction
  • shelters — to shelter something is to keep it hidden inside, and these words are keeping letters between them

Gangsters aiming lasers at aircraft from under cover.

'Criminal group lasered' reads as a headline about a gang being targeted, and it is really the definition followed by the start of the hiding place. Triads are the Chinese crime syndicates of Hong Kong and beyond.

23 down GAMUT

Dieter's good sandwiches are primarily meat spread (5)

Definition: spread · container + first letters

A gamut is the whole range from one end to the other, originally the full scale of notes, and a spread is that same span.

  • Dieter's good → GUT Dieter is a German name, and 'gut' is the German for good
  • are primarily meat → AM the opening letters of the two words on either side of the instruction
  • sandwiches — to sandwich something is to put it between two others, so these letters go inside the word in front of them
  • primarily — what comes primarily comes first, so only the opening letter of each word named is taken

A dieter's sandwich that turns out to be mostly meat paste.

Dieter is a man's name in German rather than somebody counting calories, and that swap is what hands over 'gut'. 'Spread' looks like more of the sandwich and means a range.
