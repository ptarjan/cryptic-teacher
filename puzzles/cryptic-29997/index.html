1 across WITCHES

Wife desires charming women … (7)

Definition: charming women · charade

A witch charms in the sense of casting spells, so 'charming women' names the trade rather than paying a compliment.

  • Wife → W W is the standard abbreviation for wife, straight off the crossword's own list
  • desires → ITCHES to itch for something is to desire it, and the verb agrees in number

A wife hankering after glamorous company, the thought running on into the next clue.

'Charming' is not flattery here but a job description: these are women who work charms, and the ellipsis hands the sentence on to 5 across.

5 across HEROISM

… valour is boring her – ordinary and primarily ‘macho’ (7)

Definition: valour · charade + container

Valour is the quality a hero shows in danger, and the answer is that quality named as a noun — a straight synonym in a formal register.

  • her → HER the clue's own word, lifted whole
  • ordinary → O O is the standard abbreviation for ordinary
  • is → IS another word taken at face value, drilled into the middle
  • primarily ‘macho’ → M the opening letter of the quoted word
  • boring — To bore is to drill a hole into something, so these letters go inside the assembly rather than beside it.
  • primarily — In the first place — take the opening letter of the word that follows and nothing more.

A woman bored by all this brave, ordinary macho posturing, the sentence carried over from 1 across.

'Boring' is doing engineering work rather than social work, and the quotation marks round 'macho' are the setter's nudge that the word is being used, not meant.

10 across ECHO

E-copy (4)

Definition: E · double definition

Radio operators say Echo for the letter E, and an echo is a sound that copies what was said — two everyday senses sitting a hyphen apart.

  • E → the letter as the radio alphabet says it, alongside Alpha, Bravo and Charlie
  • copy → to copy someone is to give back what they just said

An e-copy: the digital version of a document.

The hyphen does the whole of the misdirection, gluing two separate definitions into one modern-sounding compound that means neither of them.

11 across CINDERELLA

Tale of servitude recalled in reels (10)

Definition: Tale of servitude · anagram

The girl of the fairy tale is kept as a household drudge by her stepmother, and the story carries her name, so it defines the tale itself.

  • recalled in → RECALLEDIN anagram fodder, ten letters — the enumeration matches exactly
  • reels — Something that reels is staggering, unsteady on its feet, so the letters in front of it cannot keep their footing either.

A story of drudgery being remembered while the film spools turn.

'Recalled' looks like a reversal instruction and 'in' like a container one; both are only fodder, and the single instruction in the clue is its last word.

12 across QUINOA

One in five heading to Open are seeds (6)

Definition: seeds · charade + first letters

Quinoa is grown and eaten for its seed heads, so 'seeds' says what the crop physically is rather than describing it loosely.

  • One in five → QUIN a quin is one of a set of quintuplets — one baby in five
  • Open are → OA the first letters of these two words, in the order they stand
  • heading to — A heading is what stands at the top of something, so only the front letter of each word after it is wanted.

Tennis talk: a fifth of the players arriving at the Open are the seeded ones.

'Seeds' is the tennis word the whole surface is built for, and the definition wants the kitchen kind — the grain sold as a health food.

13 across WHITE LIE

Remain on the right side of suspicious cook – porky but harmless? (5,3)

Definition: porky but harmless · charade

A porky is a lie in rhyming slang, and the white sort is the harmless one told to spare someone's feelings rather than to deceive them.

  • suspicious cook → WHITE Mrs White is the cook in Cluedo, the British murder-mystery board game, and one of its six suspects.
  • Remain → LIE to lie somewhere is to remain there — the resting verb, nothing to do with untruth
  • on the right side of — In an across entry the right-hand side is the later half, so the piece named first ends up second in the grid.

Staying in the good books of a cook who suspects you, with a fib told to keep the peace.

'Porky' is rhyming slang — a pork pie is a lie — and the other half comes from a board game whose suspects are all named after colours.

14 across BEANSTALK

Way to find a giant, live, and chat about man’s missing head? (9)

Definition: Way to find a giant · charade + container + deletion

In the pantomime, Jack climbs the beanstalk to reach the giant's castle in the clouds, so it literally is the way to find one.

  • live → BE to be is to live — the verb of existing
  • man’s → ANS this word with its leading letter gone, apostrophe and all
  • chat → TALK a chat is a talk, and the two words swap in either as noun or verb
  • about — Round about — the pieces already assembled close around the letters named just before it.
  • missing head — A word's head is its front, so this one turns up without its first letter.

A live chat about a man who has lost his head, and how to find a giant.

Jack climbs one of these to reach the giant, so the definition names a route and not a plant. 'Missing head' belongs to the mechanism, not to the gruesome surface.

16 across STORY

Censors essentially blue tale (5)

Definition: tale · charade + middle letter

A story and a tale are the same object, a narrative told for entertainment — an everyday synonym with no crossword trickery in it.

  • Censors → S the middle letter of the seven, with three standing either side
  • blue → TORY Blue is the colour of the Conservative party in Britain, whose members are Tories.
  • essentially — The essence of a word is what sits at its very centre, so this asks for the middle letter alone.

Censors reacting to a blue tale — a smutty piece getting cut.

'Blue' means smutty in the surface and party-political in the wordplay, and the last word of the sentence is the only one doing an honest job.

17 across JUDGE

Barnaby ousts Romeo and takes Juliet – he’s a trier! (5)

Definition: a trier · substitution

A judge tries cases in court, so 'a trier' is the agent noun from that legal sense of try, not someone who makes a plucky effort.

  • Barnaby → RUDGE Barnaby Rudge is the title character of a Dickens novel, so the surname follows the first name.
  • Romeo → R R in the NATO phonetic alphabet
  • Juliet → J J in the same alphabet, which is why the two names appear side by side
  • ousts — To oust is to throw out and step into the vacated place, which is exactly what one letter does to another here.
  • takes — Takes on, adopts — the empty slot is filled by whatever is named immediately after.

A love triangle in which Barnaby elbows Romeo aside and takes up with Juliet.

Two of the three names are radio spelling-alphabet letters and one is a Dickens title — Romeo and Juliet look like a couple and are only R and J.

19 across INTENSIFY

INXS provided Yates’s first shoot up (9)

Definition: shoot up · charade + first letter

Prices and numbers that shoot up rise steeply, and to intensify is to grow rapidly stronger — the same movement, as a verb.

  • INXS → INTENS The band's name read out letter by letter: its X is the Roman numeral ten, so it spells longer than it looks.
  • provided → IF provided that = if, the conditional conjunction
  • Yates’s first → Y the opening letter of the name
  • first — The first of a name is its opening letter, and just that one letter joins the assembly.

A tabloid line about the band INXS, Yates, and a first injection.

X is ten in Roman numerals, which is what turns a four-letter band name into six letters of the answer. 'Shoot up' means to rise sharply, not what the surface implies.

23 across HUNTSMAN

Barbarian, turning good guy, with trimmed locks – he’s chasing foxy type? (8)

Definition: he’s chasing foxy type · charade + reversal + deletion

The huntsman is the rider in charge of the hounds at a fox hunt, so he is defined by the animal he spends the day chasing.

  • Barbarian → HUN the Huns are the invaders whose name became a byword for barbarian
  • good guy → TS a good guy is a saint, ST on any church noticeboard, and the pair is turned round
  • locks → MAN locks of hair are a mane, cut short by a letter
  • turning — Turning something round reverses it, and in an across entry that means those letters are read right to left.
  • trimmed — Trimming takes a bit off the end, so this word arrives one letter shorter than it started.

A barbarian going straight, getting a haircut and setting off after an attractive woman.

'Foxy type' is the joke — the surface means a good-looking person, the definition means an actual fox. A huntsman is the man who handles the hounds on a hunt.

24 across CASTLE

Move to try and cover King’s naked glee after fling (6)

Definition: Move to try and cover King · charade + middle letters

In chess, to castle is to move king and rook together so that the king ends up shielded, which is what the definition spells out.

  • fling → CAST to cast is to fling — the throwing verb, as in casting a line
  • glee → LE the inner two letters, with the outer ones taken off
  • naked — Stripped of its covering, so the word gives up its outer letters and keeps only what was underneath.
  • after — Coming along behind, so the letters named before it take the later position.

Gossip about a king's undisguised delight after an affair, and a move to cover it up.

Castling is the one chess move that shifts two pieces at once, tucking the king into a corner behind a rook, so 'cover King' describes it literally.

26 across BUSINESSES

Ventures boundless flair inspired by coaches (10)

Definition: Ventures · container + deletion

A business is a commercial venture, so the two plurals are everyday synonyms for the enterprises people set up and run.

  • coaches → BUSES a coach is a long-distance bus, and this word splits to make room
  • flair → INESS flair is finesse, arriving with its outer letters gone
  • boundless — Without bounds — the first and last letters, the ones that bound the word, are taken away.
  • inspired by — To inspire is to breathe in, so this piece is drawn inside the one named next.

Sports coaches inspiring limitless flair in the players they train.

'Coaches' are the vehicles rather than the trainers, and 'inspired' is being used in its old sense of breathing in — both words are pulling towards the sporting surface.

27 across IBEX

Once partner is following, beginning to introduce glimpse of billy goat (4)

Definition: goat · charade + first letter

An ibex is a wild mountain goat with huge curved horns, so 'goat' defines it by its family — the general word standing for the particular animal.

  • introduce → I the opening letter of the word
  • billy → B the first letter of the word for a male goat
  • Once partner → EX a partner one used to have is an ex
  • following — Following means coming along behind, so this piece takes the final position in the assembly.
  • beginning to — The beginning of a word is its opening letter, and no more than that is taken.
  • glimpse of — A glimpse is the briefest sight of something — here, just the letter it starts with.

An ex tagging along while someone starts to make the introductions.

'Billy goat' looks like a single phrase and the setter splits it down the middle: the first half is wordplay and the second is the definition.

28 across SLEEPER

One holding up the line returning skins again? (7)

Definition: One holding up the line · reversal

Railway sleepers are the crosswise beams that carry the rails, so 'one holding up the line' is a plain job description of the object.

  • skins again → REPEELS to skin a potato is to peel it, and doing it a second time puts RE on the front
  • returning — To return is to come back the way you went, so in an across entry these letters are read from the right.

Someone at the front of the queue holding everyone up, handing back peel a second time.

A sleeper is one of the timber beams laid under a railway track, so it holds the line up in the most literal way while the surface has you standing in a queue.

29 across ADVERTS

Trailers way back, after about 5½ days travelling west (7)

Definition: Trailers · charade + reversal

A trailer is the short film a cinema runs to advertise a coming release, so the two plurals name the same thing in different trades.

  • about → RE re = about, the word at the head of a memo
  • 5 → V the Roman numeral for five
  • ½ days → DA half of the word 'days', the first two of its four letters
  • way → ST a street is a way, and ST is how it is written on an envelope
  • back — Back means turned round, so the two letters of that piece land in the opposite order.
  • travelling west — West is leftwards on a map and on the grid, so this run of letters is read from its far end.

A convoy of caravans heading west after five and a half days on the road.

'Trailers' are cinema previews in the definition and caravans in the surface, and halving a written word to take its first two letters is a device worth knowing.

2 down INCLUDE

Add in what I did here, as stated (7)

Definition: Add · charade + homophone

To include something is to add it to a list or group, so 'Add' is the plain everyday verb for the same action.

  • in → IN the clue's own little word, taken at face value
  • what I did here → CLUDE The setter is talking from inside his own puzzle: what he did here was write clues, and that word said aloud comes out with its letters in this order.
  • as stated — Stated means spoken rather than written, so what follows is spelled the way it sounds instead of the way its source word looks.

The setter breaking off mid-sentence to remark on the job he has just done.

Setters refer to themselves and their own work inside clues all the time, and 'I' here is the man who wrote the puzzle you are holding.

3 down CROWN

Summit’s within Worcester, centrally, elevated (5)

Definition: Summit · hidden word + reversal

The crown of a hill or a road is its highest point, a topographical sense of the word a long way from the king's headgear.

  • within Worcester → NWORC Five consecutive letters sit across the gap between these two words, running the wrong way.
  • centrally — The letters wanted are not at either end of the phrase but in the middle of it, straddling the gap between two words.
  • elevated — Raised up, and in a down entry that means the buried letters are read from the bottom of the grid upwards.

A high point somewhere in the middle of Worcester, as though the city had a peak of its own.

Hidden runs that also need turning round are the hardest to see, and this one starts on the last letter of a word nobody rereads.

4 down ENCHANT

Use charm to control tendency to avoid pressure (7)

Definition: Use charm to control · deletion

To enchant someone is to charm them so completely that they do as you wish, which is the 'control' half of the definition.

  • tendency → PENCHANT a penchant is a leaning or tendency towards something
  • pressure → P P is the standing abbreviation for pressure, as in physics
  • to avoid — Avoiding something means keeping clear of it, so the letter named next is the one the word has to do without.

Advice on handling people: win them over rather than leaning on them.

'To avoid pressure' reads as more of the same management advice and is really a deletion order — the trap is taking the whole second half for the definition.

6 down EYELID

Barrier dropped to protect pupil (6)

Definition: Barrier dropped to protect pupil · cryptic definition

An eyelid is exactly a flap that drops to shield the pupil, so the clue is a straight description with one word quietly doing double duty.

  • protect pupil → The surface sends you to school, where a pupil is a child and a barrier coming down sounds like a safety gate.
  • Barrier dropped → The other kind of pupil is the black circle at the centre of an eye, and what drops over it is a fold of skin.

A safety gate coming down to keep a schoolchild out of harm's way.

Both halves change meaning at once: the pupil is the one in your eye, and the barrier is the shutter of skin that falls over it whenever anything comes near.

7 down OPERETTAS

Toast peer performing in musical dramas (9)

Definition: musical dramas · anagram

An operetta is a short, light opera with spoken dialogue — musical drama on a smaller scale — and both definition and answer are plural.

  • Toast peer → TOASTPEER anagram fodder, nine letters — exactly the enumeration
  • performing — Performing is what a company does with a script — the same material delivered in a fresh arrangement each night.

A titled actor being toasted for his turn on the light-opera stage.

The surface hands you a lord treading the boards, and both of its opening words are really only letters; 'in' is a joint here, not a container instruction.

8 down SOLDIER

Rise up, capturing aged warrior (7)

Definition: warrior · anagram + container

A soldier is a fighting man in an army, and 'warrior' is the older, grander word for the same trade.

  • Rise up → SIER the four letters of 'rise' rearranged, then split to make room
  • aged → OLD a plain synonym, dropped into the gap
  • up — Up as in stirred up, thrown into disarray — a loose signal for a shuffle, and the looseness is the setter's, not a failure of your solving.
  • capturing — A captor holds its prisoner within, so the shuffled letters close around the piece named after them.

A crowd rising in revolt and taking an old man prisoner.

'Rise up' reads as one phrase about rebellion, and that is what stops you seeing 'rise' as four loose letters with 'up' as the instruction to jumble them.

9 down SNOW BLINDNESS

White dominance troubling viewers (4,9)

Definition: White dominance troubling viewers · cryptic definition

Snow blindness is temporary sight loss caused by too much white glare, so the clue names its cause and its effect in the same breath.

  • White dominance → The surface reads as politics, one race on top; the setter means a landscape with nothing in it but white.
  • troubling viewers → Not an audience objecting to a broadcast — the trouble is in the eyes of anyone who looks at that landscape too long.

A headline about racial supremacy and the offence it causes the people watching.

Glare off an unbroken snowfield burns the surface of the eye and takes a climber's sight away for a day or two — the condition mountaineers wear goggles against.

15 down NIGHT-TIME

Dark approaching, tenses, one male, alone at the end (5-4)

Definition: Dark · charade + last letter

Nighttime is the stretch of hours when it is dark, so 'Dark' used as a noun — the dark — stands in for it exactly.

  • approaching → NIGH nigh is the old word for near, as in 'the end is nigh'
  • tenses → TT T is grammar's abbreviation for tense, and the plural doubles it
  • one → I the Roman numeral
  • male → M M is what any form prints beside the box for male
  • alone → E the final letter of the word
  • at the end — The end of a word is its final letter, and that single letter is all this instruction hands over.

A horror-film logline: dark coming on, one man tense, and finally nobody left.

'Alone at the end' reads as a fate and works as an instruction, which is where the last letter comes from. Everything before it is a stack of one- and two-letter conventions worth owning.

18 down UNUSUAL

Odd you twice caught group of students drawn in by unframed Dali (7)

Definition: Odd · charade + container + deletion

Unusual and odd both mean out of the ordinary, and either adjective slots into the same sentence without a change of grammar.

  • you twice → U the letter whose name is 'you', and this is the first of the two
  • group of students → NUS The NUS is Britain's National Union of Students, the body college student unions belong to.
  • you twice → U the second of the pair, closing the trap behind them
  • Dali → AL the painter's name with its outer letters taken away
  • caught — To catch something is to hold it fast, so the letters either side close around whatever they have caught.
  • drawn in — Drawn in means pulled inside, saying a second time that the middle piece sits within the outer ones.
  • unframed — A frame goes round the outside of a picture, so taking it off strips the first and last letters.

A Dali canvas out of its frame, with a crowd of students pulled towards it.

'Drawn' is the painter's word doing an insertion's job and 'unframed' is his word for a deletion, which is how the art-gallery surface hides two instructions.

20 down EXCUSED

Overlooked, start off angry, swear endlessly inside (7)

Definition: Overlooked · deletion + container

To excuse an offence is to overlook it, letting it pass without penalty, and the past participle matches the answer's -ed ending.

  • angry → EXED angry is VEXED, and starting it off loses the V
  • swear → CUS to swear is to cuss, and the last letter is dropped
  • start off — Starting a word off takes its start away — the opening letter is what goes.
  • endlessly — Without an end, so the final letter comes off before the piece is used.
  • inside — The letters named just before this word go within the piece already built, not after it.

Being passed over, going red, and swearing endlessly under your breath.

'Overlooked' has two senses and the clue wants the forgiving one — a fault overlooked is a fault let off. Both deletions hide inside the tantrum the surface describes.

21 down FULLEST

Most rewarding transposing soprano flute line (7)

Definition: Most rewarding · anagram

Living life to the fullest means getting the most out of it, so 'Most rewarding' matches both the sense and the superlative ending.

  • soprano → S S is the standard abbreviation for soprano on a score
  • flute → FLUTE the word itself, five of the seven letters
  • line → L L is the standard abbreviation for line
  • transposing — Transposing is putting things into each other's places, which is what happens to the letters gathered after it.

A soprano and a flute swapping parts in an orchestral score.

The music is all surface: a singer, an instrument and a line of the score are three chunks of letters, and transposing is the musician's word for moving them.

22 down IMPEDE

This setter’s regularly upset with contrary editor’s delay (6)

Definition: delay · charade + alternate letters + reversal

To impede something is to hold it up so that it takes longer, which is what a delay does to it — one verb for another.

  • This setter’s → IM The setter writing the clue calls himself I, so this is I'M with the apostrophe dropped.
  • upset → PE the second and fourth letters of the word, taken at even steps
  • editor’s → DE an editor is ED on any masthead, and the pair is turned round
  • regularly — At a regular interval — every second letter of the word is taken and the rest left behind.
  • contrary — Contrary means running the opposite way, so these letters arrive back to front.

The setter grumbling that an awkward editor is holding everything up.

'Upset' looks like an anagram indicator and is fodder instead, handing over two of its own letters. Setters routinely appear in their clues as I.

25 down SEIZE

Catch spots orally (5)

Definition: Catch · homophone

To seize something is to catch hold of it suddenly, so 'Catch' is the plain verb for the same grabbing action.

  • spots → SEIZE To spot something is to see it, and that verb said aloud is written this way in the grid.
  • orally — By mouth — the word is handed over as a noise, so what you write in the grid is a different spelling of it.

Catching a spotty illness by mouth, as though measles were something you swallow.

'Spots' is the everyday verb for noticing rather than the marks on a face, and the pun about catching a disease is what makes the surface hold together.
