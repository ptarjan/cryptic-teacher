Across

1 across OBSESS

Occupy thoughts of bosses following reorg (6)

Definition: Occupy thoughts of · anagram

To obsess someone is to fill their head so that nothing else gets in, which is exactly occupying their thoughts.

  • bosses → BOSSES the six letters of the word for those in charge are the raw material
  • following reorg — A reorg is a reorganisation, and people who come out the other side of one are not standing where they were before.

The surface is an office memo in which everyone is thinking about the managers. 'Reorg' does double duty, sounding like corporate jargon while quietly issuing the instruction.

5 across SEANCE

Spirited gathering that's not without its knockers (6)

Definition: Spirited gathering that's not without its knockers · cryptic definition

A seance is a sitting at which a medium tries to reach the dead, so it really is a gathering of spirits, sceptics included.

  • not without its knockers → read as the idiom, this says plenty of people are ready to knock the whole business
  • Spirited gathering → the setter's reading turns both words literal: the sitters are there for the dead, and the knocking is what they listen for

Table-rapping was the Victorian medium's stock in trade, so the knockers are the guests rather than the critics, and 'spirited' only pretends to mean lively.

8 across TABLE FOR TWO

I'm often the basis for a romantic evening (5,3,3)

Definition: I'm often the basis for a romantic evening · &lit + charade

A table for two is the restaurant booking a couple makes, so the clue reads straight: it is what a romantic dinner is built around.

  • the basis → TABLE the flat surface a dinner rests on is its base
  • for → FOR the clue's own word, taken at face value
  • a romantic evening → TWO romance takes a pair, so the evening is booked for that number

Read once, the sentence is the answer talking about itself. Read again, the same words hand over the three pieces in the order they are written.

11 across STEERSMAN

Fellow directing tidying-up of mare's nest (9)

Definition: Fellow directing · anagram

The steersman is the crew member at the wheel, so 'fellow directing' names the role by the job of pointing the boat.

  • mare's nest → MARESNEST a mare's nest is a supposed discovery that turns out to be a hoax or a muddle, and its nine letters are the fodder
  • tidying-up — Tidying something up means putting it into a better order than the one it arrived in, which is the licence to rearrange.

The idiom is the whole trick: a mare's nest is already a mess, so the phrase reads as something to be tidied while quietly handing over exactly nine letters.

12 across MOSEY

Walk back expressions of agreement and contemplation (5)

Definition: Walk · charade + reversal

To mosey is to amble along in no hurry, an easy-going American verb that swaps straight in for the plain one.

  • agreement → YES the word you say when you agree to something
  • contemplation → OM om is the syllable chanted in meditation
  • back — Back tells an across entry to be read from the far end, right to left along the row.

'Walk back' looks like one phrase, either retracing your steps or taking back what you said, and the two words are doing entirely separate jobs.

13 across DIABOLO

Blood Rain, essentially hopeless diversion for children (7)

Definition: diversion for children · anagram + middle letters

A diabolo is the spinning top a child whips up and down a string strung between two sticks, so it is a plaything.

  • Blood → BLOOD the whole word goes into the mix
  • Rain, essentially → AI the two letters at the heart of the four-letter word beside it
  • essentially — The essence of a word is what sits at its very centre, so only the letters at the heart of it are kept.
  • hopeless — A hopeless muddle is one nothing can be done with, and letters in that state will not stay in the order they were given.

The italics dress the first two words as a title, and that is where the misdirection lives: they are there only to be taken apart for letters.

14 across CUSTOMS

Most people do these duties (7)

Definition: Most people do these · double definition

Customs are the practices a people keeps by habit, and the same word names the duty charged on goods crossing a border.

  • Most people do these → the habits a society keeps up without ever thinking about them
  • duties → the charge collected on goods brought into a country, and the desk where you declare them

The two halves run together into one flat sentence, which is the disguise: 'do these duties' reads as a single phrase about chores.

15 across MIDTERM

Shock trimmed for election (7)

Definition: election · anagram

In America the midterms are the congressional elections held halfway through a president's term, so the word names an election in its own right.

  • trimmed → TRIMMED the seven letters of the word for cut back are the raw material
  • Shock — A shock jolts everything loose, so whatever stands next to it is not going to keep the order it arrived in.

'Shock trimmed' reads as a haircut, a shock of hair being cut back, which is exactly why the shock is so easy to miss as the instruction.

17 across NOMADIC

Zero that are crazy; 99 drifting (7)

Definition: drifting · charade

A nomadic people moves camp from grazing to grazing with no settled home, which is drifting as a way of life.

  • Zero → NO no is the everyday word for none of something
  • crazy → MAD a plain synonym, three letters for three
  • 99 → IC crossword Roman numerals: I written before C reads as one short of a hundred

The semicolon hides the seam. '99' is the piece that stops solvers: the strict Roman for it is XCIX, but crosswords allow the shorthand.

20 across ALEPH

Antediluvian letter equipping Phoenicians, Hebrews, initially? (5)

Definition: Antediluvian letter equipping Phoenicians, Hebrews, initially? · &lit + first letters

Aleph heads the Hebrew and Phoenician alphabets, so the sentence reads straight as the ancient sign those peoples started with.

  • Antediluvian letter equipping → ALE the opening letters of these three words, read straight off
  • Phoenicians, Hebrews → PH and the initials of the two ancient peoples named
  • initially — To do a thing initially is to do it at the very start, so each word here is cut down to the one it opens with.

An &lit: the sentence describes the answer and spells it at the same time. Antediluvian means before the Flood, which is as old as an alphabet gets.

21 across BLUE-GREEN

Two tones together: silly bungler ringing phone company (4-5)

Definition: Two tones together · anagram + container

The compound names the single shade sitting between the two colours, the colour of shallow sea, so 'two tones together' is literal.

  • bungler → BLUEGRN the seven letters of the word for a clumsy fool, shaken into a new order
  • phone company → EE EE is a British mobile network, and its two letters drop into the middle
  • silly — A silly bungler is all over the place, and letters described that way are not going to stay in the order they were given.
  • ringing — To ring a thing is to make a circle round it, so what follows goes inside and the shuffled letters close around it.

'Ringing phone company' is one smooth picture doing two separate jobs: the ringing is the instruction, and the company is only its initials.

22 across TOBOGGANIST

Church musician's headed for loo for want of alternative: things will go downhill fast for her (11)

Definition: things will go downhill fast for her · charade + deletion

A tobogganist rides a toboggan down a snow slope, so going downhill fast is the sport itself rather than a run of bad luck.

  • headed for → TO when you are headed somewhere you are bound for it
  • loo → BOG bog is blunt British slang for a lavatory
  • Church musician → GANIST the player at a church keyboard, once the OR has been lifted off the front
  • for want of alternative — To be short of something is to want it, and what goes missing here is OR, the little word that offers an alternative.

The surface is an organist bolting mid-service, and the colon is the join. The definition is the whole tail of the clue, where downhill is meant literally.

23 across GRETEL

With temperature receding, hail landing on lecturer, one that's lost in the woods (6)

Definition: one that's lost in the woods · charade + reversal

Gretel and her brother Hansel are the pair abandoned in the forest in the Grimm story, so she is one lost in the woods.

  • hail → GRETE to hail someone is to greet them, and the T at the end of that welcome backs up one place
  • lecturer → L L is the standard abbreviation for a lecturer
  • temperature receding — Temperature is T in the usual abbreviations, and receding means it backs up a place, changing round with the letter in front of it.
  • landing on — Something that lands on another thing comes to rest against it, so the two pieces sit side by side in the order given.

The surface is weather landing on an academic. The definition is the fairy tale: one of two children who leave a trail of crumbs and then cannot find it.

24 across ENSURE

Disapproval, but not about warrant (6)

Definition: warrant · deletion

To warrant something is to guarantee it, and to make certain of a thing is the same promise, so both say it will hold.

  • Disapproval → CENSURE formal disapproval, delivered in a motion or a court, is a censure
  • about → C c is short for circa, the Latin for about, as printed before an uncertain date
  • but not — But not marks what has to come out: whatever is named after it is missing from the word before it.

The comma is the disguise. 'Disapproval' is a whole word to write out rather than the definition, and the definition is the single word at the end.

2 down BETTER AND BETTER

Increasingly encouraging couple at the bookies? (6,3,6)

Definition: Increasingly encouraging · charade

The phrase describes something improving by stages, and news that keeps improving is what encourages.

  • at the bookies → BETTER someone laying a bet with a bookmaker is a better
  • couple → AND to couple two things is to join them, and this is the word that does the joining
  • at the bookies → BETTER the same punter a second time, since a couple is two of them

The question mark apologises for the spelling: the person who bets is more often a bettor, and the setter leans on the older spelling to build the phrase.

3 down EMBARGO

Red light for traffic - changes to amber - go! (7)

Definition: Red light for traffic · anagram

An embargo is an official ban that halts trade, so it is the red light shown to traffic in goods rather than cars.

  • amber → AMBER the middle light of the three, taken whole
  • go → GO what the green light tells you, added on
  • changes — When something changes it does not stay as it was, so the letters standing beside the word are due for rearrangement.

A traffic-light clue using all three colours: two of them are letters to be jumbled, and the red one is the definition, because trade can be stopped as well as cars.

4 down STEAM ROOM

Hot spot whereby 'don't sweat it' is poor advice (5,4)

Definition: Hot spot whereby 'don't sweat it' is poor advice · cryptic definition

A steam room is the tiled, vapour-filled chamber at a spa, and perspiring in it is exactly what you go there to do.

  • 'don't sweat it' is poor advice → the surface offers the everyday idiom that tells you to stop worrying, and calls it bad counsel
  • Hot spot → the setter means the words literally: a cabin heated until you perspire is the whole point of going in

The joke is that a reassuring phrase is being read word by word. Where the air is thick and hot, telling someone not to perspire is advice nobody could take.

5 down SWORN

Cursed as new arguments rebutted (5)

Definition: Cursed · charade + reversal

Someone who has sworn has let fly with oaths, and swearing in that sense is cursing, so the two past participles match.

  • new → N N is the standard abbreviation for new
  • arguments → ROWS a row is a noisy quarrel, so arguments are rows
  • rebutted — To rebut a claim is to push it back, and a piece pushed back is read from its far end instead.

'Rows' is the crossword's favourite two-way word, and here it is the shouting kind rather than the seating kind. 'Cursed' plays the same game: bad luck on the surface, bad language in the answer.

6 down ARTEMIS

Goddess, Astarte, misbehaved in some measure (7)

Definition: Goddess · hidden word

Artemis is the Greek goddess of hunting and the moon, so the bare word defines her as a member of that class.

  • Astarte, misbehaved → ARTEMIS the letters wanted run on straight across the comma and into the following word
  • in some measure — In some measure is a quiet way of saying partly, so only a part of the words beside it is wanted.

Two decoys at once: Astarte is herself a goddess, and 'misbehaved' reads like an instruction to shuffle. Neither is needed, because the letters are already in order.

7 down CROSSWORD SETTER

Mad dog covers news: quite the puzzler! (9,6)

Definition: quite the puzzler! · charade + container

The person who writes a cryptic is its setter, so 'puzzler' names them by what they do to you every Sunday.

  • Mad → CROSS cross is the mild British word for angry
  • news → WORD word in the sense of tidings, as in any word from home?
  • dog → SETTER a setter is a gun dog, red or English
  • covers — To cover a thing is to lie right over it, so the pair on either side close up around the piece between them.

'Mad dog' looks like one idea and is really two words to be prised apart. The exclamation mark is Everyman describing their own trade.

9 down USED

Employed house detective to eject some characters (4)

Definition: Employed · hidden word

Something employed has been put to work, and the past participle of 'use' carries that same sense of a thing pressed into service.

  • house detective → USED the four letters wanted straddle the gap between the two words
  • eject some characters — Characters here are letters rather than people, and ejecting some of them lifts a short run straight out of what stands in front.

A house detective is a store's own security officer, so the surface is a shop throwing out troublemakers. The real instruction hides inside that picture: characters get ejected.

10 down DYES

Perhaps makes Brown edgy and tense regularly (4)

Definition: Perhaps makes Brown · charade + alternate letters

A dye colours whatever it soaks into, and brown is offered as just one example of the shade the verb might produce.

  • edgy → DY the even-numbered letters of the word for jumpy
  • tense → ES and the same count through the word for taut
  • regularly — Something done regularly happens at even intervals, so you count through the words beside it and keep every other letter.

The capital B sells you a person, a prime minister or a novelist, when Brown is only a colour. 'Edgy and tense' reads as one idea and is two separate words to count through.

14 down CONSULATE

Diplomatic office not happy with clause (9)

Definition: Diplomatic office · anagram

A consulate is the office a consul runs in a foreign city, a rank below an embassy, handling visas and citizens abroad.

  • not → NOT the little word is material rather than a negative, and all three of its letters are wanted
  • clause → CLAUSE the six letters of a section of a contract join them
  • happy — Happy does its job loosely here: it is the old colloquial sense of merry with drink, and a merry state is an unsteady one, so the letters will not stand still.

The trap is reading 'not happy' as one idea. 'Not' is fodder, not a negative, and the word after it is the instruction.

15 down MEAN

Stingy Everyman getting a round - not until the fourth (4)

Definition: Stingy · charade + regular letters

Mean in this sense is not cruel but tight-fisted, the everyday word for someone who will not put a hand in their pocket.

  • Everyman → ME the setter signs the puzzle with that name, so the word stands for whoever is writing it
  • a → A the article taken at face value, a single letter
  • round → N count into the word for a pub order and stop on its fourth letter
  • not until the fourth — The instruction is a count: nothing is taken from the word beside it until you reach its fourth letter.

Everyman writes themselves into their own puzzles regularly, and here the joke is at their own expense: the round arrives late, if it arrives at all.

16 down EXHIBIT

Former partner greeting boring tool: it's for show (7)

Definition: it's for show · charade

An exhibit is an object put out to be looked at, in a museum case or handed to a jury, so it exists purely for show.

  • Former partner → EX an ex is the partner you no longer have
  • greeting → HI the shortest hello there is
  • boring tool → BIT the bit is the drilling end of a brace or a power drill, and boring is what it does

'Boring' is the trap: it reads as dull and is really doing the drilling. The colon stands in for an equals sign.

18 down MUGGINS

This chap's pullin' faces at front of stage (7)

Definition: This chap · charade + deletion + first letter

The word is British slang for a fool, applied mostly to oneself, so 'this chap' is whoever happens to be talking.

  • pullin' faces → MUGGIN to mug is to pull faces for an audience, and the g of the -ing form goes the way of the clue's own
  • stage → S the opening letter of the word for the boards
  • pullin' — The clipped spelling is itself the instruction: write the -ing word the way the clue says it, one letter short.
  • front of — The front of a word is where it starts, so only the opening letter of the one named is taken.

The dropped g is not just accent. A clue that clips a word wants the letter clipped in the answer too, and this one is British self-mockery for the person speaking.

19 down CANE

Rod for Old Testament murderer, we're told (4)

Definition: Rod · homophone

A cane is a length of bamboo or rattan, used as a walking stick or for beating, so it is a rod in the plain sense.

  • Old Testament murderer → CANE Adam's elder son, who killed his brother Abel in Genesis; that name said aloud is written another way entirely
  • we're told — Being told something puts it in the ear, so it is the sound of the word that matters and not its spelling.

A one-step homophone, and the art is spotting which biblical name is wanted: the first murder in the book is the ground the clue stands on.

21 down BAGEL

Spooner's delivering Clark, maybe, a roll (5)

Definition: a roll · spoonerism

A bagel is the ring of dough boiled before it is baked, sold sliced for filling, so it is a roll of a particular kind.

  • Clark, maybe → BAGEL Clark Gable starred in Gone with the Wind, so a Clark may be that one; now trade the opening sounds of the surname's two syllables
  • Spooner's delivering — To deliver a line is to say it, and the Reverend Spooner was famous for saying things with the opening sounds swapped over.

'Maybe' is the signal that a Clark is only one example of the name wanted. Everyman names the reverend every time, so the swap is announced rather than hidden.
