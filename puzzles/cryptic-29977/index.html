Across

9 across DECOMPOSE

Go off Geordie TV presenter, old male model (9)

Definition: Go off · charade

Food that goes off decomposes: 'go off' is the British idiom for rotting, and it matches as a verb phrase.

  • Geordie TV presenter → DEC Dec is Declan Donnelly, the shorter half of the Newcastle double act Ant and Dec — a Geordie is someone from Tyneside
  • old → O O = old, standard abbreviation
  • male → M M = male, standard abbreviation
  • model → POSE to model for a painter or a photographer is to pose

A viewer has gone off a Geordie presenter and an ageing male model.

'Go off' is milk, not affection, which the celebrity surface works hard to hide. Ant and Dec have presented Saturday-night television together for thirty years, and setters use 'Geordie' to point at either name.

10 across DUE TO

Because of two performers, start to obfuscate (3,2)

Definition: Because of · charade + first letter

'Due to' and 'because of' are both prepositional phrases naming a cause, and either can replace the other in front of a noun.

  • two performers → DUET two performers singing together make a duet
  • start to obfuscate → O the first letter of 'obfuscate'
  • start to — 'start' asks for the opening letter of the word that follows and nothing else of it.

A pair of performers begin muddying the story.

The join falls inside a word rather than between the two: the duet's own final T ends up opening the second word of the answer, which is why the enumeration reads 3,2.

11 across AFTER

Following fart, Earth tremors (5)

Definition: Following · anagram

After and following both mean coming later than something, and both work as prepositions: after the meeting, following the meeting.

  • fart → FART four letters of raw material, taken as printed
  • Earth → E E = Earth, the astronomer's abbreviation for the planet
  • tremors — A tremor shakes things out of the places they were sitting in, and the letters beside it are what gets shaken.

A lavatorial cause and a seismic effect, in that order.

'Earth tremors' is one phrase in the surface and two separate jobs underneath — the planet donates a letter and the shaking is the instruction, which is the split the schoolboy joke is there to hide.

12 across TEMPORISE

Rate increase for stall (9)

Definition: stall · charade

To temporise is to spin things out to gain time, which is exactly what somebody stalling does — the two verbs swap directly.

  • Rate → TEMPO the rate a piece of music goes at is its tempo
  • increase → RISE an increase is a rise, as in a pay rise

A rate increase of the sort that stops something in its tracks.

Both wordplay pieces are nouns and the answer is a verb, so the one-word definition has to carry all the grammar: 'stall' is what tells you the clue has changed part of speech.

13 across EPHESUS

Man stopping agitated suspect leaving court in ancient city (7)

Definition: ancient city · anagram + deletion + container

Ephesus was a great Greek and later Roman city on the Aegean coast, ruined for centuries, so 'ancient city' names it by category.

  • suspect leaving court → SUSPE SUSPECT drops CT, the abbreviation for court
  • Man → HE a man is 'he' — the pronoun, not an abbreviation
  • stopping — Something stopping in a place has come to rest inside it, so the man's two letters halt in the middle of the rest.
  • agitated — An agitated suspect will not sit still, and the letters standing next to the word do not stay in their given order either.
  • leaving — The departure sense: what it names walks out of the fodder before anything is shuffled.

A man blocks an agitated suspect's path as they walk out of court.

Order matters: the court leaves first, and only what remains is stirred. Ephesus stood on the coast of what is now Turkey, and its temple of Artemis was one of the seven wonders.

14 across ANARCHY

Some welcoming chief’s utter lawlessness (7)

Definition: utter lawlessness · container

Anarchy is the absence of government altogether, so 'utter' is fair: not disorder within the law but no law at all.

  • Some → ANY 'some' and 'any' swap over in questions — have you some tea, have you any tea
  • chief → ARCH arch- means the principal one: archbishop, arch-enemy
  • welcoming — To welcome somebody is to take them into your house, so one word opens up and lets the other sit inside it.

A hospitable chief greets a few visitors, and total disorder follows.

ARCH for chief is the piece worth owning — the prefix in archbishop and arch-rival means the top one — and the apostrophe-s reads as a possessive when it is really standing in for 'is'.

17 across SLIER

Small untruth by rector is more cunning (5)

Definition: more cunning · charade

Slier is the comparative of sly, so it means more cunning — matching the definition in degree as well as in sense.

  • Small → S S = small, as on a clothes label
  • untruth → LIE an untruth is a lie
  • rector → R R = rector, standard abbreviation
  • by — 'by' means beside here, as in standing by the door, so what it names is set down next to what has been built already.

A little fib told in the rector's hearing.

The spelling is the whole difficulty: sly gives both slyer and slier, and only one of them can be built from the three pieces the clue hands over.

19 across ONE

Individual taking ecstasy (3)

Definition: Individual · charade

One is the pronoun for a single individual — 'the one on the left' — so the noun substitutes for it directly.

  • taking → ON somebody who is on a drug is taking it, which is how the slang runs
  • ecstasy → E E = ecstasy, the standard abbreviation for the drug

Somebody at a club has dropped a pill.

Two words of clue, two pieces of wordplay and a definition — the sort of miniature where the drug slang and the letters happen to say the same thing.

20 across SHELL

Husband visiting market in Hull (5)

Definition: Hull · container

A hull is the outer casing — of a boat, or of a seed or a nut — and a shell is that same hard covering round the contents.

  • market → SELL to market something is to sell it
  • Husband → H H = husband, standard abbreviation
  • visiting — A visitor goes inside the place they are visiting, so the letter named lands in the middle of the other word.

A husband doing the rounds of a market in a Yorkshire port.

Hull is a city on the Humber, which is what the capital letter and the whole sentence want you to think — and the definition is the ordinary noun, the casing round a nut.

21 across MALTOSE

Most ale breaks down sugar (7)

Definition: sugar · anagram

Maltose is malt sugar, the one starch turns into when grain is mashed, so 'sugar' names the chemical family it belongs to.

  • Most ale → MOSTALE seven letters of raw material, the two words run together
  • breaks down — Whatever breaks down comes apart into its separate pieces, and pieces can be put back together in another order.

Beer being digested, or a brew going bad in the barrel.

The chemistry in the surface is true, which is what makes it so easy to read straight past: malt sugar is precisely what ale is brewed out of, so fodder and answer describe one drink.

22 across CHOLERA

Sickness resulting from excrement, mostly, around hovel (7)

Definition: Sickness · container + deletion

Cholera is an acute infection of the gut spread by dirty water, so 'sickness' is the general word standing in for the specific disease.

  • excrement, mostly → CRA CRAP without its last letter
  • hovel → HOLE a hole is a hovel — the word you use of somewhere miserable to live
  • mostly — 'mostly' asks for most of the word and not all of it, so the final letter is left behind.
  • around — Something placed around a thing closes on both sides of it, so these letters end up either side of the hovel's.

An illness picked up from filthy sanitation in a slum dwelling.

The surface is medically accurate — the disease really does travel in contaminated waste — and a clue that tells the truth is the hardest kind to notice you are being given wordplay in.

24 across BONAPARTE

Perhaps rib cracked by a silly prat in royal family (9)

Definition: royal family · container + anagram

The Bonapartes were the French imperial dynasty Napoleon founded, so the surname names a royal family — by example, since it is one house among several.

  • Perhaps rib → BONE a rib is one bone among many, which is what 'perhaps' is flagging
  • a silly prat → APART those five letters put into another order
  • cracked by — A cracked bone splits open, and what splits open here leaves a gap for the other letters to sit in.
  • silly — Silly behaviour is not orderly behaviour, so the letters next to the word come out in no sensible arrangement.

Someone's rib gets broken by an idiot who has got in among the royals.

'Royal family' sends you to the Windsors and the answer is French: Napoleon's people, emperors rather than kings, which is the geography the clue quietly relies on.

26 across DOING

Working at home is boring Harry (5)

Definition: Working · container

Doing and working match as gerunds: what are you doing and what are you working at name the same activity.

  • Harry → DOG to harry somebody is to dog them, following and pestering
  • at home → IN somebody at home is in
  • boring — To bore is to drill a hole into something, so the word sends what stands beside it into the middle.

Working from home is dull for Harry.

Harry is a verb wearing a capital letter, as setters like it to — to harry is to hound — and 'boring' is an instruction rather than a complaint about the job.

28 across OTHER

Different native losing millions (5)

Definition: Different · deletion

Other means not this one, which is what different means when two things are being contrasted: the other way round, a different way round.

  • native → MOTHER a mother tongue is a native tongue, and a mother country a native one
  • millions → M M = millions, and this is the letter that goes
  • losing — Something losing a part no longer has it, so the letter named next drops out of the word before it.

A local man is losing millions.

'Native' as an adjective is the step to see — mother tongue, mother country — and once that word is on the page a single letter leaves it.

29 across PLAINTIFF

Claimant’s straightforward argument (9)

Definition: Claimant · charade

The plaintiff is the party bringing a civil action, the one making the claim, so 'claimant' is the plainer English word for the same role.

  • straightforward → PLAIN plain speaking is straightforward speaking
  • argument → TIFF a tiff is a small argument, the domestic kind

A claimant putting a simple case to the court.

'Argument' means a case in the legal surface and a squabble in the wordplay, and the apostrophe-s that looks possessive is standing in for 'is'.

Down

1 down IDEA

Suggestion from middleman oddly ignored (4)

Definition: Suggestion · alternate letters

An idea put to somebody is a suggestion — the everyday sense of a proposal offered for consideration rather than a thought kept to yourself.

  • middleman → IDEA take the second, fourth, sixth and eighth letters of this word, in the order they come
  • oddly ignored — 'oddly' counts positions rather than describing behaviour, and 'ignored' throws those positions away, leaving every second letter standing.

A go-between offers a proposal and nobody pays the slightest attention to it.

A middleman is exactly the sort of person whose advice gets waved away, which is what keeps 'oddly ignored' reading as rudeness instead of as an instruction about letter positions.

2 down SCOTCH

Destroy spirit (6)

Definition: Destroy · double definition

To scotch a rumour or a plan is to destroy it, and Scotch is whisky — the setter drops the capital letter that the drink normally carries.

  • Destroy → the verb you use of a rumour or a plan you have put a decisive end to
  • spirit → the drinks-cabinet sense: the distilled stuff, not gin or brandy but the Scottish one

Crushing somebody's morale, breaking their spirit.

Two words, two senses, nothing to take apart. 'Spirit' is the bottle rather than the soul, and the drink loses its capital because the clue has to start somewhere.

3 down IMPRESARIO

Showman, one playing Paris and Rome (10)

Definition: Showman · anagram

An impresario is the person who puts on operas and concerts and sells them to the public, so 'showman' names the trade.

  • one → I one = I, the Roman numeral
  • Paris and Rome → PARISROME nine more letters of raw material, the two city names taken together
  • playing — An actor playing a part comes on as somebody other than themselves, and letters told to play arrive in a shape that is not their own.

A performer touring two great European capitals with the same production.

Paris and Rome look like the tour dates a showman would boast about, and they are the letters instead — which is the whole disguise, since a clue about theatre is entitled to mention cities.

4 down FOETUS

Supply of suet for juvenile wannabe? (6)

Definition: juvenile wannabe? · anagram

A foetus is not a juvenile yet — 'wannabe' is doing the work, since it is a would-be child, still waiting to be born into the rank named.

  • of suet → OFSUET six letters of raw material, the little word counted in
  • Supply — Not the noun about deliveries: read it as the adverb of supple, meaning limberly, and something limber bends into whatever shape you like.

A butcher hands over suet to a youngster with ambitions in the kitchen.

'Supply' opens the clue looking like a noun about provisions, and the question mark is the setter admitting the definition is a joke about ambition rather than a dictionary entry.

5 down PERMEATE

Infiltrate a couple’s enthralling exchange at last (8)

Definition: Infiltrate · charade + container + last letter

To permeate something is to spread right through every part of it, which is what an infiltrator does to an organisation — both verbs mean getting in everywhere.

  • a → PER 'per' stands in for 'a': five pounds per head is five pounds a head
  • couple → MATE to couple is to mate — animals do it in every nature documentary
  • exchange at last → E the final letter of 'exchange'
  • enthralling — A thrall was a captive, so to enthral is to hold somebody fast, and what enthrals here keeps another letter shut inside it.
  • at last — 'at last' points at the end of the word in front of it, so only that word's final letter is wanted.

A couple trade a spellbinding last word and somebody works their way into the middle of it.

PER for 'a' is the piece worth keeping — a wage per week is a wage a week — and 'enthralling' reads as praise for the exchange rather than as the order it is.

6 down EDDO

Revolutionary cut more unusual root vegetable (4)

Definition: root vegetable · reversal + deletion

An eddo is the starchy tuber of the taro plant, a staple in Caribbean and West African cooking, so 'root vegetable' names its category.

  • more unusual → ODDER more unusual = odder
  • cut → ODDE the R is trimmed off the end, leaving four letters to turn round
  • Revolutionary — A revolution takes a thing full circle, so the letters are read back the other way rather than as printed.
  • cut — The barber's sense: take something off the end, which for a word means losing its final letter.

A radical trims back a strange-looking root on the vegetable plot.

'Revolutionary' is a turn, not a person, despite a surface full of politics. The vegetable is one Caribbean and West African cooks know well and British supermarkets label as taro.

7 down MEDIOCRE

Average doctor taking nothing on (8)

Definition: Average · container + charade

Average here means no better than the middle of the pack, the faintly disappointing sense — which is the only sense mediocre has.

  • doctor → MEDIC a medic is a doctor
  • nothing → O O is nothing, the zero written as a ring
  • on → RE RE = on the subject of, the way a memo heading uses it
  • taking — The swallowing sense: what a doctor takes goes inside them, so the letter named next is swallowed by the word for doctor.

A doctor accepts nothing at all on top of what they are already owed.

RE for 'on' is the memo convention — 'Re: the meeting' means on the subject of the meeting — and it is the last two letters solvers usually find here.

8 down POSE

Claim written language doesn’t include the letter R (4)

Definition: Claim · deletion

To pose as a doctor is to claim to be one, so 'claim' is the verb of pretence rather than of demanding what you are owed.

  • written language → PROSE prose is written language, as opposed to verse
  • the letter R → R the setter names the letter outright, and this is the one that goes
  • doesn’t include — It reads as a flat statement about the language and works as an order: whatever is named after it has to come out.

A pedant insisting that written language leaves the letter R out.

The clue asserts something plainly untrue about English, and that flat wrongness is the disguise — it is not a fact at all but an instruction to remove a letter.

13 down EPSOM

Where to find classic content to keep, somehow (5)

Definition: Where to find classic · hidden word

Epsom Downs is the Surrey racecourse that stages the Derby and the Oaks, two of British flat racing's five Classics, so it is literally where a Classic is found.

  • keep, somehow → EPSOM five letters in an unbroken run across this span, the comma counting for nothing
  • content to — 'content' is the noun — the stuff held inside a thing — so what is wanted sits within the words that follow rather than being described by them.

A tip about where to track down classic material worth hanging on to.

'Classic content' reads as old films or records worth archiving, and a Classic is a horse race — one of five, two of them run on the Surrey downs every summer.

15 down ABSCONDING

Quickly leaving a relationship to keep some credibility, primarily (10)

Definition: Quickly leaving · container + first letters

Absconding is going off in a hurry and without permission, so 'quickly leaving' matches it as an -ing phrase, with the sneaking away left implied.

  • a → A used literally
  • relationship → BONDING bonding is what a relationship consists of — forming an attachment
  • some credibility, primarily → SC the first letters of those two words
  • to keep — To keep a thing is to hold on to it, so this word closes around what comes next and holds it in.
  • primarily — 'primarily' means first of all, so only the opening letters of the two words after it are wanted.

Somebody walks out of a relationship fast, trying to keep a shred of credibility intact.

The definition stops after two words even though 'leaving a relationship' reads as a single idea, and that overrun is the trap: the relationship is raw material, not meaning.

16 down YALTA

Crimean War conference site boosted at city close to Turkey (5)

Definition: Crimean War conference site · charade + last letter + reversal

Yalta, on the Crimean coast, hosted the February 1945 summit of Churchill, Roosevelt and Stalin — a conference held in Crimea during a war.

  • at → AT used literally
  • city → LA LA = Los Angeles, the city setters reach for first
  • close to Turkey → Y the last letter of 'Turkey'
  • boosted — To boost a thing is to shove it upwards, and upwards in a down entry means the letters are read back towards the top of the grid.
  • close to — 'close' is the ending sense, as in the close of play, so it takes the final letter of the word after it.

A wartime summit venue being talked up in a city near the Turkish border.

'Crimean War' invites the 1850s and the Charge of the Light Brigade; the conference meant here is the 1945 one, held in Crimea in the middle of an altogether different war.

18 down IOLANTHE

Musical show Oscar’s promoted in one advance article (8)

Definition: Musical show · charade + reversal

Iolanthe is Gilbert and Sullivan's comic opera about a fairy and the House of Lords, staged constantly by amateur societies, so 'musical show' names its kind.

  • one → I one = I, the Roman numeral
  • advance → LOAN an advance of money is a loan
  • article → THE 'the' is the definite article of English grammar
  • Oscar’s promoted — Oscar is O in the phonetic alphabet, and promoting a letter in a down entry lifts it one place up the grid, past whatever sat above it.

Oscar has gone up in the world since the advance notice for the show came out.

Only one letter moves, and 'Oscar' names it rather than referring to a person or a statuette. Gilbert and Sullivan wrote the piece in 1882, poking fun at the peerage.

19 down OVERRIPE

I reprove criminal on the way out (8)

Definition: on the way out · anagram

Fruit that is overripe is past its best and about to spoil, which is what 'on the way out' says as an idiom for something in decline.

  • I reprove → IREPROVE eight letters of raw material, the setter's 'I' counted in
  • criminal — A criminal is somebody who will not keep to the rules, and the letters beside the word will not keep to their order.

The setter tells off a crook who is already halfway to the door.

'On the way out' looks like a man leaving and means past its best — fruit rather than people. 'I' is the setter, and here it is fodder rather than a pronoun to read past.

22 down CAESAR

Emperor announced what guards should do to escaping woman? (6)

Definition: Emperor · homophone

Caesar became the title every Roman emperor took after Julius, and the root of both Kaiser and Tsar, so 'Emperor' defines it by rank.

  • what guards should do to escaping woman? → CAESAR guards watching a woman make a run for it would grab her — say that order aloud and write down what you hear
  • announced — Something announced is said out loud, so what follows describes a sound and not a spelling.

Guards being told what to do about a woman making a break for it.

The question mark marks the join between the two halves of the sound, which arrive as two words and land as one name.

23 down ELICIT

Draw Forth Bridge’s terminus, OK? (6)

Definition: Draw · charade + last letter

To elicit an answer is to draw it out of somebody, so 'draw' is the verb in its extracting sense rather than its sketching one.

  • Forth Bridge’s terminus → E the final letter of the name — the Forth Bridge is the great red railway crossing near Edinburgh
  • OK → LICIT licit means permitted, lawful — the word illicit is built on it
  • terminus — A terminus is the end of the line, so it asks for the last letter of what comes before it.

Somebody asks whether it is all right to sketch the far end of the Forth Bridge.

'Draw forth' is the phrase the surface offers and it is a word too long: Forth belongs to the bridge, which hands over a single letter.

24 down BOOK

Jeer King James, perhaps (4)

Definition: James, perhaps · charade

James is one of the letters of the New Testament, so it defines by example — a particular book standing in for the category.

  • Jeer → BOO to jeer at a performer is to boo
  • King → K K = king, as on a chessboard or a playing card

A crowd jeering King James.

'King James' is one phrase on the surface and two separate jobs underneath, and the King James Bible sitting behind the name is the setter's joke rather than the parsing.

25 down PARK

Pressure on animal sanctuary to provide recreation area (4)

Definition: recreation area · charade

A park is public ground kept open for leisure, so 'recreation area' describes what one is for rather than offering a synonym.

  • Pressure → P P = pressure, the physicist's abbreviation
  • animal sanctuary → ARK Noah's ark took two of every animal aboard and kept them safe from the flood
  • on — In a down entry 'on' stacks one piece on top of another, so the letter named sits above what follows.

Campaigners lean on an animal shelter to open a recreation ground.

'Pressure on' reads as one phrase and it is two: the noun gives a letter, and the little word that follows tells you where to put it.

27 down GIFT

Present introduction from Guardian and two shelfmates (4)

Definition: Present · charade + first letter

A gift is a present in the plainest sense: the thing handed over freely, with nothing to choose between the two words.

  • introduction from Guardian → G the first letter of the paper's name
  • two shelfmates → I the i is a British national daily, filed on the same newsagent's shelf
  • two shelfmates → FT the FT is the Financial Times, the pink business daily — the second neighbour on that shelf
  • introduction from — An introduction is what comes at the front, so this takes the opening letter of the name after it.

A present, announced by this paper along with the two sold beside it.

The setter is pointing at a newsagent's rack: the i and the FT are the two national dailies filed next to the paper the puzzle appears in, and naming its own masthead is the joke.
