Guardian Cryptic Crossword No 30,031

Across

1 across VIDEOCASSETTE

Detectives so worried about a former data-storage platform (13)

Definition: former data-storage platform · anagram

former data-storage platform → VIDEOCASSETTE: magnetic tape in a plastic shell was how households stored video before discs and downloads, so the platform is squarely a former one.

  • Detectives so → DETECTIVESSO twelve letters of fodder, handed over in plain sight
  • a → A the thirteenth letter, joined to the fodder
  • worried about — A thing worried about is fretted over and chewed at, and letters chewed at come back in a different order.

Detectives are fretting over an obsolete piece of kit that might still be holding the evidence.

The count is the way in: 'Detectives so' plus that stray 'a' is exactly thirteen letters, and the surface reads so smoothly as police anxiety that nobody counts them.

10 across XYLOPHONE

Set of wooden bars cut between axes and whetstone (9)

Definition: Set of wooden bars · charade

Set of wooden bars → XYLOPHONE: tuned wooden bars laid in a row and struck with mallets, so the definition describes what it is made of.

  • axes → XY the x and y axes of a graph
  • cut → LOP to lop is to cut, the way you lop a branch
  • whetstone → HONE a hone is the stone a blade is sharpened on
  • between — 'Between' fixes the middle position, with a piece of wordplay on either side of the one it names.

Axes and a whetstone, with something being cut in a woodshed.

'Axes' is the pivot: the surface wants chopping tools to keep company with the whetstone, and the clue means the pair of lines on a graph.

11 across OBESE

Stout or beer seen regularly (5)

Definition: Stout · alternate letters

Stout → OBESE: stout is the old polite adjective for heavily overweight, and the surface pushes you towards the glass instead.

  • or beer seen → OBESE start at the first letter and take every second one across the three words
  • seen regularly — Seen at regular intervals means every other letter across the run, not the whole of it.

A drinker's preference at the bar, and how often it gets ordered.

'Stout' is the definition dressed as a drink, and the beer either side keeps it in costume. Alternate letters is one of the few devices that leaves the clue reading as ordinary English.

12 across OUNCE

Cat bound to scratch head (5)

Definition: Cat · deletion

Cat → OUNCE: the ounce is the snow leopard of central Asia, so the plain word 'cat' defines it as one particular member of the family.

  • bound → BOUNCE to bound is to bounce, the same leap
  • scratch head → B the head of BOUNCE is its B, and that is the letter struck out
  • scratch head — To scratch something is to strike it out, and the head of a word is its front letter.

A cat gathers itself to leap and takes a swipe at somebody's head.

Losing a first letter is the whole mechanism, and 'scratch' performs it while looking like something a cat would do. The ounce is the snow leopard, a name that survives mostly in crosswords.

13 across EMBROIDER

Elaborate cycling honour and ring cyclist maybe accepts (9)

Definition: Elaborate · charade + cycling + container

Elaborate → EMBROIDER: the verb for adding decorative stitching also means adding invented detail to a story, which is exactly what elaborating a tale is.

  • cycling honour → EMB the MBE, Member of the Order of the British Empire, with its last letter brought round to the front
  • cyclist maybe → RIDER a cyclist is one sort of rider
  • ring → O a ring is a circle, written as the letter O
  • cycling — Cycling sends letters round a loop: the one on the end comes off and rejoins at the front.
  • maybe — 'Maybe' marks an example — the person named is one particular kind of the wider word wanted.
  • accepts — Whoever accepts something takes it inside, so these letters open up and make room for the other piece.

An award and a ring are handed over to a cyclist at the end of a race.

MBE is the honour crosswords hand out most often, and here its three letters roll round one place. 'Elaborate' is being used as a verb, not the adjective the surface makes it look like.

14 across DITHERY

Hesitant setter would back lines on article (7)

Definition: Hesitant · charade + reversal

Hesitant → DITHERY: somebody dithery cannot settle on a decision, which is the wavering that 'hesitant' describes, in a more colloquial adjective.

  • setter would back → DI the setter is I and 'would' contracts to 'd, and I'D is turned round
  • article → THE the definite article
  • lines → RY RY is the railway, the crossword's usual lines
  • back — 'Back' is the about-turn, and in an across entry it means those letters run the other way along the row.
  • on — In an across clue 'on' means resting after, so the lines come along behind the article rather than in front of it.

A hesitant writer would back down over a few lines in an article.

Setters put themselves in as I, so 'setter would' contracts to I'd before anything is reversed. 'Article' wants A, AN or THE, and the crossing letters are what decide which.

16 across DRIVING

Energetic daughter splitting (7)

Definition: Energetic · charade

Energetic → DRIVING: a driving force or driving rain is one that pushes hard and without let-up, which is the energetic sense rather than anything to do with cars.

  • daughter → D d is the standard abbreviation for daughter in family trees
  • splitting → RIVING to rive is to split timber, so riving is splitting

An energetic daughter is breaking something apart.

'Rive' is the old verb for splitting timber and survives now mostly in 'riven'. Three words is the whole clue, which leaves a solver very little to work with.

18 across NONPLUS

Confound what could be seen as negative? (7)

Definition: Confound · charade

Confound → NONPLUS: to nonplus somebody is to leave them so at a loss that they have nothing to say, which is what confounding them does.

  • what could be seen as negative → NON split in two, the front half is the prefix that denies whatever follows it
  • what could be seen as negative → PLUS and the back half is a positive quantity, so the pair together say 'not a plus'
  • what could be seen as — This is the invitation to split the letters into two words and read the pair at face value.

It reads as an instruction to defeat anything that might come across badly.

The wordplay only appears once you split the answer in two, and the question mark is Harpo conceding that the definition is loose: zero is not a plus either, and zero is not negative.

20 across OSSICLE

Peeling away front, disclose broken bone (7)

Definition: bone · deletion + anagram

bone → OSSICLE: an ossicle is any small bone, most famously the three tiny ones in the middle ear, so 'bone' is the general word standing over a particular kind.

  • front → D the front letter of DISCLOSE, the one peeled off
  • disclose → ISCLOSE what is left of DISCLOSE once that first letter has gone
  • Peeling away — Peeling takes the outside off, and what it takes here is named next: the letter at the front.
  • broken — A broken thing is in pieces, and pieces can be put back together in any order at all.

A dressing is peeled back and an injury is uncovered underneath.

'Disclose' looks like an instruction to reveal something and is the word being operated on, which is the one switch the clue turns on.

21 across LEGENDARY

Very famous cricket side totally gutted, eleven runs in 24 hours (9)

Definition: Very famous · charade + outer letters + container

Very famous → LEGENDARY: fame large enough to have passed into legend, which is why the definition needs the 'very' in front of it.

  • cricket side → LEG the leg side is one half of a cricket field, the side behind the batter's legs
  • eleven → EN ELEVEN with everything between its first and last letters taken out
  • 24 hours → DAY twenty-four hours make a day
  • runs → R R is runs on a cricket scorecard
  • totally gutted — Gutting takes the insides out, and gutting a word totally leaves only the letters at its two ends.
  • in — 'In' is the insertion: what stands before it goes inside what comes after it.

A cricket side is devastated after a day in which only eleven runs came.

'Gutted' does two jobs at once: it is the cricketer's word for devastated and the setter's word for a word emptied of its middle. Leg, meanwhile, is the on side of a cricket field.

23 across PAGES

Summons Pope intermittently filled out? (5)

Definition: Summons · alternate letters

Summons → PAGES: to page somebody is to call for them over a public-address system or a pager, and the verb takes the same third-person -s.

  • Pope intermittently filled out? → PAGES every other letter of PoPe leaves pp, the abbreviation used in book references, and pp is then written out in full
  • intermittently — Something intermittent stops and starts, so the letters are taken with gaps left between them.
  • filled out? — Filling a form out completes it, and here what gets completed is an abbreviation, written back into the word it stands for.

A form about the Pope has been filled in only here and there.

pp is the abbreviation for pages in any footnote, and 'filled out' is an instruction to write an abbreviation back out at full length — an unusual device worth filing away.

24 across SCI-FI

Literary genre evident in podcaster’s Athenian letters (3-2)

Definition: Literary genre · homophone

Literary genre → SCI-FI: science fiction is a literary genre, and the clipped form is what readers and bookshop shelves actually call it.

  • Athenian letters → SCI psi is a Greek letter, and said aloud it is 'sigh'
  • Athenian letters → FI phi is the next Greek letter along, said 'figh'
  • podcaster’s — A podcaster is heard rather than read, so the letters are wanted for the noise they make, not their spelling.

A podcaster is reading Greek letters out and a genre can be heard in them.

Athenian letters are Greek ones, and the two named are the ones whose spoken names run together into the clipped form of the genre — which is why the clue needs somebody speaking.

25 across IMPLOSION

Naughty kid is turning into fool, collapsing inwards (9)

Definition: collapsing inwards · charade + reversal + container

collapsing inwards → IMPLOSION: it is a collapse inwards under outside pressure, the exact opposite of the outward blast the word is usually paired with.

  • Naughty kid → IMP an imp is a mischievous child
  • fool → LOON a loon is a fool
  • is turning → SI the word IS, turned round
  • turning — Turning a thing round makes it read the other way about, which for two letters is quick work.
  • into — 'Into' is the insertion: what has just been turned goes inside the word that follows.

A naughty child is slowly turning into a fool.

The clue reads as one unbroken sentence about a child going to the bad, so the little word 'is' never looks like something you are meant to pick up and turn round.

26 across AFTER A FASHION

In one sense, food flipped in hot fat remains revolutionary number one (5,1,7)

Definition: In one sense · charade + anagram + reversal

In one sense → AFTER A FASHION: the phrase means 'in a way, but only just', so the definition catches the grudging, partial agreement it always carries.

  • hot fat → AFT the three letters of FAT, resettled by the heat
  • food flipped → ERAF fare is food, and flipped it runs backwards
  • remains → ASH what is left in the grate after a fire
  • revolutionary number → ION no. is number and the one beside it is I, and the revolution turns the pair round
  • hot — Fat in a hot pan spits and jumps, so nothing dropped into it stays where it was put.
  • flipped — Flip something and it lands the other way up, which for letters means the other way round.
  • revolutionary — A revolution carries a thing right round, so these letters come back facing the other way.

Somebody is frying food in a pan of hot fat and scraping up whatever is left behind.

'Remains' is the pivot: the surface wants the greasy residue in the pan, and the clue wants what a fire leaves in the grate. Every other word keeps up the cooking picture while giving instructions.

Down

2 down ISLINGTON

Harpo pitches books around north London borough (9)

Definition: London borough · charade + reversal

London borough → ISLINGTON: it is one of the thirty-two boroughs, the inner-north one around the Angel and Upper Street.

  • Harpo → I the setter's pen name, and a setter talking about himself is 'I'
  • pitches → SLING to pitch something is to sling it, so 'Harpo pitches' becomes 'I sling'
  • books → OT the OT, the Old Testament, is the crossword's usual set of books
  • north → N N is north on any compass
  • around — Turn a thing around and it faces you the other way, so the two letters before it are written out backwards.

Harpo is chucking books about somewhere in the northern half of London.

Setters write themselves into their own clues as 'I', which is what makes 'Harpo pitches' a first-person sentence. Books in a crossword are nearly always one of the two Testaments.

3 down ELPEE

Some steeplejack’s climbing record (5)

Definition: record · hidden word + reversal

record → ELPEE: it is LP written as it sounds, the twelve-inch long-playing disc, so 'record' is the object on the turntable rather than an achievement.

  • steeplejack’s → ELPEE five consecutive letters sit inside this word, running the other way
  • Some — 'Some' asks for a part of what follows rather than the whole of it, and the part wanted is a consecutive run.
  • climbing — In a down clue, something climbing is read upwards, so the letters are collected from the foot of the run towards its top.

A steeplejack is climbing higher than anyone has climbed before.

Crosswords sometimes spell a two-letter abbreviation out as though it were a word — LP becomes ELPEE — and here nothing announces it, so the five-letter count is the only warning you get.

4 down COOKERY

I’m here to host two kings separately for cuisine (7)

Definition: cuisine · container

cuisine → COOKERY: both words name the practice of preparing food, 'cuisine' being the borrowed word a menu uses and the answer the plain English one.

  • I’m here → COOEY cooey is the call you send up to say you have arrived
  • two kings → K K is the king on a chessboard and in a pack of cards
  • two kings → R R is Rex, Latin for king, the second of the pair
  • host — To host is to take somebody into your house, so this word opens up and keeps the guests inside it.
  • separately — The two guests do not arrive together: each king is slotted into a different gap.

Someone announces they are home and offers to put up two visiting kings in separate rooms.

'I'm here' is a shout rather than a statement — cooey is what you call coming through a friend's door. Kings arrive in two crossword flavours, chess's K and Latin's R.

5 down SEEDBED

Fostering environment created by dame and duke supporting diocese (7)

Definition: Fostering environment · charade

Fostering environment → SEEDBED: it is the prepared ground where seedlings are raised before transplanting, hence any place that brings new things on.

  • diocese → SEE a bishop's see is his diocese
  • dame → DBE a dame holds the DBE, Dame Commander of the Order of the British Empire
  • duke → D D is the standard abbreviation for duke
  • supporting — Whatever supports something sits under it, and in a down clue under means later in the entry.

A dame and a duke are throwing their weight behind the local diocese.

British honours are a Guardian staple, and a dame's is the DBE, worth learning beside the MBE and OBE. The surface has the two grandees propping up a diocese; the clue has them propping up letters.

6 down ECONOMICS

Chiefly moot ‘science’, for the most part blurry? (9)

Definition: Chiefly moot ‘science’, for the most part blurry? · &lit + anagram + deletion

the whole clue → ECONOMICS: read straight it is the standing jibe at the subject, much of it disputed and its forecasts famously unclear.

  • Chiefly moot → MOO MOOT keeps its chief part and loses the T
  • ‘science’, for the most part → SCIENC SCIENCE with its final letter left off
  • Chiefly — Chiefly means for the greater part, so the greater part of the word beside it is what you keep.
  • for the most part — Most of the word and not all of it — the same trimming again, this time on the noun in quotes.
  • blurry? — Something blurry has no clear edges, so its letters cannot be trusted to stay in the order printed.

Somebody is dismissing a subject as a largely pointless 'science' that nobody can see clearly anyway.

Every word does double duty here: the sentence you take the letters from is the same sentence that defines, which is why nothing is left over to point at as a definition.

7 down TWEED

With time, perhaps dock material (5)

Definition: material · charade

material → TWEED: it is the rough woollen cloth of jackets and flat caps, so 'material' is the plain fabric sense of the word.

  • time → T t is the standard abbreviation for time
  • dock → WEED dock is the broad-leaved hedgerow plant whose leaf you rub on a nettle sting
  • perhaps — 'Perhaps' flags an example: the plant named is one instance of the broader word the letters want.

Given enough time, a dockside might turn out to be worth something.

'Dock' is the trap: it wants to be a harbour, and it is the coarse green thing growing beside the nettles. 'Perhaps' is quietly doing the work of 'for example'.

8 down OXFORD ENGLISH

Shoe tongue, one that’s a bit stiff? (6,7)

Definition: one that’s a bit stiff? · charade

one that’s a bit stiff? → OXFORD ENGLISH: the prestige standard form of the language sounds formal and buttoned-up, which is what 'a bit stiff' is getting at.

  • Shoe → OXFORD an Oxford is the plain lace-up shoe
  • tongue → ENGLISH a tongue is a language, the sense in 'mother tongue'

The clue reads as a description of a shoe's tongue that has gone hard with age.

A shoe has a tongue, so the surface never breaks step and both halves hide inside one picture. The definition is where the joke lands: stiff meaning starchy and formal, not stiffened leather.

9 down RETROGRESSING

Poor girl almost regrets son going back (13)

Definition: going back · anagram + deletion

going back → RETROGRESSING: the verb means moving back towards an earlier and worse state, and the -ing form drops straight into the phrase.

  • girl almost → GIR GIRL one letter short
  • regrets → REGRETS seven letters of fodder, taken as printed
  • son → SON three more letters, taken whole
  • Poor — Something poor is in a bad way, and letters in a bad way are not in the order they should be.
  • almost — 'Almost' stops just short of the end, so the final letter of the word beside it never arrives.

A girl in a bad way is almost sorry that her son is leaving home.

'Going back' sits at the end of the clue where a reversal instruction usually goes, and that is the trap: nothing is turned round here, the whole heap is shuffled.

15 down EGLANTINE

Fragrant plant, say, a linnet damaged (9)

Definition: Fragrant plant · anagram

Fragrant plant → EGLANTINE: it is the sweetbriar, a wild rose whose leaves smell of apples, so it is named for its scent rather than its bloom.

  • say → EG e.g. is 'say', the standard short form
  • a linnet → ALINNET seven letters of fodder, the bird taken whole with its article
  • damaged — Something damaged has been knocked out of shape, and letters knocked out of shape do not stay in their order.

A linnet has been at a scented plant in the garden and made a mess of it.

'Say' looks like the setter clearing his throat and it is two letters of the fodder, the same eg you write in a list. A linnet, meanwhile, is a small British finch.

17 down INCOGNITO

Having ditched ‘Mike’, coming into new assumed identity (9)

Definition: assumed identity · anagram + deletion

assumed identity → INCOGNITO: as a noun the word means the disguise itself, the borrowed name somebody travels under.

  • ‘Mike’ → M Mike is M in the radio alphabet, and it is the letter thrown out
  • coming into → COINGINTO COMING INTO, nine letters once the M has gone
  • Having ditched — Ditching a thing throws it out, so the letter named in quotes is dropped before anything else happens.
  • new — New means made afresh, and letters made afresh come back in a different arrangement.

Somebody drops the name Mike and takes on a fresh identity.

Mike is the radio alphabet's M — Alpha, Bravo, Charlie and so on — and quotation marks round a name are the usual signal that only the letter is wanted.

19 down STAMINA

Dispelling onset of ennui, inspires rising energy (7)

Definition: energy · deletion + reversal

energy → STAMINA: it is staying power, the energy that keeps you going over distance rather than the sudden burst kind.

  • inspires → ANIMATES to inspire somebody is to animate them
  • onset of ennui → E the first letter of ENNUI, and the one dispelled
  • Dispelling — To dispel is to drive away, so the letter it points at is driven out of the longer word.
  • onset of — The onset is the beginning, so only the first letter of the word beside it is meant.
  • rising — In a down clue, letters rising are read upwards, from the foot of the entry towards the top.

Shaking off the first stirrings of boredom gives somebody a lift.

Two long words sit next to each other and only one of them is fodder: 'inspires' is the word being worked on, while 'dispelling' and 'rising' are both instructions dressed as description.

20 down OLYMPIA

Half of African country welcomes retiring setter’s large source of games? (7)

Definition: source of games? · deletion + reversal + container

source of games? → OLYMPIA: the sanctuary in the western Peloponnese where the ancient Games were held every four years, so it is where the whole idea of them started.

  • Half of African country → OPIA the back half of ETHIOPIA
  • setter’s → MY the setter's word for what belongs to him
  • large → L L is large on a clothing label
  • Half of — Only half the name is wanted, and the half that survives here is the four letters at its end.
  • welcomes — To welcome somebody is to take them in, so this word opens up and holds the other piece inside.
  • retiring — Retiring is withdrawing, going back the way you came, so those letters read in reverse.

An African country makes room for a shy setter and his outsize something.

'Setter's' is the possessive here, MY rather than the usual I or ME, and that is the step most solvers walk past. Ethiopia gives up only its second half.

22 down GRIEF

Composer lowering final note causes trouble (5)

Definition: trouble · substitution

trouble → GRIEF: not the bereavement sense but the colloquial one in 'don't give me any grief', where the word means hassle and bother.

  • Composer → GRIEG Edvard Grieg, the Norwegian who wrote the Peer Gynt music
  • final note → F the composer's last letter is G, itself a note, and a step below G is F
  • lowering — Lowering a note takes it one step down the scale, so the letter naming it is replaced by the letter below.

A composer takes his closing note down a step and it causes no end of bother.

Musical letter-names run only from A to G, which is the fact the whole clue turns on: there is exactly one place a G can go when it is lowered.

23 down POOCH

Dog muck held up by child (5)

Definition: Dog · charade

Dog → POOCH: affectionate slang for somebody's pet dog rather than a word for the species, so the register of the definition is deliberately flat.

  • muck → POO the nursery word for muck
  • child → CH ch is the standard abbreviation for child
  • held up by — In a down clue whatever does the holding sits underneath, so the piece being held rides above it.

Something nasty is being carried aloft by a small child.

'Held up by' looks like a reversal instruction and is a support instruction: in a down entry the thing doing the holding goes below the thing it holds.
