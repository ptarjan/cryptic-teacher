Across

1 across AGROCHEMICAL

Pharma giant getting into development of magical pesticide, perhaps (12)

Definition: pesticide, perhaps · anagram + container

A pesticide is one kind of agrochemical, the umbrella word for the chemicals farming uses, so 'perhaps' flags a definition by example.

  • development of magical → AGMICAL seven letters reordered, then split open to take a passenger
  • Pharma giant → ROCHE Roche of Basel is one of the world's largest drug companies
  • development of — Developing a site means pulling it about and putting it back another way, which is what the letters do here.
  • getting into — A company that gets into something has moved inside it, so these five letters land in the middle of the rest.

The surface sells a magic potion and the answer is the industrial reality. When a clue says 'pharma giant' it usually wants a real company's name, and that is a habit worth acquiring.

9 across SHUTTER

Screen that opens in silence, say (7)

Definition: Screen that opens · charade

A shutter is the hinged panel fitted across a window, and its whole purpose is to swing open and shut — a screen that does exactly that.

  • silence → SH 'sh' is the sound you make to hush somebody
  • say → UTTER to say something is to utter it

'Silence, say' looks like one phrase asking for an example of quiet, and it is really two separate instructions standing side by side. The comma is the setter's only warning.

10 across ROBUSTA

Coach breaks work schedule for coffee (7)

Definition: coffee · container

Robusta is one of the two species of bean the world drinks, alongside arabica, so the plain word 'coffee' names it by its kind.

  • work schedule → ROTA a rota is the British word for the list saying who works when
  • Coach → BUS a coach is a long-distance bus
  • breaks — Something that breaks into a thing forces its way inside and splits it, which is where the shorter piece ends up.

The surface is a driver's tea break and the definition is a single word at the far end. 'Coach' is capitalised only because it opens the clue, not because it is anybody's name.

11 across EMPHASISE

Underline M representing magnitude in report (9)

Definition: Underline · homophone

Underlining a word on the page is how writing gives it extra weight, and that is precisely the job this verb names.

  • representing → FOR one thing representing another stands for it
  • magnitude → SIZE magnitude is size — how big a thing is
  • M representing magnitude → EMPHASISE the letter is said 'em', and the three spoken words run together into one
  • in report — Something given in report is being repeated as spoken, so what you are handed is a sound to write down some other way.

Nothing is anagrammed or hidden here. The clue asks you to read three words aloud and hear a fourth, and 'M for size' said at speed is the whole mechanism.

12 across LURVE

What leads Veronica into temptation? Affection, ironically (5)

Definition: Affection, ironically · container + first letter

The answer is the deliberately gushy, over-sung spelling of love, so it means affection while the misspelling supplies the irony the clue admits to.

  • temptation → LURE a lure is the bait that tempts, so the noun and it come to the same thing
  • Veronica → V the leading letter of the name
  • leads — What leads a word is the letter standing at its head, the one that goes in front of all the others.
  • into — It marks a placement rather than a journey: the single letter is dropped inside the longer word.

The clue borrows the Lord's Prayer for its surface — lead us not into temptation — and the question mark owns up to the borrowing. 'Ironically' is the setter admitting the spelling is a joke.

13 across PASTIS

Doctor sips at aperitif (6)

Definition: aperitif · anagram

Pastis is the aniseed spirit drunk before dinner in the south of France, which is exactly the job an aperitif does.

  • sips at → PASTIS six letters, taken from the two words and reordered
  • Doctor — To doctor something is to tamper with it, and letters tampered with come back rearranged.

'Doctor sips' reads as a sentence about a medic having a drink, which is the neatest kind of anagram surface: indicator and fodder disguised as subject and verb.

15 across HEIGHTEN

Intensify stock cube using chicken (8)

Definition: Intensify · container

To heighten a feeling is to raise its pitch, which is what intensifying does — the word survives mostly in this figurative sense rather than about physical height.

  • chicken → HEN a hen is the chicken of the henhouse, the female bird
  • cube → EIGHT two cubed — a cube here is arithmetic, not a shape
  • stock → surface only: it welds the cube and the chicken into a kitchen ingredient, and contributes no letters
  • using — A word that uses another has it in hand, so the shorter piece is held inside the longer one.

A chicken stock cube is exactly the picture the setter wants in your head, and 'stock' is the one word in the clue with no job to do beyond painting it.

18 across EAU DE NIL

Pale green used by teenage Auden illegibly (3,2,3)

Definition: Pale green · hidden word

Eau de nil is French for 'water of the Nile', the soft grey-green shade decorators and dressmakers have been using for a century.

  • teenage Auden illegibly → EAU DE NIL eight unbroken letters, running from the end of the first word straight through the second and into the third
  • used by — A colour used by something is being carried around inside it, so the letters are already sitting in the words that follow.

'Illegibly' is planted to look like an anagram instruction, and it cannot be one — its own letters are part of what is being concealed. The poet's name sits in the middle untouched.

19 across CENSUS

On the radio, maybe hear America's checking who's in the country (6)

Definition: checking who's in the country · homophone

A census is the official head-count of everyone resident, so it is precisely the exercise of checking who is in a country at a given moment.

  • maybe hear → SENSE hearing is one of the five senses, which is what 'maybe' flags
  • America's → US US is the everyday abbreviation for America
  • maybe hear America's → CENSUS the two words said one after the other make a single spoken word
  • On the radio — What comes over the air is heard and never seen, so the clue is describing a sound and leaving you to spell it.

The apostrophe-s does two jobs at once: it makes 'America's' possessive on the surface, and it carries the verb 'is' in the reading that matters. Everything after it is the definition.

21 across HATCH

Leave egg where food is served (5)

Definition: Leave egg · double definition

One sense is the chick breaking out of its shell; the other is the serving hatch, that opening between a kitchen and the room next door.

  • Leave egg → HATCH what a chick does when it breaks out of the shell
  • where food is served → HATCH the opening in a wall between kitchen and dining room, with a shelf for the plates

Two senses that share nothing but a spelling, in a clue five words long with no joinery at all, so the only question worth asking is where the seam falls.

23 across ERIK SATIE

French composer recalled job on island in Ireland (4,5)

Definition: French composer · charade + container + reversal

Erik Satie wrote the Gymnopedies, so the definition names only a nationality and a trade and leaves you to supply the man.

  • Ireland → EIRE Eire is the Irish name for the Republic, and it opens up to take the rest
  • island → I I is the map abbreviation for island
  • job → TASK a job you are given to do is a task
  • recalled — To recall a thing is to call it back, and letters called back are read the other way round.
  • in — It puts what comes before it inside what comes after, rather than merely joining the two together.

The pieces are assembled the right way round and only then turned over, which is why no part of the answer looks like any of them. Satie is the composer of the Gymnopedies.

25 across SWELTER

Husband and wife changing in tent, perhaps are too hot (7)

Definition: are too hot · substitution

To swelter is to suffer oppressive heat, so the phrase describes people in exactly the state this verb names.

  • tent, perhaps → SHELTER a tent is one kind of shelter, which is what 'perhaps' is flagging
  • Husband → H H is the standard abbreviation for husband, and it is the letter that goes
  • wife → W W is the standard abbreviation for wife, and it takes the vacated place
  • changing — Changing one thing for another is a swap, so a letter here is not removed but replaced by a different one.

Getting changed in a tent is a whole camping holiday in four words, and it conceals an instruction to swap one letter for another. Both letters are the crossword's usual shorthand for the two people.

26 across READMIT

Let back in to lecture at American uni (7)

Definition: Let back in · charade

To readmit somebody is to let them in again after they have been turned out, the RE- prefix carrying the sense of going back.

  • lecture → READ to read a paper to an audience is to deliver it, the old sense of lecturing
  • American uni → MIT the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, known everywhere by its initials

'Let back in' opens the clue, which is the awkward place for a definition and the reason it reads as part of the story. Nothing is reversed here despite the word 'back'.

27 across SALESMANSHIP

Mincing Spanish males marketing art (12)

Definition: marketing art · anagram

Salesmanship is the skill of persuading people to buy, which is what marketing is an art of — the noun names the craft, not the trade.

  • Spanish males → SALESMANSHIP twelve letters, taken from the two words and reordered
  • Mincing — Mincing chops a thing into small pieces which are then pressed back together in a new shape.

'Mincing' is placed to describe how the Spanish males walk rather than what becomes of their letters, which is the oldest trick in the anagram book and still a good one.

2 down GRUMP

Bellyache from drinking rum punch (5)

Definition: Bellyache · hidden word

Both are nouns for a moan: a bellyache is a whinge, and the answer is the sulky grumble a bad-tempered person indulges in.

  • drinking rum punch → GRUMP five consecutive letters spanning the end of the first word, all of the second and the start of the third
  • from — Here the word points at a source you draw something out of, so the answer is already sitting in the words that follow.

A rum punch is a real drink, which is exactly what stops you counting letters. Hidden answers hide best inside phrases that read as ordinary English, and this one reads as a complaint about a party.

3 down OSTRACISE

Exclude East German breed taking lead in Crufts? (9)

Definition: Exclude · charade + container + first letter

To ostracise someone is to shut them out of a group by common consent, which is precisely what excluding them means.

  • East German → OST 'Ost' is simply German for east
  • breed → RAISE a farmer who breeds animals raises them
  • Crufts → C the leading letter of the dog show's name
  • taking — One piece takes another in the sense of swallowing it, so the new letter goes inside rather than alongside.
  • lead in — The lead of a word is the letter standing at its front, and dogs are led as well, which is what keeps the surface honest.

'East German breed' is trying hard to look like a dog — an Alsatian, say — and Crufts, Britain's national dog show, keeps the picture going. Neither phrase is naming a breed at all.

4 down HERMIT

Someone who has quit company misusing HR time (6)

Definition: Someone who has quit company · anagram

A hermit is a recluse who has withdrawn from society altogether, so the definition names the person by what they walked away from.

  • HR time → HERMIT six letters — the personnel department plus a word for a spell
  • misusing — To misuse a thing is to put it to the wrong job, and letters put to the wrong job come back in the wrong order.

'Company' is the pivot. On the surface it is the firm whose HR department is meant; in the definition it means other people's society. Note that the fodder is handed over as an abbreviation.

5 down MURDERER

Gunman maybe spinning barrel before start of roulette (8)

Definition: Gunman maybe · charade + reversal + first letter

A gunman who kills is one instance of the wider word, which is what 'maybe' signals: the answer covers every method, not only shooting.

  • barrel → MURD a barrel is a drum, and the spin turns those four letters round
  • before → ERE 'ere' is the old poetic word for before
  • roulette → R the leading letter of roulette
  • spinning — A cylinder that spins comes round to face the other way, so those letters are read in the opposite direction.
  • start of — It asks only for the letter a word begins with, never for the word itself.

Everything on the surface belongs to a revolver and a casino, but the barrel wanted is the wooden kind. 'Gunman maybe' is definition by example — one sort of the answer, not the only one.

6 down CABAL

Intriguing group from California set up research establishment (5)

Definition: Intriguing group · charade + reversal

A cabal is a small group that meets in secret to scheme, so 'intriguing' carries its literal sense of plotting rather than fascinating.

  • California → CA CA is the postal abbreviation for California
  • research establishment → BAL a research establishment is a lab, stood on its head here
  • set up — In a down entry, setting something up stands it on end, so those letters climb back the way they came.

'Intriguing' is not the adjective it looks like. It means engaged in intrigue — plotting — rather than fascinating, and that shift is the whole of the clue.

7 down LAST RITES

Girl hosts banal ceremony before popping off (4,5)

Definition: ceremony before popping off · container

The last rites are the prayers a priest gives someone who is dying, so the ceremony really does come immediately before death.

  • Girl → LASS a lass is a girl, chiefly in northern British use
  • banal → TRITE something banal is trite — worn out by repetition
  • hosts — A host keeps guests inside the house, so one word here takes another in and closes around it.

'Popping off' is British slang for dying, so the definition reads on the surface like a send-off before somebody nips out for a moment.

8 down MAKE ENDS MEET

To run successful budget, introduce limits? (4,4,4)

Definition: To run successful budget · container

Making ends meet is the idiom for spending no more than you earn, which is exactly what running a household budget successfully amounts to.

  • introduce → MAKE...MEET to introduce two people is to make them meet, which supplies both outer words at once
  • limits → ENDS the limits of anything are its ends

The question mark is doing real work: 'introduce limits' sounds like a Treasury announcement, and the definition sounds like more of the same. Both halves talk about money, which is why the seam is so hard to find.

9 down STEEPLECHASE

Taps in expensive rental run with water periodically (12)

Definition: run with water periodically · charade + container

A steeplechase is a distance race whose every lap includes a water jump, so it really is a run that meets water at regular intervals.

  • expensive → STEEP a steep price is an expensive one
  • rental → LEASE a lease is the rental agreement on a property
  • Taps → CH a sink has two taps, cold and hot, and those are their letters
  • in — Here the little word is an instruction rather than joinery: what comes before it is placed inside what comes after.

The definition is the whole of the tail, not just 'run': the water is the jump the athletes clear once every lap. Plumbing runs right through the surface, which is what hides the pair of taps.

14 down SQUATTERS

Wicket-keepers perhaps, who can be difficult to get out? (9)

Definition: Wicket-keepers perhaps, who can be difficult to get out? · cryptic definition

People who move into an empty property without permission crouch on ground that is not theirs and are notoriously slow to evict — both readings at once.

  • Wicket-keepers perhaps → the surface is cricket, where the keeper crouches behind the stumps all day; but a wicket is also a small gate, and the people who keep one are not fielders
  • who can be difficult to get out? → in cricket getting someone out means dismissing the batter; in a building it means eviction, and the law makes that slow

There is no wordplay to unpick here, only two readings of one sentence. Move it off the cricket field and into an empty house and every word in it changes job.

16 down GREY SEALS

These perhaps disturbed eelgrass catching prey finally (4,5)

Definition: These perhaps disturbed eelgrass catching prey finally · &lit + anagram + last letter

The whole sentence reads straight as a description of the animals: they really do churn up seagrass beds while hunting fish, and 'these perhaps' points at them.

  • eelgrass → EELGRASS eight letters of seagrass, waiting to be reordered
  • prey → Y the last letter of the word, added to the shuffle
  • disturbed — Seagrass that has been disturbed is churned up and no longer lying as it was, which is what happens to these letters.
  • catching — A thing catching something has taken it in, so an extra letter joins the pile being churned about.
  • finally — It asks for the last letter of the word in front of it, the one that comes at the finish.

Read straight, the sentence describes real animals foraging in a seagrass bed; read as instructions, the same words build the answer. Doing both at once is what makes a clue an &lit.

17 down FINE ARTS

Tailors around close to Royal Academy's area (4,4)

Definition: Royal Academy's area · container

The fine arts are painting, sculpture and their relatives — the disciplines a Royal Academy exists to exhibit and teach, so this really is its field.

  • Tailors → FITS a garment that has been tailored fits, and the verb works the same way
  • close to → NEAR close to something is near it
  • around — One word goes round the other like a coat, so the shorter piece ends up in the middle of the longer.

The Royal Academy of Arts is the London institution whose summer exhibition fills its walls with painting and sculpture, so the definition is pointing at a discipline rather than a place.

20 down SIERRA

Car repairs botched with loss of power (6)

Definition: Car · anagram + deletion

The Sierra was a Ford model sold in Britain for a decade, so the general word 'Car' points at it as one instance.

  • power → P P is the physics symbol for power, and it is dropped before anything else happens
  • repairs → SIERRA the seven letters minus that one, rearranged into six
  • botched — A botched job has come out wrong and misshapen, which is what happens to letters put back in the wrong order.
  • with loss of — It marks a letter that leaves before the shuffling starts, rather than one joining in.

The Ford Sierra was the family car on every British driveway in the 1980s, so 'Car' defines it by example. The surface reads as a botched repair that cost the engine its power.

22 down HOTEL

Permit I see to put up tiny building on Park Lane? (5)

Definition: tiny building on Park Lane? · charade + reversal

In Monopoly you buy houses and then a hotel for a square like Park Lane, so the answer really is a tiny building placed there.

  • Permit → LET to let a flat is to permit its use for rent
  • I see → OH 'oh' is the noise of understanding, the spoken form of 'I see'
  • put up — In a down entry, putting something up sends it back the way it came, so the assembled letters are read from the bottom.

Park Lane is the dark-blue square at the expensive end of the British Monopoly board, and what you put on it is a small plastic block — which is what makes 'tiny building' fair.

24 down TEMPI

Police force raised detective's rates (5)

Definition: rates · charade + reversal

Tempi are the speeds at which pieces of music are played, and a speed is a rate — the Italian plural of tempo, kept whole in English.

  • Police force → TEM the Met is London's Metropolitan Police, stood on its head here
  • detective's → PI a PI is a private investigator, the detective for hire
  • raised — A down entry that is raised reads from the bottom upwards, so those letters arrive in the opposite order.

'Rates' means speeds here rather than charges, and the surface is pretending to be about pay. The answer is the Italian plural of tempo, which is why it ends the way it does.
