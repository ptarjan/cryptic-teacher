Across

1 across BREAD SAUCE

A crude base pounded for condiment (5,5)

Definition: condiment · anagram

Bread sauce is a thick milk-and-breadcrumb sauce spooned alongside roast poultry, so 'condiment' names it by the job it does at the table.

  • A crude base → BREADSAUCE ten letters of fodder — A, C, R, U, D, E, B, A, S and E — rearranged
  • pounded — Pounding something in a mortar breaks it up so the pieces settle wherever they land, in no order the cook chose.

A rough foundation being hammered flat before something gets laid on top of it.

The whole sentence reads as building work, so 'condiment' arrives as a surprise at the end. Bread sauce is the milk-and-crumb sauce British cooks serve with roast turkey and game.

6 across FILM

Auteur's work, French, in retrospective: '1051' (4)

Definition: Auteur's work · charade + reversal

An auteur is a director whose personal signature runs through everything they shoot, so their work is a film — one picture, matching the singular definition.

  • French → F F is the standard short form for French, the label on a dictionary entry or a car plate
  • '1051' → ILM in Roman numerals 1051 is M, then L, then I, and these arrive the other way round
  • in retrospective — A retrospective looks back over what has already gone by, so the numerals beside it are read from their far end first.

A director's French picture being looked back over, catalogued under a four-figure number.

The quoted number reads as an exhibit reference in a career retrospective, and it is Roman numerals; the retrospective is the order to take them from the back.

9 across EAGLE

Bird of prey left in earnest? Not quite (5)

Definition: Bird of prey · container + deletion

An eagle is a large hooked-beaked hunting bird, so 'bird of prey' defines it by the class it belongs to rather than by name.

  • earnest? Not quite → EAGE earnest gives EAGER, and stopping short of the end leaves its R behind
  • left → L L is the standard short form for left, as on a stage direction or a boot
  • in — 'In' puts the letter beside it inside the following word rather than in front of it.
  • Not quite — 'Not quite' means you stop just short of the whole word, so the letter it ends on never arrives.

A bird of prey abandoned in all seriousness — or not quite all seriousness.

'Left' looks like the past tense of leave, carrying on the sentence about the bird, and it is a single letter waiting to be dropped inside.

10 across HARD WATER

Calcium-rich liquid hurtling earthward (4,5)

Definition: Calcium-rich liquid · anagram

Hard water carries dissolved calcium and magnesium salts picked up from chalk and limestone, so the definition states its chemistry plainly.

  • earthward → HARDWATER nine letters of fodder — E, A, R, T, H, W, A, R and D — rearranged
  • hurtling — Something hurtling is travelling too fast to be controlled, and letters thrown about like that land in an order nobody chose.

Rain or hail coming down out of the sky.

Reading 'hurtling earthward' as a description of falling rain is the trap; it is an instruction plus the single word it acts on. Hard water is water heavy in dissolved calcium, the kind that furs up a kettle.

12 across ENTOMBS

Says goodbye forever to snob met in resort (7)

Definition: Says goodbye forever to · anagram

To entomb someone is to seal them in a tomb, the last farewell anybody gets, so the definition names burial without using the word.

  • snob met → ENTOMBS seven letters of fodder — S, N, O, B, M, E and T — rearranged
  • resort — Read as re-sort rather than as a seaside town, it asks for the letters to be put in order a second time.

Waving off a stuck-up acquaintance you happened to run into on holiday.

'Resort' holds the holiday scene together while quietly meaning re-sort, and the definition is a euphemism: saying goodbye forever is burial talk.

13 across MYANMAR

When reversing, drive into no Shakespearean master somewhere in SE Asia (7)

Definition: somewhere in SE Asia · charade + reversal

Myanmar is the Southeast Asian state between Bangladesh and Thailand, so the definition places it by region instead of naming anything about it.

  • drive into → RAM to drive into something is to ram it — the phrase works as one instruction, not a verb plus a preposition
  • no Shakespearean → NAY nay is the old word for no that fills the plays
  • master → M M is the standard short form for Master, the title once written on a boy's envelope
  • When reversing — Reversing sends a vehicle back the way it came, and an across entry taken that way is written out from its right-hand end.

A driver backing into somebody who is no Shakespeare scholar, out in Southeast Asia.

Myanmar is the country the British called Burma. All three pieces are assembled first and the whole string is turned round at once, which is why no single word looks reversed.

14 across CONVENIENCES

Perhaps ladies' devices offering comfort (12)

Definition: Perhaps ladies' · double definition

Public conveniences are British public lavatories, of which the Ladies is one; conveniences are equally the appliances that make a household comfortable.

  • Perhaps ladies' → the Ladies is the British sign on a women's public lavatory, and 'perhaps' flags it as one instance of a politer general word
  • devices offering comfort → the labour-saving household fittings advertised as modern: the washing machine, the dishwasher, the immersion heater

Gadgets aimed at women, sold on the comfort they promise.

'The Ladies' is the sign on a British women's public lavatory, and such a place is a public convenience. The apostrophe is the only clue that a possessive noun is meant rather than an adjective.

18 across BREWER'S YEAST

Terry saw bees, bizarrely, as help in making beer (7,5)

Definition: help in making beer · anagram

Brewer's yeast is the fungus that ferments malt sugar into alcohol, so 'help in making beer' names the ingredient that does the actual work.

  • Terry saw bees → BREWERSYEAST twelve letters of fodder — T, E, R, R, Y, S, A, W, B, E, E and S — rearranged
  • bizarrely — Bizarre is well outside the ordinary, and letters put in an order nobody would expect are exactly that.

Terry, oddly, reckoned that bees were what you needed to help brew a beer.

Bees really do make something to drink — mead is fermented honey — which keeps the surface plausible for long enough to stop you counting the letters in front of you.

21 across INTERNS

Detains trainees ... (7)

Definition: Detains · double definition

To intern someone is to hold them in custody without charge, and interns are trainees on placement — one spelling, two unrelated senses.

  • Detains → the verb, stressed on its second syllable: confining people without charge, as governments do in wartime
  • trainees → the noun, stressed on its first syllable: junior staff learning a trade on the job, especially in American hospitals

Somebody locking up the new recruits.

The ellipsis runs the clue on into 23 across so the two read as one sentence, but each half is a complete clue by itself. Verb and noun are spelled alike and stressed differently.

23 across INITIAL

... first sign (7)

Definition: first · double definition

Initial means first — an initial attempt — and to initial a document is to sign it with your initials rather than your full name.

  • first → the adjective: coming before all the others, as in a first attempt
  • sign → the verb: putting your letters on a document to show it has passed through your hands

The earliest hint that something is starting to happen.

The leading ellipsis makes this look like the tail of the previous clue, so the two words read as one phrase about an early symptom when they are separate definitions, an adjective and a verb.

24 across TANGERINE

Argentine mixed fruit (9)

Definition: fruit · anagram

A tangerine is a small loose-skinned citrus, so 'fruit' defines it by its class — the broadest label the setter could have chosen.

  • Argentine → TANGERINE nine letters of fodder — A, R, G, E, N, T, I, N and E — rearranged
  • mixed — Mixing leaves everything in the bowl in some order other than the one it went in, which is what happens to these letters.

An Argentinian dish of mixed fruit.

'Mixed fruit' reads as one phrase off a menu, which hides the fact that only the first of those two words is doing any mixing and the second is the definition.

25 across NADIR

Deliver a new flipping low (5)

Definition: low · charade + reversal

The nadir is the point directly beneath you, opposite the zenith, so it serves as the low point of a career, a mood or a season.

  • Deliver → RID to rid someone of a burden is to deliver them from it
  • a → A the clue's own word, taken as the single letter it is
  • new → N N is the standard short form for new
  • flipping — Flipping a thing turns it over end for end, so the letters assembled before it are read out the other way.

Somebody handing over something new and grumbling that it is flipping low.

'Flipping' passes as the mild British swear word attached to 'low', and that is the whole disguise: it is the instruction, and the word it seemed to be modifying is the definition.

26 across EWER

Lamb's mum runs for source of water (4)

Definition: source of water · charade

A ewer is a wide-mouthed jug kept on a washstand, so 'source of water' is where the water in the basin came from rather than a spring or a river.

  • Lamb's mum → EWE a lamb's mother is a female sheep
  • runs → R R stands for runs on a cricket scorecard, the column holding each batter's total

A mother sheep making a dash for the nearest stream.

'Runs' reads as the verb the sheep is doing, and it is a cricketing abbreviation standing still. A ewer is the tall jug that stood beside a washbasin before plumbing.

27 across CROSSWORDS

Troubles, overwhelming perhaps, foil Everyman's business (10)

Definition: Everyman's business · container

Everyman sets the Observer's Sunday puzzle, so crosswords are literally his business, and the definition is the setter pointing at himself.

  • Troubles → CROSS to cross somebody is to trouble or thwart them
  • foil → SWORD a foil is the light blunted blade used in fencing, so it is one kind of the weapon wanted
  • overwhelming — To overwhelm something is to come down over the top of it, so the first word closes round the one that follows.
  • perhaps — 'Perhaps' warns that the word beside it is one example of the thing wanted rather than a synonym for it.

Overwhelming problems getting in the way of Everyman's line of work.

Everyman is the pen name of the setter of this puzzle, so the definition is him naming his own trade — and 'foil' is a blade here, not the verb the sentence wants it to be.

Down

1 down BREMEN

Having each time bumped off 50%, sombre hitmen in German city (6)

Definition: German city · deletion + charade

Bremen is a Hanseatic port on the Weser and a city-state in its own right, so 'German city' is a plain label rather than a sly one.

  • sombre → BRE the front half, S, O and M, is the fifty per cent done away with, leaving the back three
  • hitmen → MEN H, I and T go the same way, and the three that survive follow on
  • each time bumped off 50% — To bump something off is to do away with it, and 'each' aims that at both words in turn: half of a six-letter word is three letters gone.

Grim contract killers at work in a German city, each of them halfway through the list.

The instruction is buried in a sentence about assassins, where 'bumped off 50%' reads as a body count rather than as an order to cut two words in half.

2 down EIGHTS

Groups of rowers called out: 'Little islands!' (6)

Definition: Groups of rowers · homophone

An eight is a racing shell crewed by eight rowers, so 'groups of rowers' names the crews themselves rather than the boats they sit in.

  • Little islands → EIGHTS an ait is a small island in a river — the Thames is full of them — and the plural said aloud is spelt quite differently in the grid
  • called out — Calling something out is saying it aloud, so what follows is offered as a noise to be written down some other way.

A crew of rowers shouting about the islets they are sweeping past.

A rowing eight is named by the number of oarsmen in the boat, so the plural means crews rather than numbers, and 'ait' is the old English word for a river islet.

3 down DREAM COME TRUE

Supply more cured meat: all I ever hoped for (5,4,4)

Definition: all I ever hoped for · anagram

A dream come true is the thing you wanted most actually happening, which is what 'all I ever hoped for' says in the first person.

  • more cured meat → DREAMCOMETRUE thirteen letters of fodder — M, O, R, E, C, U, R, E, D, M, E, A and T — rearranged
  • Supply — Read as the adverb from 'supple' — SUP-lee, meaning pliantly — it describes letters bending into a new shape, not a delivery of goods.

A butcher being asked for more bacon and ham than anyone could reasonably wish for.

The first word looks like a verb ordering the meat about, and it is an adverb stressed on its own first syllable: supply, as in supplely.

4 down ADHESIVES

Hides vase that's damaged, wanting glue etc (9)

Definition: glue etc · anagram

Adhesives are the substances that stick things together, so 'glue etc' points at glue and everything else in that family — paste, gum, epoxy.

  • Hides vase → ADHESIVES nine letters of fodder — H, I, D, E, S, V, A, S and E — rearranged
  • damaged — Damage knocks a thing out of the shape it was made in, and letters knocked about like that come back in some other order.

Animal skins and a broken vase, with somebody hunting round for the glue.

'Hides' opens the clue looking like a verb concealing something, and it is a noun: a stack of animal skins sitting next to the vase.

5 down CAROM

Primarily: cannon - adroit rebound - or miscue? (5)

Definition: Primarily: cannon - adroit rebound - or miscue? · &lit + first letters

At billiards a carom is a cannon, an adroit rebound off both other balls, so the whole clue describes the shot at the same time as spelling it.

  • cannon → C the first letter of the first item on the list
  • adroit → A the first letter of the second
  • rebound → R the first letter of the third
  • or → O even the little joining word gives up its opening letter
  • miscue → M the first letter of the last item, and the run is printed in order
  • Primarily — Primarily means first of all, and applied to a list it asks for the letter each item starts with and nothing more.

A billiards player running through what the shot he has just played might turn out to be.

A cannon is the British name for the stroke that strikes both other balls, so every item on the list describes one shot — which is what stops the list reading as a list.

7 down INTIMACY

Affection gleaned from Tin Tim, a cyborg (8)

Definition: Affection · hidden word

Intimacy is close personal warmth between people, so 'affection' stands in for it as an everyday synonym — the shared-life sense, not the physical one.

  • Tin Tim, a cyborg → INTIMACY eight consecutive letters run through this phrase, starting one letter into the first word and crossing three word breaks
  • gleaned from — To glean is to pick up what is already lying in a field, so the letters are gathered from where they sit rather than built.

A cyborg called Tin Tim, and the warmth somebody manages to get out of him.

The odd name and the comma make you read 'Tin Tim' as a character being introduced, and a sentence that strains for a name like that is usually straining to hide a run of letters.

8 down MARY ROSE

Herb's rearranged components to present Tudor ship (4,4)

Definition: Tudor ship · cycling

'Tudor ship' names one particular vessel, Henry VIII's flagship, rather than describing a class of ship — a definition by the single famous instance.

  • Herb's → MARY the herb is rosemary, and these are its last four letters, sent round to the front
  • Herb's → ROSE the first four letters of the same herb, now bringing up the rear
  • rearranged components — The herb's name is built from two four-letter words, and rearranging the components means swapping which of the two comes first.

Somebody shuffling sprigs of a herb about to make a display of a Tudor warship.

The Mary Rose was Henry VIII's flagship, which sank in the Solent in 1545 and was raised from the seabed in 1982, so 'Tudor ship' means one vessel rather than a type.

11 down WHAT ELSE IS NEW

Question about York and Orleans that's no surprise? (4,4,2,3)

Definition: Question about York and Orleans · double definition

Read straight it asks what else carries the word both cities begin with, and read as idiom it is the sarcastic response to news that surprises nobody.

  • Question about York and Orleans → both cities wear the same word in front of their names, so the question asks what other things wear it
  • that's no surprise? → the weary reply you make when somebody reports something entirely predictable

A quiz question about two cities, of a kind nobody could find startling.

New York and New Orleans share their first word, so taken literally the phrase is a genuine question about which other things are new; taken as idiom it is a shrug.

15 down NEEDINESS

Once called, enjoys fancy meal, initially suppressing desire for attention (9)

Definition: desire for attention · charade + first letter

Neediness is wanting more notice and reassurance than other people are giving you, which is what 'desire for attention' names outright.

  • Once called → NEE née is the label for the surname a married woman was born with — what she was once called
  • enjoys fancy meal → DINES to dine is to take a proper sit-down meal rather than merely eat
  • initially suppressing → S the opening letter of the word the instruction points at
  • initially — 'Initially' asks for the very start of the word it introduces, one letter and no more of it.

A woman once known by another name sitting down to a grand dinner and holding her feelings in.

'Née' introduces a married woman's maiden name, so 'once called' is a standing crossword convention. 'Suppressing' looks like a container instruction and is wanted for its first letter alone.

16 down ABSINTHE

Strong drink and some kebabs - in theory (8)

Definition: Strong drink · hidden word

Absinthe is a wormwood spirit bottled at up to seventy per cent alcohol, so 'strong drink' names it by the property everyone knows it for.

  • kebabs - in theory → ABSINTHE eight consecutive letters run through this phrase, starting three letters into the first word and carrying straight on across the dash
  • some — 'Some' asks for a part of what follows rather than all of it, and the part wanted is one unbroken run.

An order of strong drink with a few kebabs, at least in principle.

The dash makes 'in theory' look like a comment on the whole sentence rather than material. Absinthe is the green wormwood spirit banned across Europe for most of the twentieth century.

17 down SENTENCE

Punishment dispatched: receiver of stolen goods decapitated (8)

Definition: Punishment · charade + deletion

A sentence is the punishment a court hands down after conviction, so the definition wants the legal sense of the word and not the grammatical one.

  • dispatched → SENT a parcel that has been dispatched has been sent
  • receiver of stolen goods → ENCE a fence is the dealer who takes stolen property off a thief, and the F at its head is cut away here
  • decapitated — Decapitation takes the head off, and the head of a written word is the letter it begins with.

A punishment being handed down, and a handler of stolen goods losing his head.

Both halves belong to crime, which is what hides the join: 'dispatched' reads as the punishment being carried out, and the fence reads as a person rather than as four letters.

19 down KINDER

Relatively pleasant young Germans (6)

Definition: Relatively pleasant · double definition

Kinder is the comparative of kind, so 'relatively pleasant' means more pleasant; the same six letters are the German plural for children.

  • Relatively pleasant → 'relatively' asks for the comparative degree — more so than something else — of a word meaning pleasant
  • young Germans → the German plural of Kind, meaning children, familiar in English from Kindergarten

German youngsters being called reasonably agreeable company.

'Relatively' is not the hedge meaning 'fairly' but an instruction to put the adjective into its comparative form, and the second definition is a German word rather than an English one.

20 down FLARES

Trousers - loud and large - are last thing in grooviness (6)

Definition: Trousers · charade + last letter

Flares are trousers that widen sharply from the knee down, so 'trousers' names the garment and leaves out the shape that gives it its name.

  • loud → F F is forte on a score, the musician's mark for loud
  • large → L L is the size printed on a clothing label
  • are → ARE the clue's own word, lifted out of the sentence and used exactly as it stands
  • last thing in grooviness → S the final letter of the word that closes the clue
  • last thing in — The last thing in a word is the letter it ends on, and only that one letter is being asked for.

Trousers described as loud and large, and the very last word in 1970s style.

'Grooviness' is in the clue because these trousers belong to the early 1970s, and 'are' is the one word doing no wordplay job beyond handing over its own three letters.

22 down SKIER

Heavens! Abrupt right for one doing slaloms? (5)

Definition: one doing slaloms · deletion + charade

A skier is anyone travelling on skis, and the slalom — weaving between gates — is one branch of the sport, which is what the question mark flags.

  • Heavens! → SKIE the heavens are the skies, docked here of the letter they end on
  • right → R R is the standard short form for right, as on a map or a stage direction
  • Abrupt — Something cut abruptly stops before it has finished, so the word beside it loses the letter it ends on.

An exclamation of alarm at a sudden swerve to the right.

'Heavens!' opens as an exclamation and is really a plural noun, and the question mark admits that slalom racing is only one of the things the answer might be doing.
