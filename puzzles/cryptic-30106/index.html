Across

1 across RUNAWAY

Out-of-control aircraft’s first to occupy landing zone (7)

Definition: Out-of-control · container + first letter

A runaway horse, train or lorry is one nobody can stop, so the adjective sits straight in for 'out-of-control'.

  • landing zone → RUNWAY the strip of tarmac an aircraft comes down on, taken whole before anything is let in
  • aircraft’s first → A the opening letter of the word aircraft, cut off because the clue asks for its first
  • first — It asks for the opening letter of the word in front of it, the letter that word starts on.
  • to occupy — To occupy a space is to sit inside it, so the single letter takes up residence within the longer piece.

A plane nobody can steer is coming down and takes over the landing strip.

'Out-of-control' reads as part of the aircraft's plight and is quietly the definition, which leaves the rest of the sentence as a strip of tarmac with one letter parked on it.

5 across WHEELIE

Simple stunt with part of foot attached to bike regularly kicked out (7)

Definition: Simple stunt · charade + alternate letters

A wheelie is about the simplest trick there is on a bike — front wheel up, back wheel rolling — so 'simple stunt' names it plainly.

  • with → W the short form you write in notes and addresses, as in w/ and w/o
  • part of foot → HEEL the back part of a foot, the bit a shoe is built up under
  • bike → IE the second and fourth letters of the word, the two left standing when the others are booted out
  • attached to — It says the piece just built is fastened straight on to the next one, with nothing coming between them.
  • regularly kicked out — Kick letters out at regular intervals and the ones left standing are every second one.

An easy trick performed with one foot on a bike that keeps throwing its rider off.

'Bike' looks like part of the definition, since the stunt is done on one; it is fodder, and everything defining happens in the clue's first two words.

9 across CARAT

Oscar attempts to hide measure of gold (5)

Definition: measure of gold · hidden word

A carat states how many twenty-fourths of an alloy are pure gold, so it is literally a measure of gold rather than of a jewel.

  • Oscar attempts → CARAT run the two words together and five consecutive letters, starting two letters in, are the ones wanted
  • to hide — To hide a thing is to tuck it out of sight inside something else, and the words before it are the something else.

Oscar is trying to conceal how much gold there is.

For gold a carat measures purity in twenty-fourths — 24-carat is the pure stuff — while the near-identical carat for gemstones measures weight, and 'of gold' picks the right one.

10 across KISSINGER

Controversial US politician snogging that Cockney woman (9)

Definition: Controversial US politician · charade

Kissinger was US Secretary of State and national security adviser, and few American politicians of the century are fought over harder.

  • snogging → KISSING snogging is British slang for kissing at length
  • that Cockney woman → ER the word is 'her', and dropped-aitch speech leaves only the two letters that follow
  • Cockney — East End speech drops the aitch from the front of a word, so the word it labels arrives short of its first letter.

A controversial American politician is caught kissing a local woman.

Henry Kissinger ran American foreign policy for Nixon and Ford, and 'controversial' is the definition doing a lot of quiet work — Cambodia and Chile are still argued over.

11 across COAT HANGER

Surprisingly, not a charge for item in wardrobe (4,6)

Definition: item in wardrobe · anagram

A coat hanger is the shaped wire or wood a jacket hangs from, and any wardrobe is full of them — hardware rather than clothing.

  • not a charge → NOTACHARGE ten letters of fodder, exactly the length the grid wants
  • Surprisingly — A surprise is what you were not expecting, and these letters do not turn up in the order you were expecting either.

To everybody's surprise, nothing was charged for it.

The fodder is the whole phrase, little words included, which is where a solver who counts only the letters of 'charge' comes unstuck.

12 across AMMO

After revolution, commandant stores bullets etc (4)

Definition: bullets etc · hidden word + reversal

Ammo is the everyday shortening of ammunition, and 'etc' widens it past bullets to shells and cartridges.

  • commandant → OMMA four consecutive letters sitting inside the word, starting at its second letter
  • After revolution — A revolution turns a thing full circle, so what is found has to be read the other way round.
  • stores — To store something is to keep it inside, so the word beside it holds the letters wanted within itself.

After the uprising, the commandant's stores still hold the bullets.

'Revolution' is doing two jobs at once — an uprising in the surface, a turn of the letters in the wordplay — and 'stores' looks like a noun while working as a verb.

14 across DRIVING RANGE

How far one can travel with a Tesla in place of Golf? (7,5)

Definition: How far one can travel with a Tesla in place of Golf? · cryptic definition

A driving range is where golfers hit practice balls, and an electric car's driving range is how far it goes on one charge.

  • How far one can travel with a Tesla → an electric car is sold on this number: the miles it will do before the battery needs charging
  • in place of Golf → put the electric car where the Volkswagen was and the other reading surfaces — a place laid out for golfers, where balls are hit down a strip

Somebody works out the mileage they would get if a Tesla replaced the family Volkswagen.

Golf has its capital G because it is the Volkswagen, and the swap is the joke: put the electric car in and the phrase stops meaning a practice ground and starts meaning miles per charge.

18 across BENEFACTRESS

Patron beats fencers off (12)

Definition: Patron · anagram

A benefactress is a woman who pays for a person or an institution to keep going, which is what a patron does.

  • beats fencers → BEATSFENCERS twelve letters of fodder, drawn from both words and used entire
  • off — Milk that has gone off is no longer in its proper state, and neither are the letters in front of the word.

A patron sees off a troop of fencers in a fight.

'Beats fencers off' reads as a single action, so the eye takes the last word as part of the fight rather than as the instruction, leaving one word at the front to define.

21 across RARE

Two-thirds of Zimbabwean capital is unusual (4)

Definition: unusual · deletion

Something rare is seldom met with, which is 'unusual' in its plainest sense: the two adjectives swap without a wobble.

  • Zimbabwean capital → HARARE Harare is the capital city of Zimbabwe, six letters before anything goes
  • Two-thirds of → the opening HA falls away, leaving the last four letters standing
  • Two-thirds of — Two-thirds of a six-letter word is four letters, and the third dropped here is the one at the front.

Most of Zimbabwe's capital city is out of the ordinary.

'Capital' is the city, not money and not a big letter, and the fraction is counted in letters rather than in anything the surface is talking about.

22 across ASPIRATION

Eager desire of Parisian to corrupt (10)

Definition: Eager desire · anagram

An aspiration is something you long for and work towards, so 'Eager desire' is desire with the striving built into it.

  • Parisian to → PARISIANTO ten letters of fodder, and the little word at the end is part of them
  • corrupt — Corrupt a file or a text and the characters no longer sit where they should.

A Parisian's longing to lead other people astray.

'Of Parisian' looks like it wants a French word — DE, UN, the usual suspects — and it wants nothing of the kind: the whole phrase is simply letters, small words included.

25 across ARGONAUTS

Angostura rum for heroic sailors (9)

Definition: heroic sailors · anagram

The Argonauts crewed the Argo for Jason, and Greek myth counts every one of them a hero, so 'heroic sailors' names the whole company.

  • Angostura → ANGOSTURA nine letters of fodder, off the bitters bottle behind the bar
  • rum — Rum is an old British adjective meaning odd or peculiar, so the letters it labels come out peculiar.

Angostura and rum, the drink you would pour for heroes home from the sea.

'Rum' is a spirit in the surface and an adjective meaning odd in the wordplay, and the sailors are Jason's crew, who went after the Golden Fleece in the Argo.

26 across ENTER

Go into middle of America with shaved head (5)

Definition: Go into · deletion

To enter a room is to go into it — the same movement, with the two-word phrasal version standing in for the single verb.

  • middle of America → CENTER the middle, spelt the way America spells it, which is what 'of America' is asking for
  • with shaved head → the opening C is shaved off, leaving five letters
  • with shaved head — A shaved head loses what sits on top, so the word gives up the letter it begins with.

Cross into the heart of America with a freshly shaved head.

'Middle of America' looks like an instruction to pull letters out of the middle of the word AMERICA; it is asking instead for a word meaning middle, in the American spelling.

27 across TEST BED

International match with live feed at last, a place to experiment (4,3)

Definition: a place to experiment · charade + last letter

A test bed is the rig an engine, a system or an idea is tried out on, so it is literally a place where things are experimented with.

  • International match → TEST a Test is an international fixture at cricket or rugby, the one country plays against country
  • live → BE to live is to be, so the verb hands over two letters
  • feed at last → D the final letter of the word feed
  • at last — It calls for the final letter of the word in front of it and nothing else of that word.

The international was covered with a live feed right at the end.

'Live feed' reads as broadcasting and is two unrelated pieces of wordplay, while the definition is the whole tail of the sentence rather than a single word.

28 across TOTALLY

Shot colleague dead (7)

Definition: dead · charade

Dead used as an intensifier — dead right, dead easy — means completely, and the answer is that adverb in its ordinary form.

  • Shot → TOT a tot is a small measure of spirits, the shot poured in a British pub
  • colleague → ALLY an ally is somebody on your side, which is what a colleague is at work

A workmate has been shot dead.

'Dead' is not the corpse the surface offers: it is the intensifier of 'dead right' and 'dead certain', so the definition is an adverb meaning completely.

1 down ROCOCO

Runs over clown in theatrical style (6)

Definition: theatrical style · charade

Rococo is the frilled, gilded eighteenth-century manner in art and decoration, and 'theatrical' catches its showiness: a style defined by a style.

  • Runs → R the letter a cricket scorebook uses for runs
  • over → O an over is the six balls a bowler sends down, and the scorebook writes it as one letter
  • clown → COCO Coco the Clown was Britain's best-known circus clown for decades, and his name is the long piece here

A clown is run over, and the accident is played for all the theatre it is worth.

Cricket supplies both short pieces, R for runs and O for an over, and it supplies the surface too — which is why 'Runs over' reads as one verb rather than two abbreviations.

2 down NORMAN

French person in Opera North (6)

Definition: French person · charade

A Norman comes from Normandy, so the definition names a French person by region — one instance of the nationality rather than the nationality itself.

  • Opera → NORMA Bellini's Norma is one of the standards of the operatic repertoire, and the clue wants its title
  • North → N the compass point in its one-letter form, as printed on a map

A French singer has joined the northern opera company.

Opera North is a real company, based in Leeds, and that is what makes the two words look like one name instead of two separate pieces of wordplay.

3 down WITCHCRAFT

Bad spelling of broomstick? (10)

Definition: Bad spelling · charade

Witchcraft is spell-casting of the malevolent kind, so 'Bad spelling' reads it in the sorcery sense rather than the classroom one.

  • broomstick → WITCH in every storybook a broomstick has exactly one rider, and the rider is the first piece
  • broomstick → CRAFT craft also means a vessel you travel in, which is what a broomstick is to the person on it

Somebody has misspelled the word broomstick.

'Spelling' is the trap: not the arrangement of letters but the casting of spells, and 'Bad' is what makes those spells the black sort. The question mark admits the broomstick is being read whimsically.

4 down YUKON

It’s disgusting, working in Canadian territory (5)

Definition: Canadian territory · charade

The Yukon is a territory of Canada, not a province, which is exactly the distinction the definition is leaning on.

  • It’s disgusting → YUK the noise you make at something revolting, spelt the British way
  • working → ON a machine that is working is on, and setters use that equivalence constantly

A complaint that something repulsive is going on up in the Canadian north.

'It's disgusting' looks like reported speech and is a two-letter noise plus a switch position. The Yukon is one of Canada's three territories, up beside Alaska, rather than one of its provinces.

5 down WESTERNER

Nearly married more severe American? (9)

Definition: American · deletion + charade

A Westerner is anyone from the West, and an American is the readiest example of one — which is the looseness the question mark concedes.

  • married → WED the ordinary three-letter word for married, taken whole before anything is lost
  • Nearly → the D on the end of the previous piece drops off, leaving two letters
  • more severe → STERNER stern means severe, and the comparative just adds -er
  • Nearly — Something done nearly stops just short of the end, so the word beside it turns up without its final letter.

A marriage that almost happened, and a sterner sort of American.

The question mark is doing real work: this is a definition by example, and plenty of the people the answer covers have never been near America.

6 down EVIL

On return, live in sin (4)

Definition: sin · reversal

Evil is moral wrong taken as a whole, and 'sin' is the same idea in religious dress — both nouns for the bad thing itself.

  • live → LIVE four letters that only have to be read the other way round
  • On return — Anything on its return comes back along the way it went, so the word beside it is read from its far end.

Somebody comes home to find a couple living in sin.

'Live in sin' is the phrase the surface leans on, and the clue quietly splits it down the middle: one half is a word to turn round, the other is the definition.

7 down LIGAMENT

Venison, perhaps, wrapped in cotton tissue (8)

Definition: tissue · container

A ligament is connective tissue, the fibrous strap holding one bone to another, so 'tissue' names it by its biological class.

  • cotton → LINT lint is the soft fluff cotton sheds, and it opens and closes the wordplay
  • Venison, perhaps → GAME venison is one kind of game, the meat of hunted animals, with 'perhaps' flagging the example
  • wrapped in — Wrap one thing in another and it finishes up inside, which is where the meat goes here.

A haunch of venison comes wrapped in cotton cloth.

'Tissue' wants to be the cotton's partner, the paper kind on the kitchen roll; it is the body's sort, the fibrous stuff that ties a joint together.

8 down EARLOBES

Nobleman honours stud locations? (8)

Definition: stud locations · charade

Earlobes are where a stud earring is pushed through and fastened, so the definition names them by what gets fixed there.

  • Nobleman → EARL the British peerage rank between viscount and marquess
  • honours → OBES an OBE is the Order of the British Empire, handed out in the twice-yearly honours lists, and more than one makes OBEs

A nobleman pays tribute to the country's horse-breeding farms.

A stud in the surface is the place thoroughbreds are bred; the studs meant are the small earrings, and the question mark admits people wear those elsewhere too.

13 down PRESS AGENT

President books inspiring wise man as promoter (5,5)

Definition: promoter · charade + container

A press agent is hired to get a client into the newspapers, which is promotion, so 'promoter' names the job rather than the trade.

  • President → PRES the office in its usual short form, as printed on a nameplate
  • wise man → SAGE a sage is a wise man, and these four letters are the ones breathed in
  • books → NT the New Testament, a shelf of books that setters cut to two letters
  • inspiring — To inspire is to breathe in, so what follows is drawn inside the letters already standing.

The president's reading is an inspiration to a wise old man.

'Books' with no title attached is nearly always the Bible in a cryptic — NT for the New Testament, OT for the Old — and it is worth learning both.

15 down VICE SQUAD

Cave with squid bemused investigative team (4,5)

Definition: investigative team · anagram

A vice squad is the police unit put on prostitution, gambling and drugs, so 'investigative team' describes it rather than naming it.

  • Cave with squid → CAVESQUID nine letters of fodder; the 'with' only joins the two words together
  • bemused — Somebody bemused is thoroughly muddled, and the letters handed over beside the word come out muddled too.

A squid sits bemused in a cave.

'Bemused' reads as the squid's expression and is the only instruction in the sentence — everything in front of it is letters, and everything after it defines.

16 down ABERRANT

Deviant president, Republican, with tirade (8)

Definition: Deviant · charade

Aberrant means straying from the normal course, which is what 'deviant' says — both adjectives about departure from the norm.

  • president → ABE Abraham Lincoln, shortened the way headline writers shorten him
  • Republican → R the party's initial, the same letter American papers print after a congressman's name
  • tirade → RANT a rant is a tirade delivered at volume

A president of odd habits, a Republican, is off on a tirade.

Lincoln was the first Republican president, which is what makes the first three pieces read as one description of one man rather than three separate scraps.

17 down INERT GAS

Perhaps neon lit up Tangiers (5,3)

Definition: Perhaps neon · anagram

Neon is one of the gases that barely react with anything, so 'Perhaps neon' defines by example, with 'perhaps' owning up to it.

  • Tangiers → TANGIERS eight letters of fodder, the Moroccan port supplying every one of them
  • lit up — To be lit up is to be drunk, and somebody in that state cannot keep anything in the order it was given.

Neon signs light up the streets of Tangiers after dark.

'Lit up' does two jobs: neon tubes in the surface, and an old slang word for drunk in the wordplay. Neon is one of six such gases, with helium and argon.

19 down PISTOL

Arm private investigator, with many uprising (6)

Definition: Arm · charade + reversal

A pistol is a small firearm, and 'arm' is the collective noun for a weapon, as in small arms and arms dealer.

  • private investigator → PI the two initials a private eye goes by in every detective story
  • many → STOL many is 'lots', and those four letters climb back up the entry
  • uprising — In a down entry a rising runs back towards the top of the grid, so the letters before it are read the other way.

Hand a private detective a weapon, with plenty of people rising in revolt.

'Arm' is a verb in the surface — to arm somebody is to hand them a weapon — and a noun in the definition, which is the switch the whole clue rests on.

20 down ENERGY

Refreshed Green party at last showing intensity (6)

Definition: intensity · anagram + last letter

Energy is the vigour something is done with, and 'intensity' is that same force of feeling — the sense in 'she argued with energy'.

  • Green → GREEN five letters of fodder, from the party's name
  • party at last → Y the last letter of the word party, joined on after the others have settled
  • Refreshed — To refresh a thing is to make it new again, and the letters beside it come back new-made in a different order.
  • at last — It points at the final letter of the word in front of it, the one the word finishes on.

The Green party is finally showing some fire.

'Refreshed' looks like a comment on the party's mood and is the instruction, while 'party' is not part of the fodder at all — it contributes a single letter from its end.

23 down INSET

Clubs abandoning cricket, perhaps? It should be seen as part of the bigger picture (5)

Definition: It should be seen as part of the bigger picture · deletion

An inset is the small picture printed inside a larger one — the corner map, the boxed photograph — so it really is part of the bigger picture.

  • cricket, perhaps → INSECT a cricket is one kind of insect, with 'perhaps' flagging that it is an example
  • Clubs → C clubs is C at the bridge table, and that C is the letter walking out
  • abandoning — To abandon a thing is to walk out on it, so the letter named simply leaves the word that held it.

Cricket clubs are folding, which matters when you look at the wider view.

Both halves of the trap are sporting: the cricket is the insect rather than the game, and 'Clubs' is the card suit rather than anybody's sports club.

24 down KNOB

Handle screw-up (4)

Definition: Handle · reversal

A knob is the round handle on a door or a drawer, so 'Handle' is the plain noun it stands in for.

  • screw → BONK British slang for sex, and 'screw' is the coarser word for the same thing
  • up — In a down entry up runs back towards the top of the grid, so the word before it is read in reverse.

Somebody is asked to handle a blunder.

'Screw-up' reads as one word meaning a mess, and the clue wants it as two: a verb to turn round, and the instruction telling you to turn it.
