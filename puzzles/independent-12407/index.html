Across

6 across EXPERT

Once upon a time, one of great skill (6)

Definition: one of great skill · charade

one of great skill -> EXPERT: the noun sense, someone whose skill is authoritative enough to be called on for it.

  • Once → EX an ex is what somebody once was, as in ex-president
  • a → PER 'twice a day' and 'twice per day' say the same thing, so the article swaps for the Latin
  • time → T t is the standard abbreviation for time, as in any physics formula
  • upon — a piece resting upon another is laid against it, which fixes this chunk at the front of the assembly

This is the puzzle's opening line and the fairy tale's, so 'Once upon a time' looks like scene-setting; not one of its four words is spare.

7 across HIGHNESS

intoxicated by head royal's title (8)

Definition: royal's title · charade

royal's title -> HIGHNESS: the form of address a prince or princess takes, always preceded by Your, His or Her.

  • intoxicated → HIGH somebody high on a drug is intoxicated by it
  • head → NESS a ness is a headland, a promontory running out into the sea — Orford Ness, Sheerness
  • by — standing by something means standing next to it, so these two pieces simply run together in the order given

'Head' wants to mean chief or boss and means a bit of coastline instead; that geographical sense is worth banking, because setters reach for it constantly.

10 across WITCH

a sorcerer, was (when hidden) longing (5)

Definition: a sorcerer · deletion + charade

a sorcerer -> WITCH: one who works magic; the word covered either sex in older use, though the fairy tale has fixed it as a woman.

  • was (when hidden) → W 'when' can be swapped for 'as' — 'as I arrived' — and hiding AS inside WAS leaves a single letter
  • longing → ITCH an itch for something is a longing for it
  • hidden — what is hidden is put out of sight, so the letters named disappear and only the remainder shows

The bracket looks like an aside in the story and is the whole instruction: 'when' is there as a synonym, to name the two letters that go.

11 across PROTESTED

for, primarily, the emperor's stepdaughter. The emperor's daughter objected (9)

Definition: objected · charade + first letters

objected -> PROTESTED: to protest is to voice an objection out loud, and the past tense matches.

  • for → PRO to be pro something is to be for it, as in pro-European
  • the emperor's stepdaughter → TES the opening letters of these three words, in the order the clue sets them down
  • The emperor's daughter → TED the same trick again on the next three words
  • primarily — what comes primarily comes first, so each word gives up only its opening letter — and the instruction carries on past the full stop

The full stop is a genuine sentence break in the story running through this puzzle, and it hides the fact that one instruction governs both halves.

12 across RETIRED

concerned with needing rest, and left (7)

Definition: left · charade

left -> RETIRED: the sense of withdrawing from a room or a field, so it substitutes straight into 'he left after dinner'.

  • concerned with → RE re means concerning, the word at the head of a memo
  • needing rest → TIRED somebody who needs a rest is tired

'Concerned with' looks like the definition of a whole word and is only two letters; the definition is the single word at the very end, which is the last place anyone looks.

14 across STOLEN

Hot, horribly lost, occasionally mean (6)

Definition: Hot · anagram + alternate letters

Hot -> STOLEN: thieves' slang, as in 'hot goods' — property taken so recently that handling it is dangerous.

  • lost → LOST four letters of fodder, straight from the clue
  • occasionally mean → EN the second and fourth letters of mean, taken every other one
  • horribly — a thing done horribly comes out badly formed, and badly formed letters are letters out of their proper order
  • occasionally — what happens occasionally happens every so often rather than all the time, so every second letter is taken

'Hot' is the whole trick: goods too hot to handle are goods the police are looking for. Read as temperature it hides the definition in plain sight.

16 across LECHEROUSNESS

he cursed endlessly, unravelling with no less depravity - (13)

Definition: depravity · anagram + deletion

depravity -> LECHEROUSNESS: the noun for habitual lustfulness, one particular form of moral depravity rather than the whole of it.

  • he cursed endlessly → HECURSE HE CURSED with its last letter gone: seven letters of fodder
  • no less → NOLESS the two little words used as themselves, six more letters
  • endlessly — a word without its end has lost its final letter, and only what survives goes into the mix
  • unravelling — something unravelling comes apart thread by thread, and the threads are laid down again in a new order

'With no less' reads as an emphatic aside — depravity, no less — and it is nearly half the fodder. A phrase that sounds like commentary is the best hiding place there is.

19 across TIRADE

ego invested in dealing an abusive harangue (6)

Definition: an abusive harangue · container

an abusive harangue -> TIRADE: a plain synonym for a long, angry, unbroken outpouring aimed at somebody.

  • dealing → TRADE dealing in goods is trade, and the word splits open after its first letter
  • ego → I the ego is the I, the self as Freud's English translators named it
  • invested in — money invested in a business goes into it, and so does this letter, landing inside the other word rather than beside it

Ego = I is a convention worth owning: Freud wrote das Ich, the I, and the crossword has used the single letter for it ever since.

20 across PREHEAT

turning her into a fairy-tale vegetable, with temperature ready for baking (7)

Definition: ready for baking · reversal + container + charade

ready for baking -> PREHEAT: to preheat an oven is to bring it up to temperature before the food goes in, readying it.

  • a fairy-tale vegetable → PEA the princess in the tale felt one through twenty mattresses; the word opens after its first letter
  • her → REH the pronoun turned about, three letters read the other way
  • temperature → T T is the standard abbreviation for temperature, on a dial or in a formula
  • turning — a piece turned about ends up facing the other way, so its letters are read from the back
  • into — one word going into another lands inside it, splitting the host rather than sitting beside it

'Ready' is a verb here — to ready something — and reading it as an adjective leaves the definition with nothing to do.

23 across SWALLOWED

Little bird entered, empty inside, and ate (9)

Definition: ate · charade + outer letters

ate -> SWALLOWED: to swallow food is to get it down and so to have eaten it, a near synonym rather than an exact one.

  • Little bird → SWALLOW the small fork-tailed migrant that nests in barns
  • entered, empty inside → ED ENTERED hollowed out leaves its first and last letters
  • empty inside — a word emptied of its inside keeps nothing but the letters standing at its two ends

'Little bird' sits at the front looking like a definition and is seven letters of the build; the definition is the three-letter word right at the end.

24 across WHOLE

every bit, with difficulty (5)

Definition: every bit · charade

every bit -> WHOLE: the noun sense, as in 'the whole of it' — everything counted in, nothing left over.

  • with → W w is the standard abbreviation for with, as in a recipe or a note
  • difficulty → HOLE to be in a hole is to be in difficulty

'With' looks like the joinery that holds a clue together and is a single letter. In a four-word clue there is no room for a word that is not working.

26 across AVERSELY

Critically out of depth, reluctantly (8)

Definition: reluctantly · deletion

reluctantly -> AVERSELY: with aversion, doing a thing while disinclined to do it, the -ly answering the -ly.

  • Critically → ADVERSELY an adverse review is a critical one, so this is the nine-letter word before the loss
  • depth → d is the abbreviation for depth, and that is the letter coming out
  • out of — taking one thing out of another removes it, and what is named here is a single letter of the longer word

'Out of depth' is a whole idiom in the surface — a critic beyond their expertise — and in the wordplay it is one letter leaving one word.

27 across AFRAID

sounding like a stressed chicken (6)

Definition: chicken · homophone

chicken -> AFRAID: the slang adjective for cowardly — calling somebody chicken says they have not the nerve for it.

  • a stressed → AFRAID a rope or a nerve under stress is frayed, and 'a frayed' said out loud is spelt quite differently
  • sounding like — what is wanted matches in speech and not in spelling, which is where the letters are free to change

'Chicken' reads as poultry in a clue full of fairy-tale animals, and the sense wanted is the playground one, a coward.

Down

1 down SPAT

this pathetic specimen spewed out (4)

Definition: spewed out · hidden word

spewed out -> SPAT: the past tense of spit, ejecting something forcibly from the mouth, which is what spewing something out is.

  • this pathetic → SPAT four consecutive letters that start in the last letter of one word and finish inside the next
  • specimen — a specimen is a small sample cut out of something larger, which is exactly what a run of consecutive letters is

'Specimen' does two jobs at once: in the story it is the pathetic creature being sneered at, and in the wordplay it is the instruction to take a sample of the letters.

2 down ARCHER

a parcel - after removing wrappings, the girl! A sign in the sky (6)

Definition: A sign in the sky · deletion + charade

A sign in the sky -> ARCHER: Sagittarius, the zodiac sign drawn as a bowman, goes by that plain English name.

  • a parcel → ARC PARCEL unwrapped: the P at the front and the EL at the back come away
  • the girl → HER the pronoun that stands in for a girl already named
  • after removing wrappings — the wrapping of a parcel is what encloses it, so the letters at each end come off and the middle is kept

The exclamation mark is the storyteller's voice rather than a signal, and the sign wanted is Sagittarius — the zodiac, not the weather.

3 down BIGOT

stupendous and uplifting to one of strong beliefs (5)

Definition: one of strong beliefs · charade + reversal

one of strong beliefs -> BIGOT: the kindest possible wording for someone whose opinions are held so rigidly that no argument can shift them.

  • stupendous → BIG something stupendous is big on a scale that impresses
  • to → OT the two letters of the little word, climbing the column and so read the other way
  • uplifting — a piece lifted up in a down entry climbs the grid, so its letters arrive bottom to top

'Stupendous and uplifting' reads as praise, which is the last tone the answer deserves; that mismatch between surface and sense is the setter's joke.

4 down THREATEN

a well-mannered fellow, extremely ideal, being “good breeding” as they once said - (8)

The paper printed the wrong text against this clue — the words above are not the ones this answer came from, so there is no wordplay in them to explain. The Independent printed 5-down's clue here, with a different ending glued on. The setter's own wordplay for this one, in the paper's data feed, is an anagram of THERE AT plus N — words that never appeared on the page.

5 down GENTILESSE

a well-mannered fellow, extremely ideal, being nobility formerly (10)

Definition: nobility formerly · charade + outer letters

nobility formerly -> GENTILESSE: the medieval word for nobility of birth and of conduct together, long obsolete, which is what 'formerly' warns you about.

  • a well-mannered fellow → GENT a gent is a gentleman, the courteous sort
  • extremely ideal → IL the first and last letters of ideal, in that order
  • being → ESSE esse is the Latin 'to be', borrowed into English for a thing's very being
  • extremely — the extremes of a word are the letters at its two far ends, and both of them are wanted

The word is Chaucer's, which is why the definition has to say 'formerly': the surface's well-mannered fellow is describing the very quality being built.

6 down EDWARD

a prince, one in a guardian's care having Balls, perhaps above all (6)

Definition: a prince · charade

a prince -> EDWARD: definition by example, the name of several English kings and of the present Duke of Edinburgh.

  • Balls, perhaps → ED Ed Balls was the Labour MP and shadow chancellor, one of several public figures with that first name
  • one in a guardian's care → WARD a child placed in a guardian's legal care is that guardian's ward
  • perhaps — the surname named is only one of the people carrying that first name, and 'perhaps' is what marks a definition by example
  • above all — in a down entry the piece put above everything else takes the top cells, so it is written first

The capital B is surface work: it makes 'Balls' look like a dance at the palace instead of a politician's surname.

8 down SEDAN

quietly commandeering, located centrally between two poles in a movable chair (5)

Definition: a movable chair · middle letters + container

a movable chair -> SEDAN: the sedan chair, an enclosed seat slung on poles and carried by two bearers, was the taxi of Georgian towns.

  • between two poles → SN S for south and N for north, one letter at each end of the build
  • quietly → E the middle letter of the word itself, the fourth of its seven
  • commandeering → D the seventh letter of thirteen, dead centre
  • located → A the middle letter of another seven-letter word
  • centrally — a letter taken centrally is the one standing at the exact midpoint of a word, and three words here give one each
  • between two poles — the poles of the earth are north and south, so their initials go one at each end of what they enclose

The poles of the surface are the chair's own carrying poles; in the wordplay they are the earth's. Note that 'located' is fodder as well as sentence — two middles would leave the entry a letter short.

9 down SPIDERY

Thin and straggly, see the tragic princess upset, supported by the old queen inside! (7)

Definition: Thin and straggly · container + reversal + charade

Thin and straggly -> SPIDERY: said of handwriting or of legs, thin and sprawling in the way a spider's limbs are.

  • see → SPY to spy something is to see it, and these three letters open up after the second
  • the tragic princess upset → ID Diana, who died in the 1997 Paris crash, is DI to crosswords — here turned over
  • the old queen → ER ER is Elizabeth Regina, the late Queen's cypher on British postboxes
  • upset — something upset has been tipped over, so the two letters come back the other way round
  • supported by — in a down entry whatever supports a piece sits underneath it, which puts this chunk second
  • inside — the assembled chunk is dropped into the middle of another word rather than joined on at either end

'See' is the word most likely to be read as a link between two halves of the clue, and it is the outer shell of the whole build.

13 down TIE-BREAKER

Couple having split, he, initially leaving her for a decisive struggle (3-7)

Definition: a decisive struggle · charade + deletion

a decisive struggle -> TIE-BREAKER: the extra contest played to settle a draw, in tennis or anywhere else a result has to be produced.

  • Couple → TIE to couple two things is to tie them together — a verb, not the pair in the surface
  • having split → BREAK a split between two people is a break
  • he, initially leaving her → ER HER loses the H that 'he' begins with
  • initially — the letter a word starts with is the one picked out here, and it is the letter that has to go
  • leaving — whatever leaves a word is subtracted from it, so the named letter comes out of the front

The surface is a marriage coming apart and the answer is out of the sports pages; the switch of 'Couple' from noun to verb is the step that unlocks it.

15 down CUSPIDS

seconds after, succeeded stabbing the flying lover-boy with arrows and teeth! (7)

Definition: teeth · container + charade

teeth -> CUSPIDS: the pointed canines, named for the single cusp each one carries, so this is teeth of a particular kind.

  • the flying lover-boy with arrows → CUPID the winged Roman god of love with his bow; the name opens up after its second letter
  • succeeded → S s. is the genealogist's abbreviation for succeeded
  • seconds → S s is the abbreviation for seconds on any stopwatch
  • after — a piece that comes after the rest is tacked on at the end, whichever order the clue names it in
  • stabbing — a blade stabbing something ends up buried inside it, and that is where this letter lands

Two different S abbreviations in one clue, and the exclamation mark belongs to the fairy tale rather than to the wordplay.

17 down HIDELESS

She, left aside with no answer, cracked - wanting the protector of her body (8)

Definition: wanting the protector of her body · anagram + deletion

wanting the protector of her body -> HIDELESS: a hide is an animal's skin, the body's own armour, and the -less suffix takes it away.

  • She → SHE the pronoun used as itself, three letters of fodder
  • left → L L is the standard abbreviation for left
  • aside → SIDE ASIDE once the A has gone: four more letters for the mix
  • answer → A is the abbreviation for answer, and it is the letter taken out
  • with no — saying a thing goes without something is the plainest way of removing it, so that letter never joins the mix
  • cracked — something cracked has broken into pieces, and the pieces go back together in a different arrangement

'Left aside' reads as one idiom and is two separate scraps of fodder, which is why counting the letters before splitting the phrase gets you nowhere.

18 down ATTEND

engaged in combat, tenderly to be present (6)

Definition: to be present · hidden word

to be present -> ATTEND: to attend a meeting is to be there in person, the infinitive matching the clue's 'to be'.

  • combat, tenderly → ATTEND the run begins inside the first word and finishes inside the second, straight across the comma
  • engaged in — to be engaged in something is to be busy inside it, so the letters are already sitting within the words that follow

The letters cross a comma and a word break, which is exactly where the eye stops looking; punctuation counts for nothing once you are reading letters.

19 down TESLA

The electric superstar, least surprisingly - (5)

Definition: The electric superstar · anagram

The electric superstar -> TESLA: Nikola Tesla, the celebrated pioneer of alternating current, whose name now sits on a car as well.

  • least → LEAST five letters of fodder, the whole of the word
  • surprisingly — a surprise is something turning up where you did not expect it, and so is every letter of the fodder

One word of fodder and one instruction, so the only real trap is reading 'least surprisingly' together, as a comment on how obvious the answer ought to be.

21 down EX-WIFE

if wee kiss excited one of the parties after the marriage was over - (2-4)

Definition: one of the parties after the marriage was over · anagram

one of the parties after the marriage was over -> EX-WIFE: the woman who was the wife, with ex- marking the relationship as ended.

  • if wee → IFWEE five letters of fodder, wee being the Scots word for small
  • kiss → X X is the kiss written at the foot of a letter
  • excited — letters in an excited state will not stand still in the order they were handed to you

The surface is a wedding and the definition is what came long afterwards. Kiss = X is the step most solvers miss, and it is the letter the answer cannot do without.

22 down DWELT

turned bawdy and there, not in our world, lived (5)

Definition: lived · reversal + deletion + charade

lived -> DWELT: the past tense of dwell, to reside somewhere, and the form every fairy tale uses of where someone once lived.

  • bawdy → DWEL LEWD means bawdy, and here it is read back to front
  • there, not in our world → T our world is HERE, and taking HERE out of THERE leaves one letter
  • turned — a word turned round is read from its far end back to its start
  • not in — saying one thing is not in another is an instruction to take those letters out and keep what is left

'Not in our world' sounds like a line about somewhere enchanted and is a subtraction: the other place with this one taken out of it.

25 down OKAY

well, happily ever after (4)

Definition: well · charade

well -> OKAY: the adverb of health or progress — doing well, doing okay — rather than the deep hole in the ground.

  • happily → OK being OK with something is being happy about it
  • ever → AY ay is the old poetic word for ever, as in 'for ay'
  • after — the piece named as coming after the other is written second, which fixes the order of the two halves

The last clue of the puzzle is the last line of the story, so 'happily ever after' has to read as a set phrase and come apart into two pieces at the same time.
