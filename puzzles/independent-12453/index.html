1 across BUBBLY

Get nurses behind surgery high on gas (6)

Definition: high on gas · container

high on gas -> BUBBLY: a drink loaded with dissolved gas is bubbly, and so is a person in irrepressibly high spirits.

  • Get → BUY to get something is to buy it, and this word parts after its first two letters
  • behind surgery → BBL a BBL is a Brazilian butt lift, the cosmetic operation performed on the behind
  • nurses — A nurse cradles a baby in both arms, so the word that nurses closes right round the letters handed to it.

Nurses out behind the doctor's surgery, giddy on laughing gas.

'Surgery' in the surface is the British word for a GP's premises; the wordplay wants an operation instead, and that operation is known by its three initials.

4 across CALCULUS

Tricky maths that could be a pain to pass (8)

Definition: Tricky maths · double definition

Calculus is the maths of rates of change, and a calculus is also a stone formed in the body, of which a kidney one is famously painful to pass.

  • Tricky maths → the branch of mathematics that handles rates of change and areas under curves, and the one sixth-formers dread
  • a pain to pass → the medical sense: a hard deposit that forms in the kidney or the bladder and has to come out the hard way

A student grumbling that the maths paper is impossible to get through.

'Pass' carries the exam sense on the surface and the medical one underneath, and the word itself is Latin for a pebble, from the counting stones an abacus used.

10 across OVERRATES

Reform voters are for values without moderation (9)

Definition: values without moderation · anagram

values without moderation -> OVERRATES: to overrate a thing is to put a value on it beyond what it deserves, with 'over-' carrying the immoderation.

  • voters are → VOTERSARE anagram fodder, nine letters
  • Reform — To reform something is to form it again in a new shape, and letters formed again come out in an order they were not given in.

Reform UK's voters being given a great deal more credit than they deserve.

Reform is a British political party, which is what the capital R is doing; as an instruction the word means form again, and the fodder is sitting right beside it.

11 across REALM

Ignoring the odds, Arsenal limp to dominion (5)

Definition: dominion · alternate letters

dominion -> REALM: a realm is the territory a monarch holds sway over, which is what a dominion is, both words naming the land rather than the power.

  • Arsenal limp → REALM run the two words together and keep the second letter and every other one after it
  • Ignoring the odds — The odd letters are the first, third and fifth, and ignoring those leaves the even ones to be read off in order.

Arsenal limping through a match nobody gave them a chance in.

Arsenal are a London football club, so the capital A passes for a name in a sentence about betting; the only odds in play are letter positions.

12 across DEER

A few bucks is expensive for auditors (4)

Definition: A few bucks · homophone

A few bucks -> DEER: a buck is a male deer, so a few of them are deer, the definition naming the animal by one kind of it.

  • expensive → DEER expensive is dear, and that word said aloud is spelt another way in the grid
  • for auditors — An auditor is a listener, the word sharing its root with audio, so what counts is the noise a word makes.

Accountants who reckon a few dollars is a lot of money.

'Bucks' is dollars in the surface and male animals in the definition, while 'auditors' are accountants there and listeners here. Every word is working two jobs.

13 across AIR MARSHAL

Officer uncovered Kamala Harris in disguise (3,7)

Definition: Officer · deletion + anagram

Officer -> AIR MARSHAL: an air marshal is a senior Royal Air Force rank, so the definition names the class and the answer supplies one instance of it.

  • uncovered Kamala → AMAL the opening K and the closing A come off the forename, leaving the four letters between them
  • Harris → HARRIS the surname joins the fodder whole, six letters more
  • uncovered — What covers a word is the letter at each end of it, so uncovering the name takes the first and the last away.
  • in disguise — Someone in disguise is not recognisable, and letters put in disguise arrive in an order you would not know them by.

An officer spotting the former US vice-president travelling incognito.

'Uncovered' reaches back to the forename alone while 'in disguise' shuffles both names, and the picture of somebody travelling incognito carries the two instructions between them.

15 across SOPHIST

It's posh abroad, one argues (7)

Definition: one argues · anagram

one argues -> SOPHIST: a sophist is a clever, plausible arguer, after the Greek teachers of rhetoric whose reasoning persuaded without being sound.

  • It's posh → ITSPOSH anagram fodder, seven letters once the apostrophe is dropped
  • abroad — A thing abroad is away from where it belongs, and these letters are not where they belong either.

Somebody insisting that things are altogether smarter overseas.

Everything before the comma is fodder, which is unusual: the S of the contraction counts as a letter, and 'abroad' is the only instruction in a sentence about being away.

16 across SCAMPI

Fraud detective tails out of a restaurant (6)

Definition: tails out of a restaurant · charade

tails out of a restaurant -> SCAMPI: scampi are the peeled tails of Dublin Bay prawns, breadcrumbed and fried, a fixture of British pub and restaurant menus.

  • Fraud → SCAM a fraud is a scam
  • detective → PI PI is a private investigator, the American private eye

A fraud squad detective tailing a suspect out of a restaurant.

'Tails' is a verb in the surface and a plural noun in the definition, and the detective shrinks to two letters instead of doing any following at all.

19 across BRACES

Rubbish bags tear in sets of two (6)

Definition: sets of two · container

sets of two -> BRACES: a brace is a pair, as in a brace of pheasants or a brace of goals, so the plural covers any number of such pairs.

  • Rubbish → BS rubbish is BS, the printable short form of the two-word dismissal, and the pair comes apart here
  • tear → RACE to tear down the road is to race along it
  • bags — To bag something is to drop it into a bag, so what stands beside this word ends up inside it.

Bin bags splitting open as they are carried out two at a time.

Every word of the surface belongs to bin day and not one of them survives the parse: 'bags' is an instruction, and 'tear' is speed rather than damage.

21 across AREOLAE

Rings from Circle Line stopping District Line ultimately (7)

Definition: Rings · container + last letter

Rings -> AREOLAE: an areola is the circle of darker skin round a nipple, and areolae is its Latin plural, used of any small coloured ring.

  • District → AREA a district is an area, and the word opens after three letters
  • Circle → O a circle drawn on the page is an O
  • Line → L L is the standard short form of line
  • Line ultimately → E the last letter of the second Line
  • stopping — A train stopping at a station comes to rest inside it, so these letters halt within the word around them.
  • ultimately — What comes ultimately comes last, so the final letter of the word in front is the only one taken.

A stalled Circle Line train bringing the District Line to a halt as well.

The Circle and the District are London Underground lines and the surface is a commuter's morning; the wordplay wants a circle, a line and a district as plain nouns.

23 across PARALYTICS

They can't easily move sports event, making politician tense (10)

Definition: They can't easily move · substitution

They can't easily move -> PARALYTICS: paralytics are people whose limbs are paralysed, so movement is exactly what is denied them.

  • sports event → PARALYMPICS the games staged after each Olympics for athletes with disabilities
  • politician → MP MP is a Member of Parliament, the letters that follow a British politician's name
  • tense → T T is the grammarian's short form of tense
  • making — To make one thing into another is to put the second in the first's place, so the letters named after it take over from the letters named before.

A sports event that leaves a politician on edge.

'Tense' is the politician's mood in the surface and the grammarian's noun in the wordplay, and the games take their name from running parallel to the Olympics.

25 across AVID

Eager leader carved out of hunk of marble? (4)

Definition: Eager · deletion

Eager -> AVID: an avid reader is an eager one, the adjective covering appetite for a thing rather than mere willingness.

  • hunk of marble? → DAVID Michelangelo cut his David from one block of Carrara marble
  • leader → D the front letter of that statue's name
  • leader — The leader of a word is the letter at its head, the one out in front of all the others.
  • carved out of — To carve a shape out of a block is to cut material away, so the letter named is cut from what comes after.

A keen leader immortalised in a block of marble.

'Hunk of marble' is one particular statue, and the question mark is the warning that a general phrase is standing in for a single famous example.

27 across ORIEL

Conservatives exposed Liberal - you can see through it (5)

Definition: you can see through it · deletion + charade

you can see through it -> ORIEL: an oriel is a bay window jutting out from an upper floor on a bracket, and a window is glass to look through.

  • Conservatives exposed → ORIE the Conservatives are the Tories, and the T and the S at the two ends come off
  • Liberal → L L is the abbreviation for Liberal on a ballot paper
  • exposed — Exposing a thing strips off whatever covers it, and what covers a word is the letter standing at each end.

The Tories showing up a Liberal whose story nobody is buying.

'You can see through it' works twice over: a transparent excuse in the surface, and a pane of glass in the answer. Tories is the everyday name for the Conservative party.

28 across REPRODUCE

South American neighbours, one heartless and one short, sent back copy (9)

Definition: copy · deletion + charade + reversal

copy -> REPRODUCE: to reproduce a document or a painting is to make a copy of it, the verb a printer or a gallery would use.

  • South American neighbours, one heartless → ECUDOR Ecuador and Peru share a border, and Ecuador is the one giving up the A at its centre
  • one short → PER Peru is the other, docked of its last letter
  • heartless — A heartless word has lost the letter at its centre, which is what leaves it with no heart.
  • short — A word cut short stops before it reaches its final letter.
  • sent back — In an across entry, sending letters back has them read from the right-hand end towards the left.

Two neighbouring countries sending a reporter's copy back, one of them cold and one of them curt.

'Copy' is a newsroom word in the surface and a plain verb in the definition, and 'neighbours' is the fair warning that the two countries have to share a border.

29 across BAGPIPER

Sack irrational salesperson lying about tattoo artist (8)

Definition: tattoo artist · charade + reversal

tattoo artist -> BAGPIPER: a military tattoo is an evening display of marching bands, and the Edinburgh one is led by pipers, so a piper is one of its performers.

  • Sack → BAG a sack is a bag
  • irrational → PI pi, the ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter, is the standard example of an irrational number
  • salesperson → PER a salesperson is a rep, and this one arrives the other way round
  • lying about — Something left lying about has been dropped any way up, so this word is read from its far end.

Dismissing an unreasonable salesman who tells lies about a tattooist.

A tattoo here is the military display of pipes and drums rather than the ink, the Edinburgh one being the famous example, and 'irrational' is a number rather than a temper.

30 across SEXTET

Three couples send rude messages on film (6)

Definition: Three couples · charade

Three couples -> SEXTET: a sextet is a group of six, musicians or dancers or anyone else, which is what three pairs come to.

  • send rude messages → SEXT to sext is to send a sexually explicit text message
  • film → ET Spielberg's ET, the film about the stranded alien

Three couples swapping filthy texts all through a film.

Both halves are recent enough to feel like slang: 'sext' reached the dictionaries in the 2000s, and the film's title is the two initials of extra-terrestrial.

1 down BROADEST

Trump holds course with extreme latitude (8)

Definition: extreme latitude · container

extreme latitude -> BROADEST: latitude is breadth or scope, so the widest allowance anyone could be given is the broadest.

  • Trump → BEST to trump a rival is to best them, and the word opens up after its first letter
  • course → ROAD the course a journey takes is the road it runs along
  • holds — To hold a thing is to keep it in your grasp, so one word here closes round another rather than sitting beside it.

Trump staying on course while being allowed an enormously free hand.

The capital T points at a president and does nothing else; 'trump' is the ordinary verb meaning to beat, and 'course' is a road rather than a direction of travel.

2 down BEEKEEPER

"Get in goal!" shouted manager of busy team? (9)

Definition: manager of busy team · homophone

manager of busy team -> BEEKEEPER: bees are proverbially busy and a hive works as one, so their keeper is the person managing the lot of them.

  • "Get in goal!" → BEEKEEPER telling a player to get in goal is telling them to be keeper, which is what every team calls that position
  • shouted — A shout arrives as noise from across a pitch, so the words are taken down by ear and spelt however the sound falls.

A manager bawling at a player to pull the gloves on and go in goal.

The shout and the answer are the same noise chopped in different places: two words become one, and the E that ends the first is heard again at the start of the second.

3 down LIRA

Spreader of fake news raises Republican money (4)

Definition: money · reversal

money -> LIRA: the lira was Italy's currency until the euro arrived, and it is still one of the coins a crossword means by plain 'money'.

  • Spreader of fake news → LI somebody who spreads fake news is a liar, and the first two letters of that word stay put
  • raises Republican → RA Republican is R, and lifting it up the entry puts it above the A it followed in liar
  • raises — Raising a letter in a down entry lifts it above its neighbour, so the pair it belongs to ends up written the other way round.

A propagandist talking up Republican campaign funds.

'Raises' looks like it should turn the whole word round, and it lifts a single letter one place instead. The letter doing the moving is the Republican R.

5 down ASSUMES

I'm not sure interrupting idiots is prejudicial (7)

Definition: prejudicial · container

prejudicial -> ASSUMES: to assume is to take something as true in advance, which is prejudging rather than weighing what is there.

  • idiots → ASSES an idiot is an ass, and the plural opens up in the middle
  • I'm not sure → UM um is the noise somebody makes when they have no answer ready
  • interrupting — To interrupt is to cut in on someone mid-sentence, so these letters land inside the word rather than before or after it.

Butting in on fools is unfair, says a speaker not certain of it.

'I'm not sure' looks like the setter hedging and is really the noise of hesitation written down. A phrase meaning doubt aloud is usually two or three letters in a cryptic.

6 down CARD READER

One used to pay charge for reassuring driving instructor (4,6)

Definition: One used to pay · double definition

A card reader is the terminal you tap to pay; divided a letter earlier it is a car dreader, which is the nervous pupil needing a calm instructor.

  • One used to pay → the machine on the shop counter that takes a debit or credit card and asks for the PIN
  • charge for reassuring driving instructor → a charge is a person in somebody's care, and the learner in that car is the one dreading it

Somebody who used to pay for a soothing driving instructor.

The second definition only works once the letters are divided one place to the left, which is why nothing in it sounds like a payment terminal. The pupil dreads the car.

7 down LOATH

'Unenthusiastic' is large word (5)

Definition: Unenthusiastic · charade

Unenthusiastic -> LOATH: loath means unwilling, and 'loath to admit it' is exactly the shrug of somebody with no appetite for doing it.

  • large → L L is the clothing size large, printed on every label
  • word → OATH an oath is a word in two senses, the curse and the solemn promise

Somebody remarking that 'unenthusiastic' is a long word.

The quotation marks turn the clue into a comment about a word, so 'large word' reads as a note on its length instead of two pieces waiting to be joined.

8 down SAMPLE

Taste first drops of Stella Artois, making Peroni look elegant (6)

Definition: Taste · first letters

Taste -> SAMPLE: to sample a beer is to take a little of it to taste, the same verb doing the same job in a more formal register.

  • Stella Artois, making Peroni look elegant → SAMPLE the opening letters of the six words, taken in the order they stand
  • first drops of — The first drop of a drink is the very start of it, so only the opening letter of each word that follows is poured out.

The first sips of a Stella Artois flattering the Peroni beside it.

Both lagers are real brands and the whole sentence is a drinker's verdict on them, which is what keeps the instruction disguised as the act of tasting.

9 down STRICT

No-nonsense detective abandoning precinct (6)

Definition: No-nonsense · deletion

No-nonsense -> STRICT: a strict teacher takes no nonsense, so the adjective covers anyone who holds to the rules without give.

  • precinct → DISTRICT an American police precinct is a district, the patch a station covers
  • detective → DI DI is a Detective Inspector, the rank in every British police drama
  • abandoning — To abandon a place is to walk out of it, so the two letters named in front leave the word that follows them.

A detective walking out on the precinct they work for.

'Precinct' is the American police word and DI the British rank, so a five-word clue manages to cross two different cop shows.

14 down PICCALILLI

Sauce, still primarily considered the greatest, served up poorly (10)

Definition: Sauce · charade + first letter + reversal

Sauce -> PICCALILLI: a mustard-yellow British pickle of chopped vegetables in a sharp sauce, set on the table beside cold meat and pork pies.

  • still → PIC a still is a single photograph lifted from a film, and a photograph is a pic
  • primarily considered → C the opening letter of considered
  • the greatest → ALI Muhammad Ali called himself the Greatest and the boxing world agreed with him
  • poorly → LLI somebody poorly is ill, and that word arrives climbing the entry
  • primarily — What comes primarily comes first, so only the opening letter of the word beside it is taken.
  • served up — In a down entry, up points back towards the start, so the word this governs is written from its last letter to its first.

A sauce still reckoned the finest, let down by careless serving.

Four pieces, and three of them are conventions the surface keeps out of sight: the photographic 'still', the boxer who is always the Greatest, and 'poorly' as plain ill.

17 down POLE VAULT

Staff leading Treasury try to pass the bar? (4,5)

Definition: try to pass the bar · charade

try to pass the bar -> POLE VAULT: the whole attempt is to clear a bar set overhead, which is the one thing the event asks of the athlete.

  • Staff → POLE a staff you lean on or carry is a pole
  • Treasury → VAULT the strongroom where a treasury keeps what it holds
  • leading — Whoever leads goes in front, so the staff is written before the treasury rather than after it.

The staff running the Treasury having a go at qualifying as lawyers.

Passing the bar is a lawyer's qualification in the surface and a height to be cleared in the answer, and 'staff' is the object you hold rather than the people you employ.

18 down DEADBEAT

Lazy person's pop hit plugged by Adele finally (8)

Definition: Lazy person · container + charade + last letter

Lazy person -> DEADBEAT: a deadbeat is an idler who lives off others and settles nothing, American slang that British papers now use without blinking.

  • pop → DAD pop is the affectionate word for a father, and it opens after its first letter
  • Adele finally → E the last letter of the singer's name
  • hit → BEAT to hit something is to beat it
  • plugged by — To plug a gap is to fill it, so the letter doing the plugging drops into a space opened inside the word.
  • finally — The final letter of a name is the last one, and that single letter is all the name contributes.

A slacker's pop single finally getting a plug from Adele.

'Pop' is the father rather than the music, and 'plugged' is the publicist's word on the surface and an instruction about a gap underneath.

20 down SET FREE

Wrote a crossword Independent released (3,4)

Definition: released · charade

released -> SET FREE: to set a prisoner or an animal free is to release it, the two-word idiom sitting in the same past tense as the single verb.

  • Wrote a crossword → SET whoever writes a crossword sets it, and the puzzle is said to be set
  • Independent → FREE an independent country or an independent thinker is a free one

The paper's crossword compiler being let go.

The italics point at the newspaper this puzzle appears in, which is the joke; the word itself is doing its ordinary work of meaning unattached to anyone.

21 down ACCEPT

Believe part of play about church power (6)

Definition: Believe · container

Believe -> ACCEPT: to accept a claim is to take it as true, which is believing it rather than merely putting up with it.

  • part of play → ACT a play is divided into acts, and this one splits after two letters
  • church → CE CE is the Church of England, the established church there
  • power → P P is the physics symbol for power
  • about — What is written about a thing goes round it, so the letters that follow are wrapped inside the word before them.

Being asked to take on trust a scene from a play about the church's power.

'About' is doing positional work in a sentence that wants it to mean concerning, and the church and the power are single letters rather than words.

22 down APLOMB

A quiet look by male bachelor shows confidence (6)

Definition: confidence · charade

confidence -> APLOMB: aplomb is confidence carried with poise, the borrowed French word for self-possession under pressure rather than mere self-belief.

  • A → A the first word of the clue is handed over as the letter it is
  • quiet → P p is piano in music, the mark telling a player to be quiet
  • look → LO lo is the old word for look, kept alive by lo and behold
  • male → M M is male on any form with a tick-box
  • bachelor → B B is bachelor, as in BA and BSc
  • by — A thing placed by another is placed beside it, so these pieces are laid side by side in the order the clue gives them.

An unmarried man's quiet glance giving away how sure of himself he is.

Five pieces come out of six words, and four of them are single-letter conventions worth owning: piano for quiet, M for male, B for bachelor, with the archaic 'lo' between them.

24 down RUING

Sorry for decision to expel student (5)

Definition: Sorry for · deletion

Sorry for -> RUING: to rue a thing is to regret it, so somebody ruing a decision is somebody sorry for having made it.

  • decision → RULING a decision handed down by a judge or a referee is a ruling
  • student → L L is the learner plate a British driving student carries
  • to expel — A student expelled from a school is thrown out of it, and this letter is thrown out of the word in the same way.

A school regretting its decision to throw a pupil out.

Expelling a student is the surface picture and the instruction at the same time, which is what makes 'to expel' read so naturally; the L is a plate rather than a person.

26 down SOME

Not a lot covered up by Kate Moss (4)

Definition: Not a lot · hidden word + reversal

Not a lot -> SOME: some is the quantity word for a moderate amount, more than none and short of many, as in 'we had some, not a lot'.

  • Kate Moss → EMOS four letters in a row, starting inside the first name and finishing inside the second
  • covered — What is covered lies concealed under something else, so these letters are sitting in the words exactly as printed.
  • up — A down entry runs downwards, so up takes the letters back towards the start and they are found here in the opposite order.

The model wearing very little indeed.

The name is doing nothing but holding letters, and the picture of a supermodel with not a lot covered up is what stops anybody reading the phrase one letter at a time.
