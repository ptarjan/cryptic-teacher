Across

1 across WATTS

Needs time for new power measurement (5)

Definition: power measurement · substitution

Power is measured in watts, so the definition names the answer as the unit any electrical reading is quoted in.

  • Needs → WANTS to need something is to want it, the plainest synonym in the clue
  • time for new → T time is T and new is N in crossword shorthand, so the T moves into the N's seat
  • for — In wordplay 'A for B' asks you to put A where B was standing, so one letter takes another's place.

A new way of measuring power that needs longer before it is ready.

'A for B' is the setter's swap instruction, and it passes here as ordinary English about a meter needing more time rather than as an order to exchange one letter.

4 across THIS

Indicated item belonging to ancient history (4)

Definition: Indicated item · hidden word

THIS is the word you use for the item you are pointing at, so the definition describes the demonstrative's job rather than giving a synonym.

  • ancient history → THIS four consecutive letters span the join between these two words, starting at the last letter of the first
  • belonging to — What belongs to something sits inside it, so the letters are already lying within the words named next.

An object that has come down to us from antiquity.

'Ancient history' is doing duty as the idiom for something long over, and the letters wanted lie across its middle. The definition is a pointing word rather than a thing.

6 across TORY

Politician closed railway (4)

Definition: Politician · charade

A Tory is a member of the British Conservative Party, so the answer is named by the class it is one example of.

  • closed → TO a door pushed to is a door closed — the adverb survives mainly in that phrase
  • railway → RY ry is the contraction used on maps and timetables for a railway line

A politician who shut a rail line down.

'Closed' hides the little adverb from 'pull the door to', which is why those two letters read as part of the sentence rather than as a piece of the answer.

10 across INN

Local Nordic people banning outsiders (3)

Definition: Local · deletion

Your local is the pub at the end of your road in British usage, and an inn is exactly that kind of house — the noun sense, not the adjective.

  • Nordic people → FINNS the people of Finland, of whom only the middle three letters are kept
  • banning outsiders — The outsiders of a word are its first and last letters, and banning them keeps only what lies between.

A northern nation shutting its borders to foreigners.

'Local' is a noun here, British for the pub you drink at, which is what makes a one-word definition fair. The surface reads as immigration policy instead.

11 across BALLY

Times describes everyone as “damned” (5)

Definition: damned · container

Bally is the Edwardian euphemism for 'bloody', so it stands in as a mild intensifier — 'you bally fool'.

  • Times → BY three times four is three by four, so the multiplication word gives these two letters, parted to make room
  • everyone → ALL everyone is all of them, and the three letters drop into the gap
  • describes — To describe a circle is to draw it round something, so the piece named first is written around the other.

The Times calling everybody damned.

The italics point at the newspaper while the wordplay wants arithmetic — six times seven is six by seven. The quotation marks round the definition are the setter flagging dated slang.

12 across MORAY

Fish ethically removed from lakes - quite the reverse (5)

Definition: Fish · deletion

A moray is a large eel of warm seas, so 'Fish' names the answer by the class it belongs to rather than by a synonym.

  • ethically → MORALLY behaving ethically is behaving in this manner
  • lakes → LL L is the map abbreviation for a lake, and two lakes give the pair that comes out
  • removed from — Removing one thing from another takes its letters out of the longer word, which is the operation the clue then turns round.
  • quite the reverse — Quite the reverse says the two nouns swap jobs: what looked like the thing being taken out is really the word being cut down.

Fish being lifted out of lakes by humane methods.

The subtraction is stated the wrong way round on purpose, and 'quite the reverse' is the setter's licence for it: the lakes come out of the ethics, not the other way about.

13 across EULER

Algebra oddly omitted by European University mathematician (5)

Definition: mathematician · charade + alternate letters

Euler was the Swiss mathematician behind the number e and the identity linking it to pi, so the word names him by his trade.

  • European → E E is the standard abbreviation for European
  • University → U U is the abbreviation for a university, as in the name of any of them
  • Algebra → LER its second, fourth and sixth letters, taken in order
  • oddly omitted — Omitting the odd ones leaves only the letters in even positions — the second, fourth and sixth.

A European university dropping algebra from the syllabus.

'Oddly omitted' reads as a casual complaint about a subject strangely left off the timetable, and it is a precise instruction about letter positions.

14 across SOLITUDE

Anxiety leaving Channel Islands in the absence of company (8)

Definition: the absence of company · deletion

Solitude is the state of being alone, so the phrase defines the condition itself rather than the loneliness someone might feel in it.

  • Anxiety → SOLICITUDE solicitude is anxious concern for somebody, the older and warmer word
  • Channel Islands → CI CI is the standard abbreviation for the Channel Islands, the British islands off the coast of Normandy
  • leaving — What leaves goes out, so the letters named next are taken away from the word standing before them.

Worry lifting from the Channel Islands once the visitors have gone.

The definition is the whole tail of the clue and reads as an ordinary continuation of the sentence; only 'leaving' marks the join between the two halves.

16 across EVERY NOW AND THEN

Henry wanted oven adjusted periodically (5,3,3,4)

Definition: periodically · anagram

Every now and then means at irregular intervals, which is what periodically covers in loose speech — both are phrases of frequency.

  • Henry wanted oven → HENRYWANTEDOVEN anagram fodder, fifteen letters
  • adjusted → anagram indicator
  • adjusted — Adjusting a thing shifts its parts into a new setting, so these letters will not stay in the order they are given.

Henry needing his cooker seen to from time to time.

The fodder is a flat little sentence about a man and his cooker, and its fifteen letters give no sign of the idiom they hold. 'Periodically' at the end is the definition, not part of the picture.

20 across ATTORNEY GENERAL

Lawyer who briefly represents German company (8,7)

Definition: Lawyer who briefly represents German company · cryptic definition

The post is the government's chief legal officer, a lawyer; read the other way, the two initials of that title are the German suffix for a public company.

  • Lawyer who briefly represents → the surface offers a solicitor standing in for a client at short notice
  • German company → AG follows the name of a German firm, short for Aktiengesellschaft, their equivalent of plc

A lawyer stepping in at short notice to act for a firm from Germany.

The mechanism runs backwards from the usual one: shorten the job title to its two initials and you have written the tag a German public company carries after its name.

23 across DOMINEER

Arrogantly command activist to carry bomb (8)

Definition: Arrogantly command · container

To domineer is to order people about high-handedly, so the adverb and verb together substitute for the single verb.

  • activist → DOER a doer is somebody who acts rather than talks; the word is split open to make room
  • bomb → MINE a mine is the bomb that waits buried in the ground or moored at sea
  • to carry — To carry a thing is to have it inside you, so the word named next is planted within the one already given.

Telling a campaigner high-handedly to carry an explosive.

'Activist' looks like the definition of a person and is a four-letter word to be prised apart. The definition is the two words at the front, an adverb and a verb read as one idea.

26 across COOMB

My storage unit in valley (5)

Definition: valley · charade

A coomb, also spelt combe, is a short valley or hollow in the hills, a word that survives mainly in southern English place names.

  • My → COO 'Coo!' is the British exclamation of mild surprise, the spoken equivalent of 'My!'
  • storage unit → MB MB is the megabyte, the unit a computer's storage is counted in

Somebody's lock-up sitting down in a valley.

'My' is an exclamation here rather than a possessive, which is exactly what makes the surface's rented lock-up so convincing.

29 across MIDGE

Small tailless pest (5)

Definition: pest · deletion

Midges are the tiny biting flies that swarm on summer evenings, so 'pest' defines the answer by what it does to people.

  • Small → MIDGET the dated word for a very small person, docked here of its last letter
  • tailless — A tail is the end of a thing, so a tailless word is written without its final letter.

A tiny pest that has no tail.

Three words, two of which could be definitions, and only 'tailless' says which is which: 'Small' is the wordplay and 'pest' the definition.

30 across GLASS

Almost expressionless composer (5)

Definition: composer · deletion

Philip Glass writes the repeating, slowly shifting music known as minimalism, so 'composer' names him by his trade.

  • expressionless → GLASSY the adjective for a blank, staring look, taken here without its final letter
  • Almost — Almost means not quite all of it, so the word beside it is written short of its last letter.

A composer whose face gives almost nothing away.

A surname on its own is fair for 'composer', the definition by example crosswords use constantly, and here it is left behind by a single docked letter.

31 across EGO

Self esteem of love god (3)

Definition: Self esteem · hidden word

In everyday use the ego is the opinion you hold of yourself, so the definition takes the popular sense rather than Freud's technical one.

  • love god → EGO three consecutive letters bridge the gap between these two words
  • of — Of carries the whole hiding instruction here, read as 'out of' — a looser signal than most setters allow themselves.

The vanity of a god of romance.

A love god invites Eros, Cupid and Amor, and none of them is wanted: the letters are lying in plain sight across the two words.

32 across TERN

Reportedly go off to see flier (4)

Definition: flier · homophone

A tern is a slender sea bird, so 'flier' names the answer by the broadest class it belongs to — anything that flies.

  • go off → TERN milk that goes off has turned, and that word said aloud is what the entry sounds like
  • Reportedly — What is reported is passed on by word of mouth, so the clue wants the sound of a word rather than its spelling.

Word is that somebody has set off to watch a bird.

'Go off' is the kitchen sense, milk turning, rather than departure — the false path the surface pushes you towards with 'to see'.

33 across ARMS

Cockney damages weapons (4)

Definition: weapons · deletion

Arms are weapons in the collective sense — to take up arms, a coat of arms — so the plural noun substitutes directly.

  • damages → HARMS the verb for doing damage, which loses its opening aitch in London speech
  • Cockney — A Cockney speaker drops the H at the front of a word, so that letter is missing from what the clue hands over.

Damage as a Londoner would pronounce it.

Cockney in a clue is nearly always an instruction about a missing aitch, and the dropped letter here turns a verb about hurting into a noun about hardware.

34 across AUNTS

Outings without Jack's relatives (5)

Definition: relatives · deletion

Aunts are the sisters of a parent, so 'relatives' names them by the wider family class they belong to.

  • Outings → JAUNTS short pleasure trips, the sense 'Outings' is pointing at
  • without Jack's → J the jack is J on a playing card, and that is the letter taken away
  • without — Without means lacking, so the letter named after it is missing from the word it comes out of.

Days out that leave Jack's family at home.

The apostrophe-s reads as a possessive belonging to Jack and is doing nothing of the sort — 'relatives' stands alone at the end as the definition.

Down

1 down WHITE METAL

Alloy Matthew and Eli smelted (5,5)

Definition: Alloy · anagram

White metal is any of the pale tin-and-lead based alloys used for bearings and cheap castings, so 'Alloy' names it by class.

  • Matthew and Eli → MATTHEWELI anagram fodder, ten letters, with the joining 'and' contributing none
  • smelted → anagram indicator
  • smelted — Smelting melts an ore right down so its material can be re-formed, and letters put through that do not keep the order they came in.

Two men melting metal down together in a foundry.

Two ordinary first names hold exactly the letters wanted, which is why they can be named outright without looking like fodder. Biblical-sounding names are a standard place for a setter to hide them.

2 down TONAL

Two notes, one endlessly rising, using conventional harmony (5)

Definition: using conventional harmony · charade + deletion + reversal

Tonal music is organised around a home key, which is the conventional harmony a Western ear expects to hear.

  • one endlessly → TON the second of the two is the word NOTE itself, docked of its last letter and then read from the bottom up
  • Two notes → AL the first is LA, the sixth degree of the sol-fa scale, its two letters also read upwards
  • endlessly — Something endless has had its end taken off, so the word it governs gives up its final letter.
  • rising — What rises travels upwards, and a down entry read upwards sets its letters down from the bottom of the grid to the top.

A pair of musical notes, one of them climbing without end.

'Two notes' offers LA, TE, MI and the rest, and the second of the pair is the word 'note' itself — the setter counting his own vocabulary as one of the two.

3 down SOBERLY

Seriously very well-built, we hear (7)

Definition: Seriously · homophone

To take something soberly is to take it seriously — the adverb's sense of grave and level-headed, not merely of being off the drink.

  • very well-built → SOBERLY a burly man is heavily built, and 'so' does the work of 'very'
  • we hear — What we hear is the sound of words rather than their spelling, so the answer is written differently from what is said.

Someone reporting that a man is impressively muscular.

Two words said quickly become one, and only a single vowel separates them from the entry. 'Seriously' at the front reads as an intensifier for what follows, and it is the definition.

4 down TELESCOPE

Crush an optical instrument (9)

Definition: Crush · double definition

As a verb it means to compress into less space; as a noun it is the lensed tube for viewing distant things — the verb was named after the object.

  • Crush → to squash things so they slide into one another and take up less room
  • an optical instrument → the tube of lenses an astronomer looks through

An instruction to smash a piece of viewing equipment.

The verb comes from the noun: the sections of the instrument slide inside each other when it is shut, so anything compressed that way is said to do the same.

5 down IDYLL

Poem your setter would loudly call pointless (5)

Definition: Poem · charade + deletion

An idyll is a short pastoral poem of the kind Theocritus wrote about shepherds, so the bare word names it by class.

  • your setter would → ID the setter speaking of himself is I, and 'I would' contracts to I'D
  • loudly call → YLL a loud call is a YELL, which arrives here stripped of its E
  • pointless — Pointless means having no point, and the points of the compass are single letters, so an E is what goes missing.

The setter shouting that somebody's poem has nothing to say.

'Pointless' finishes the surface's insult and names the compass point E for removal at the same time. 'Your setter' is the crossword's standard way of saying I.

7 down OARED

Audibly amazed to be provided with blades (5)

Definition: provided with blades · homophone

A boat fitted with oars is oared, and the flat end of an oar is its blade — so the phrase describes equipment, not weaponry.

  • amazed → OARED somebody amazed is awed, and in British speech that word and the entry are one syllable with the same sound
  • Audibly — Audibly means as heard rather than as written, so the entry is spelt differently from the word the clue hands you.

Someone gasping in astonishment at being handed a set of blades.

In non-rhotic British speech the R is silent, so the two spellings collapse into a single noise. 'Blades' means oars here rather than knives or grass.

8 down YO-YO

Ma, possibly, gets you toy (2-2)

Definition: toy · double definition

The toy is the spool that runs down its string and climbs back up; 'Ma, possibly' points at the cellist Yo-Yo Ma, whose forename it is.

  • Ma, possibly → 'possibly' flags a definition by example: the world's best-known cellist has this for a first name
  • toy → the spool on a string that drops and climbs back to the hand

A mother buying her child a plaything.

'Ma' reads as a mother, which is what hides the surname: Yo-Yo Ma is one of the few people whose forename fills a grid. The hyphen in the enumeration gives the doubling away.

9 down EMOTED

Showed strong feelings when first duke departs reduced in rank (6)

Definition: Showed strong feelings · deletion

To emote is to display feeling openly, often more than the moment warrants, which is what showing strong feelings describes.

  • reduced in rank → DEMOTED an officer sent down a grade has been given this treatment, the opposite of promotion
  • duke → D D is the standard abbreviation for a duke, and this is the letter that leaves
  • first — First fixes which letter goes: the one at the front of the word, not any of the others.
  • departs — Something that departs leaves altogether, so the letter it governs comes out of the word entirely.

Emotion on show as a duke, stripped of his rank, is the first to leave.

'Reduced in rank' looks as though it belongs to the duke, a nobleman losing his title, and it is the word the wordplay works on instead. Losing the D is what the surface calls his departure.

15 down ANKLE BOOTS

Perhaps not a bloke's type of footwear (5,5)

Definition: type of footwear · anagram

Ankle boots are boots cut to finish at the ankle instead of higher up the leg, so the phrase names them by class.

  • not a bloke's → NOTABLOKES anagram fodder, ten letters, the possessive S among them
  • Perhaps → anagram indicator
  • Perhaps — Perhaps says the letters offered are only one possible arrangement of themselves, so they may be set down in another.

Shoes that might not belong to a man.

'Perhaps' does anagram duty from the very front of the clue, where it reads as hedging about whose shoes these are. A setter's mildest indicators pass as ordinary qualification.

17 down EST

In short established a psychological training programme (3)

Definition: a psychological training programme · deletion

est was Erhard Seminars Training, the 1970s American self-improvement course run over long weekends in hotel ballrooms.

  • In short established → EST the shopfront contraction of 'established', the one printed beside a founding date
  • In short — In short calls for the everyday abbreviation of the word beside it, the one used on shopfronts and letterheads.

Someone who briefly set up a course in mental training.

'In short' looks like part of the story — a course established quickly — and is the instruction. The programme itself was always written in lower case, which is why the capital letters here mislead.

18 down ALGERIANS

25D confused Mr. Hislop's country-folk (9)

Definition: country-folk · anagram + charade

Algerians are the people of Algeria, so 'country-folk' means the folk of a country rather than rustics — the hyphen does the steering.

  • 25D → ALGER the solution to 25 down, its five letters resettled into a new order
  • Mr. Hislop's → IANS Ian Hislop edits the satirical magazine Private Eye; his forename plus the S of the possessive
  • confused — Something confused has been thrown out of its proper arrangement, so these letters will not stay in the order they are given.

Something has thrown the people of Mr Hislop's country into confusion.

A cross-reference is fodder like any other word: solve 25 down first and those five letters are the ones shuffled. The apostrophe-s does double duty, possessive in the surface and a plain letter in the wordplay.

19 down HER

Picture of goddess scratching bottom (3)

Definition: Picture · deletion

Her is Spike Jonze's 2013 film about a man in love with an operating system, so 'Picture' defines the answer as a movie title.

  • goddess → HERA the queen of the Greek gods, wife of Zeus, whose final letter is scratched off
  • scratching bottom — Scratching a thing out removes it, and the bottom of a down entry is its last letter, so that letter goes.

A portrait of a goddess with the bottom scraped away.

A pronoun looks unclueable as an answer until you notice that films get titles like that, and the capital P of 'Picture' is the only sign the definition is a proper noun.

21 down RAIDER

Pirate king and queen capturing assistant (6)

Definition: Pirate · container

A raider makes sudden armed attacks in order to plunder, which is what a pirate does at sea — the noun substitutes directly.

  • king → R R is Rex, the Latin king who signs royal documents
  • assistant → AID an aid is a help or a helper, as in a teaching aid
  • queen → ER ER is Elizabeth Regina, the royal monogram on British postboxes
  • capturing — Capturing somebody is taking them and holding them inside, so the piece named next sits within the letters already assembled.

A pirate king and queen taking a helper prisoner.

The 'and' between king and queen reads as a royal couple in the surface and as a plain join in the wordplay, which is what stops the two monarchs looking like one piece.

22 down NICOSIA

Revolutionary top socialist in capital (7)

Definition: capital · charade + reversal

Nicosia is the capital of Cyprus, still split between the Greek and Turkish sides, so the bare word names it by class.

  • in → NI the clue's own little word, set down from the bottom upwards
  • socialist → COS soc. is the standard short form of a socialist, its three letters then read upwards
  • top → IA A1 means first-rate, the crossword writes its 1 as an I, and the pair is read upwards
  • Revolutionary — A revolution turns a thing full circle, so the letters it governs are written in the opposite order — bottom to top in a down entry.

A revolutionary senior socialist somewhere in the capital.

Every piece is small and every piece is turned, and one of them is 'in' — a word that reads as pure grammar and is really two of the letters.

24 down ORDER

Type of rook entering river (5)

Definition: Type · container

An order is a class or category — biology uses it for the rank above family — so 'Type' is a plain synonym in the noun sense.

  • river → ODER the Oder runs along the border between Germany and Poland, and it is opened up after its first letter
  • rook → R R is the chess notation for a rook
  • entering — Something entering goes inside, so the letter named first is placed within the word that follows it.

A rook flying down into a river.

'Type of rook' reads as a species of bird, which is the false path: 'Type' has already finished on its own as the definition, and the rook is the chess piece.

25 down ELGAR

Tease the French over composer (5)

Definition: composer · charade + reversal

Elgar wrote the Enigma Variations and the Pomp and Circumstance marches, so 'composer' names him by his trade.

  • the French → EL le is French for 'the', its two letters read upwards
  • Tease → GAR to rag somebody is to tease them, and those three letters are set down bottom to top
  • over — Turning something over leaves it the other way up, so these letters are written back to front — bottom to top in a down entry.

Making fun of a Frenchman on top of a musician.

'The French' asks for the French word for 'the' rather than for a nationality, which is one of the most reliable two-letter gifts a setter can offer.

27 down OCEAN

Main part of our home (5)

Definition: Main · double definition

'The main' is the old poetic noun for the open sea, and salt water is the greater part of the planet everyone lives on.

  • Main → the poets' noun for the open sea, as in the Spanish Main
  • part of our home → water covers seven tenths of the planet we all live on

The largest part of the place we live in.

'Main part' looks like one phrase meaning the biggest bit, and the two words belong to different halves of the clue. 'Main' as a noun for the sea now survives in poetry and crosswords.

28 down SMUT

Primarily salacious material used titillatingly (4)

Definition: Primarily salacious material used titillatingly · &lit + first letters

Read straight through, the clue states what the thing is: obscene material published to excite, so the whole sentence serves as the definition.

  • salacious → S its opening letter; salacious means lewd
  • material → M its opening letter; the material is the stuff itself, in print or on film
  • used → U its opening letter, taken like the two before it
  • titillatingly → T its opening letter; to titillate is to excite mildly
  • Primarily — Primarily means first of all, so each word it governs surrenders its opening letter and nothing more.

A blunt description of what pornography is and what it is for.

The sentence describes exactly what its own initials spell, so definition and wordplay are the same four words read two different ways.
