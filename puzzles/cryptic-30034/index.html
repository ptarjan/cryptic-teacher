Across

1 across DOG'S BREAKFAST

How many setters start the day in chaos? (4.9)

Definition: chaos · charade

chaos → DOG'S BREAKFAST: the idiom names a job made such a mess of that nothing in it is in order, not any actual meal.

  • many setters → DOGS a setter is a gun dog — the red setter, the Irish setter — so several setters are several of those
  • start the day → BREAKFAST the meal you start the day with
  • How → surface only: the question word that turns the wordplay into a riddle, and it hands over no letters

Crossword compilers asked how their mornings go, with the suggestion that they begin in disorder.

'Setters' is the trap: it reads as the people who write these puzzles, and the wordplay wants the breed of dog instead.

10 across APENNINES

A long lens ultimately keeping Lenny’s body in range (9)

Definition: range · charade + container + last letter + middle letters

range → APENNINES: a plain category definition — the Apennines are the mountain chain down the spine of Italy, one range among many.

  • A → A the clue's own opening word, taken as it stands
  • long → PINE to long for something is to pine for it
  • lens ultimately → S the last letter of lenS
  • Lenny’s body → ENN lENNy with head and feet removed
  • ultimately — What comes ultimately is what comes last, so only the closing letter of the word beside it is taken.
  • keeping — Keeping something means holding it inside you, so the piece kept sits within the letters doing the keeping.
  • body — A body is the trunk once head and feet are off, so the name loses its first and last letters and the middle survives.

A photographer with a telephoto lens keeping Lenny's torso inside the frame.

'Range' is mountains rather than the reach of a lens, and this chain runs the length of Italy. 'Long' is the verb: to long is to pine.

11 across ABETS

Supports while accepting risk (5)

Definition: Supports · container

Supports → ABETS: the legal sense of support, encouraging and helping somebody in a crime, as in aiding and abetting.

  • while → AS while and as both introduce something happening at the same time
  • risk → BET a bet is money put at risk
  • accepting — To accept something is to take it in, so the word in front opens up and the other piece goes inside it.

Somebody standing by a friend and taking on the danger that comes with it.

'Supports' is the courtroom sense — helping somebody do wrong — and the two-letter conjunction carrying the wordplay is easy to read straight past.

12 across ENSUE

Check Republican’s been sacked – result! (5)

Definition: result · deletion

result → ENSUE: to ensue is to follow on as a consequence, which is what a thing does when it results from something.

  • Check → ENSURE to check that something is so is to ensure it
  • Republican → R R is the standard short form of the American party
  • been sacked — Being sacked is being put out of your place, so the letter named alongside is dismissed from the longer word.

A political headline: a Republican loses his job, and see what happens next.

'Check' looks like an order to stop or inspect something and is a six-letter synonym for making sure. 'Result!' is the definition, a verb, dressed as a football fan's shout.

13 across NEIGHBOUR

Awkward our being close to Spanish border (9)

Definition: border · anagram + last letter

border → NEIGHBOUR: countries that neighbour each other run along the same boundary, so to border is exactly to neighbour.

  • our being → OURBEING anagram fodder, eight letters
  • close to Spanish → H the last letter of SpanisH
  • Awkward → anagram indicator
  • Awkward — An awkward thing will not lie the way it was put down, and these letters refuse the order they are given in.
  • close to — The close of a word is where it finishes, so this asks for the single letter the next word ends on.

Two people uncomfortably aware of one another as they approach the Spanish frontier.

'Spanish border' wants to be read as one phrase pointing at geography; the border is the verb, what one country does along the edge of another, and 'Spanish' only lends a letter.

14 across IONESCO

Is company taking on somebody as playwright? (7)

Definition: playwright · container

playwright → IONESCO: definition by example, since he is one dramatist standing in for the whole category rather than a synonym for it.

  • Is → IS the clue's own second word, used as it stands
  • company → CO Co. is the abbreviation on a firm's letterhead
  • somebody → ONE one is the impersonal somebody — one does not do that sort of thing
  • taking on — A firm taking somebody on brings them in among its own people, so the new piece lands inside the letters already there.

A firm hiring a new employee, with a hint that the recruit writes for the stage.

Eugène Ionesco was the Romanian-French father of absurdist theatre — Rhinoceros, The Bald Soprano — and the question mark admits that the definition names a trade, not the man.

16 across SNUFF IT

Enjoyment’s over – ready to leave for good (5,2)

Definition: leave for good · charade + reversal

leave for good → SNUFF IT: the informal phrase means to die, the one departure nobody comes back from.

  • Enjoyment’s over → SNUF enjoyment is fun, and FUN'S turned over gives these four letters
  • ready → FIT somebody ready for action is fit for it
  • over — Something turned over lands the other way up, so the word before it is read from its last letter back to its first.

The party has ended and somebody is packed and willing to walk out for the last time.

To snuff it is British slang for dying, from snuffing out a candle, which is what the polite 'leave for good' is getting at.

18 across GLEANED

‘Gathered government’s not productive’ (Davey) (7)

Definition: Gathered · charade

Gathered → GLEANED: to glean is to gather what is left in a field after the harvest, and by extension to gather scraps of information.

  • government → G G is the standard short form of government
  • not productive → LEAN a lean year yields very little
  • (Davey) → ED Ed Davey has led the Liberal Democrats, Britain's third party, since 2020

A politician quoted in the press, writing off the government's record as unproductive.

Quotation marks and a name in brackets make the clue look like a press cutting, which is the only reason the opening word reads as reported speech rather than as the definition.

20 across BESPOKE

Keep boss cut off, exactly as ordered (7)

Definition: exactly as ordered · anagram + deletion

exactly as ordered → BESPOKE: a bespoke suit is cut to the customer's own measurements, made to order rather than bought off the peg.

  • Keep → KEEP anagram fodder, four letters as they stand
  • boss cut → BOS BOSS shortened by its last letter
  • off → anagram indicator
  • cut — A word cut is a word shortened, and what goes is the letter on the end of it.
  • off — Milk left out goes off, and letters gone off no longer sit in the order they were in.

An instruction to keep the manager out of the way, and to do it precisely as told.

'Cut off' reads as one phrase meaning excluded, and the two words are doing separate jobs: one trims a letter, the other shuffles what is left.

21 across LIFE CLASS

Go with girl to collect a little cabinet – it should contain drawers (4,5)

Definition: it should contain drawers · container + first letter

it should contain drawers → LIFE CLASS: the pun is on drawers as people who draw — the room where artists sketch a live model.

  • Go → LIFE go meaning vim and vigour — plenty of go in him, full of life
  • girl → LASS a lass is a girl in northern English and Scottish usage
  • a little cabinet → C just the first letter of Cabinet
  • to collect — Collecting something is gathering it in, so the piece named next is taken inside the letters already assembled.
  • a little — A little of a word is the smallest part you can take of it, which is the letter it begins with.

Furniture shopping: fetch a small cabinet, and make sure the thing has drawers in it.

'Drawers' is underwear in the surface and people who draw in the definition, and the room they fill is the art class working from a nude model.

23 across CARGO

Load coach before journey (5)

Definition: Load · charade

Load → CARGO: the goods a ship, plane or lorry carries, a load in the freight sense rather than a burden.

  • coach → CAR a railway coach is a car
  • journey → GO to journey somewhere is to go there
  • before — A piece placed before another comes first in the answer, so these letters lead and the rest follow on.

Getting the bus loaded up before everyone sets off on a trip.

'Coach' is the railway carriage rather than the bus or the trainer, and 'load' is the noun — what a ship carries, not the act of loading it.

25 across TROOPSHIP

I made a mistake taking heroin during journey – 19s might be on it (9)

Definition: 19s might be on it · container + charade

19s might be on it → TROOPSHIP: 19 down is a draftee, and a troopship is the vessel that carried conscripted soldiers overseas.

  • journey → TRIP a trip is a journey — and, with a drug in the clue, the other kind too
  • I made a mistake → OOPS what you actually say out loud the moment you have made one
  • heroin → H H is the street abbreviation for heroin
  • taking — Somebody taking a drug has it inside them, so the drug's letter joins the letters that swallowed it.
  • during — What happens during something happens inside it, so the assembled letters land in the middle of the word beside it.

A traveller confessing that taking heroin somewhere along the way was a bad idea.

'I made a mistake' is not a story but a quotation, the word you say at the time. 19 down is a draftee, and conscripts were carried to their wars aboard these.

26 across PRINCE ANDREWS CLOSE

Prices down, cleaners refurbished undesirable address in Norwich? (6,7,5)

Definition: undesirable address in Norwich? · anagram

undesirable address in Norwich? → PRINCE ANDREWS CLOSE: a real Norwich street whose name, since the former prince's disgrace, is the undesirable part of living there.

  • Prices down, cleaners → PRICESDOWNCLEANERS anagram fodder, eighteen letters
  • refurbished → anagram indicator
  • refurbished — A refurbished building is stripped out and put back together in a new arrangement, and the letters get the same treatment.

A small ad for a run-down block: prices cut, cleaners renewed, at an address nobody wants.

The question mark carries the joke. Nothing is wrong with the houses — the street is named after the royal who was stripped of his titles, and residents of such roads have been asking for new signs.

2 down OBEISANCE

Honour thrilled niece – ASBO! (9)

Definition: Honour · anagram

Honour → OBEISANCE: the noun means homage paid to a superior — honour done to somebody, not honour possessed.

  • niece – ASBO → NIECEASBO anagram fodder, nine letters
  • thrilled → anagram indicator
  • thrilled — Someone thrilled is shaken to the core, and letters shaken that hard will not stay in the order they were handed over in.

A tabloid snippet: a niece delighted by some award, then the sting of a court order.

An ASBO was the Anti-Social Behaviour Order English magistrates handed to persistent nuisances, which is why the clue can print it as news. The word wanted is a deep bow of respect.

3 down SINGE

Confess, having originally eaten char (5)

Definition: char · charade + first letter

char → SINGE: to char is to scorch a surface, and to singe is that same light burning, both verbs and neither needing a flame.

  • Confess → SING the crook who sings tells the police everything
  • originally eaten → E the first letter of Eaten
  • originally — What a word was originally is what it opens with, so only the very first letter of the word beside it is wanted.

An accusation round the table: own up, you were the one who ate the char.

'Char' is placed to look like the fish or the cleaning lady and it is the definition, a verb — to scorch something brown.

4 down RUN INTO

Drive home to meet (3,4)

Definition: meet · charade

meet → RUN INTO: the phrasal verb is the accidental meeting, bumping into somebody you were not looking for rather than keeping an appointment.

  • Drive → RUN a drive out in the car is also a run
  • home → IN somebody at home is in, the way a caller finds them
  • to → TO the word is lifted from the surface exactly as it stands

Directions for the end of a journey: drive back to the house in time to see somebody.

'To' does double duty, holding the sentence up and handing over its own two letters. 'Home' is the crossword's IN — at home, so in.

5 down ASSAILS

Lays into American behind troubles (7)

Definition: Lays into · charade

Lays into → ASSAILS: both mean to set about a person with blows or abuse, and both are third-person singular verbs.

  • American behind → ASS the American word for the backside, where a British speaker says arse
  • troubles → AILS whatever ails you is troubling you

A row in which somebody tears strips off an American standing behind all the trouble.

'Behind' is doing anatomy rather than position, and 'lays into' is the definition in its physical sense — going at somebody with fists or with words.

6 down FLASHGUNS

Gordon, comically tight, sent back shooting accessories (9)

Definition: shooting accessories · charade + reversal

shooting accessories → FLASHGUNS: a flashgun is the lamp clipped to a camera, and a photographer shoots pictures with one.

  • Gordon, comically → FLASH the comic strip supplies the first name of its spaceman Gordon
  • tight, sent back → GUNS a tight fit is a snug one, written backwards
  • sent back — Anything sent back arrives the other way round, so the word before it is written out from its final letter to its first.

A man called Gordon, funny once he has had a drink, returns some hunting kit.

'Shooting' is photography, not the field sport, and 'tight' can mean drunk or close-fitting — the wordplay wants the second sense while the surface sells the first.

7 down SEE TO

Take responsibility for diatomic carbon, they said (3,2)

Definition: Take responsibility for · homophone

Take responsibility for → SEE TO: to see to something is to take charge of it and get it done, the phrase a host uses about arrangements.

  • diatomic carbon → C2 carbon's chemical symbol is C, and a diatomic molecule has two atoms, so chemists write C2
  • they said → SEE TO say the formula out loud and the letter becomes one word, the digit another
  • they said — Reporting what people say points at the noise the wordplay makes rather than the way it is spelt.

A chemistry claim quoted at second hand: apparently carbon goes about in pairs.

Chemistry supplies a sound, not letters: the formula for the two-atom molecule is what you say aloud, and English spells that noise quite differently.

8 down WATERING PLACE

Women doctors taking train east nervous – ‘Where can one get a drink?’ (8,5)

Definition: Where can one get a drink? · charade + container + anagram

Where can one get a drink? → WATERING PLACE: the phrase names a spa or a pub, and before that the spot where animals go down to drink.

  • Women → W W is the standard short form of women, as on a lavatory door
  • train east → ATERIN TRAIN plus E for east, six letters resettled
  • doctors → GP a GP is a general practitioner, the family doctor
  • doctors → LACE the same word read as a verb — to doctor a drink is to lace it
  • taking — One thing taking another swallows it whole, so the letters being taken end up inside the letters doing the taking.
  • nervous — A nervous person cannot keep still, and letters that cannot keep still do not stay in the order the clue gives them.

Female medics on an eastbound train, jittery, while somebody asks where the bar is.

'Doctors' is spent twice, once as the noun for a family physician and once as the verb for spiking a drink. Nothing in the clue warns you that one word is doing two jobs.

9 down ESPRIT DE CORPS

Dire prospects unsettled group morale (6,2,5)

Definition: group morale · anagram

group morale → ESPRIT DE CORPS: the borrowed French names the shared pride and loyalty holding a team or a regiment together.

  • Dire prospects → DIREPROSPECTS anagram fodder, thirteen letters
  • unsettled → anagram indicator
  • unsettled — To unsettle something is to knock it out of the arrangement it had settled into, which is what happens to these letters.

A gloomy forecast: the outlook is bad, and the mood of the team has gone with it.

The phrase came into English from French military usage — the spirit of the body of men — and English borrowed the whole thing rather than translating it.

15 down SUNSCREEN

Students about to have sex endlessly – nurse providing some sort of protection (9)

Definition: some sort of protection · charade + reversal + deletion

some sort of protection → SUNSCREEN: it protects skin from ultraviolet burning, one particular kind of protection, which is what 'some sort of' concedes.

  • Students about → SUN British students belong to the NUS, the National Union of Students, turned round here
  • to have sex endlessly → SCRE to screw is to have sex, and the last letter never arrives
  • nurse → EN an EN is an enrolled nurse on a British hospital rota
  • about — Turning about is facing the other way, so the letters in front of it are written out from the back.
  • endlessly — A word taken endlessly never reaches its end, so the final letter is dropped before the rest is used.

Undergraduates on the pull with a nurse hovering nearby, all under the heading of staying safe.

'Protection' is the misdirection: the smutty surface points at contraception, and the answer is the lotion that guards you against the sun.

17 down FLOOR SHOW

Shocks on the way in cabaret (5,4)

Definition: cabaret · charade

cabaret → FLOOR SHOW: the entertainment put on at floor level in a nightclub, cabaret's other name.

  • Shocks → FLOORS news that floors you knocks you flat
  • the way → HOW the way you did it is how you did it
  • on — In a down entry one piece sits on another like a brick, so the first word's letters go above and the second word's below.

Bad news reaching somebody as they walk in through the door of a nightclub.

A floor show is the cabaret performed among the tables rather than up on a stage, which is how the surface manages to walk you in through the entrance.

19 down DRAFTEE

Crazy English repeatedly defending king – one chosen to serve (7)

Definition: one chosen to serve · container + charade

one chosen to serve → DRAFTEE: the American word for a conscript, somebody selected by the draft for military service instead of enlisting.

  • Crazy → DAFT daft is the everyday British word for crazy
  • English repeatedly → EE English is E in wordplay shorthand, and it is asked for twice
  • king → R R is rex, Latin for king, the letter monarchs sign after their name
  • repeatedly — Doing a thing repeatedly means doing it more than once, so the letter it points at is written twice over.
  • defending — Defenders stand round whatever they are protecting, so the letters doing the defending close over the one named next.

Mad Englishmen standing up for their king over and over, and somebody picked out for duty.

American conscription is the sense wanted: a draftee is called up rather than volunteering, while the surface sells you a crowd of patriots.

20 down BASSOON

Idiot stops benefit – blow it! (7)

Definition: blow it · container

blow it → BASSOON: a definition by what you do with the object, since the bassoon makes its low buzzing note only when somebody blows into the reed.

  • benefit → BOON a boon is a blessing, something you benefit from
  • Idiot → ASS an ass is a fool
  • stops — To stop a hole is to plug it, so one piece is jammed inside the letters lying on either side of it.

A fool blocking somebody's welfare payment, with an exasperated curse thrown in at the end.

'Blow it!' reads as the curse and is really an instruction: the answer is the thing you blow, the orchestra's deep double-reed woodwind.

22 down FLOUR

Cake maker with large number over (5)

Definition: Cake maker · container

Cake maker → FLOUR: the ingredient that makes a cake a cake, credited as its maker in place of the baker who mixes it.

  • number → FOUR the number itself, spelled out
  • large → L L is the clothes-shop large
  • over — One thing laid over another covers it completely, so the number closes round the single letter.

A baker with a large number of cakes left over at the end of the day.

'Cake maker' wants to be a person and is an ingredient, and 'over' is a covering instruction rather than the leftovers the surface is counting.

23 down CAPER

Skip picked up more packing cases (5)

Definition: Skip · hidden word + reversal

Skip → CAPER: to caper is to skip and frisk about, the light-footed jump a lamb makes, and both are verbs.

  • more packing → REPAC five letters in a row run across the gap between these two words
  • picked up — In a down entry the letters climb, so a run picked up is read from the bottom of the grid towards the top.
  • cases — To case something is to enclose it, so the letters wanted are shut inside the two words in front.

A skip being hoisted away with a few more packing cases loaded into it.

'Skip' looks like the builder's rubbish container the surface is describing and it is a verb, to skip along. The letters are already in the clue, running the wrong way.
