Across

1 across SHEDS A TEAR

Edward's of Islamic faith, Spooner tells us, and is visibly moved (5,1,4)

Definition: is visibly moved · spoonerism

  • Edward's → TEDS Ted is the everyday short form of Edward
  • of Islamic faith → SHIA the Shia are one of the two great branches of Islam
  • Spooner tells us → SHEDS A TEAR say those three words with the opening sounds of the first and last traded, T for SH

A spoonerism is easiest solved from the definition end: think of the phrase for weeping, trade its opening sounds back, and the clue's picture of Edward appears. Everyman always names the reverend so you know to try it.

6 across SCAR

Mark and Cliff (4)

Definition: Mark · double definition

  • Mark → the line a healed wound leaves behind on skin
  • Cliff → a bare rock face on a northern English fell, a word that turns up all over Yorkshire place names

Two capital letters do all the misleading: Mark and Cliff read as a pair of blokes, and neither is a name here. The second sense is landscape English rather than dictionary English.

9 across DEMOCRATIC

Comic rated 'poor' as the public chooses (10)

Definition: as the public chooses · anagram

  • Comic rated → COMICRATED ten letters of fodder, the two words run together

The quotation marks are the giveaway. The reviewer's verdict is the instruction, while the word immediately before it is material, and 'a comic rated poor' is a perfectly ordinary sentence meaning none of that.

10 across SPAR

Pole and box (4)

Definition: Pole · double definition

  • Pole → a length of timber or metal holding up a ship's sails
  • box → to trade practice punches in the ring rather than fight in earnest

The capital P offers a nationality and 'box' offers a container; neither reading survives. One noun and one verb, sharing a spelling and nothing else.

11 across ONCE IN A WHILE

Love disposing of Chilean wine now and again (4,2,1,5)

Definition: now and again · anagram

  • Love → O love is a score of nothing at tennis, written as a zero
  • Chilean wine → CHILEANWINE eleven letters of fodder, which with the tennis score make the twelve wanted

'Love disposing of' reads as getting rid of the O, and that is the trap. Dispose here is the tidy-minded sense, arrange, so the O joins the shuffle instead of leaving it.

15 across REINS IN

Checks score in Singapore when some are ignored (5,2)

Definition: Checks · hidden word

  • score in Singapore → REINSIN seven letters running consecutively across the three words, beginning part-way into the first

'Checks score' reads as one phrase about a scoreboard, which is the misdirection: the split falls after the very first word, and the rest of the clue is the hiding place.

16 across NUTTIER

Increasingly deranged old teachers having row (7)

Definition: Increasingly deranged · charade

  • old teachers → NUT the National Union of Teachers, a British union that existed until it merged into a larger one in 2017, hence 'old'
  • row → TIER a row of seats set one above another, as in a theatre

The surface promises a staffroom argument, and 'row' is the pivot: read as a quarrel it goes nowhere, read as a line of seats it pays.

17 across CHATGPT

Chirpy haphazard assistant that'll give plausibleish text, primarily? (7)

Definition: Chirpy haphazard assistant that'll give plausibleish text, primarily? · &lit + first letters

  • Chirpy haphazard assistant that'll give plausibleish text → CHATGPT seven words standing in a row, each handing over the letter it begins with

An all-in-one: the sentence is both the description and the recipe, so nothing is left over. 'Plausibleish' is the joke — confident, fluent and not necessarily right.

19 across THROUGH

Cast can be heard from beginning to end (7)

Definition: from beginning to end · homophone

  • Cast → THROUGH to cast a stone is to throw it, and cast is already a past tense, so the word said aloud is the past tense too

'Cast' looks like the theatrical noun and is a verb, and specifically a past tense — that is what makes the sound land. Everyman's 'can be heard' is a homophone marker in plain clothes.

20 across DOWN THE AISLE

As trolleys and some trains proceed? (4,3,5)

Definition: As trolleys and some trains proceed? · cryptic definition

  • As trolleys → the surface offers a supermarket, where a trolley is pushed along the gangway between the shelves
  • some trains proceed → the trains the setter means are not railway ones but the trailing part of a bride's dress, which proceeds at walking pace

No wordplay to find, only two senses of 'train' — the railway sort with a gangway down the middle, and the sort a wedding dress has. Both go the same way to get where they are going.

23 across ROVE

After drop of Riesling (round #5), Everyman's beginning to ramble (4)

Definition: ramble · charade + first letters

  • Riesling → R the first letter of the German white wine's name
  • round → O a round shape drawn on the page is the letter O
  • #5 → V the hash sign means number, and five as a Roman numeral is V
  • Everyman's → E the setter signs his own puzzle and only his initial is wanted

Everyman writes himself into his clues most weeks, so his name in a clue is nearly always one letter. The bracket is the tricky part: a hash makes 5 a number, and a number wants Roman numerals.

24 across WIDOW'S PEAK

Women's group talk including stock index and what may resemble recession (6,4)

Definition: what may resemble recession · charade + container

  • Women's group → WI the Women's Institute, the long-established British organisation of countrywomen
  • stock index → DOW the Dow Jones average, the New York market's headline index
  • talk → SPEAK a plain synonym, five letters

Every money word is misdirection: the recession here is a hairline in retreat, not an economy shrinking. The name comes from an old superstition that the shape foretold early widowhood.

25 across SASH

Frame small tree (4)

Definition: Frame · charade

  • small → S S is the clothes-label abbreviation for small
  • tree → ASH a common British hardwood, three letters

Three words, no indicator, no joinery: the shortest kind of charade there is. 'Frame' is the window sense, not a picture's or a boxer's.

26 across ASSISTANCE

Nitwit with one point of view: some help! (10)

Definition: some help · charade

  • Nitwit → ASS an ass is a fool, the donkey pressed into service as an insult
  • one → I the Roman numeral for one
  • point of view → STANCE the position you take on a question is your stance

The exclamation mark sells 'some help!' as sarcasm aimed at the nitwit, and that is the misdirection — it is the straight definition. I for one is the commonest single letter in the game.

Down

1 down SODS

Lays the ground for irritating types (4)

Definition: Lays the ground · double definition

  • Lays the ground → turf arrives as cut squares of earth and grass, and laying them over bare soil is a groundsman's job
  • irritating types → mild British abuse for an annoying person, printable enough for a Sunday paper

Two plain senses laid side by side with 'for' as the only join. The gardening one is the one that hides: a lawn is laid in squares, and the verb comes straight off the noun for a square.

2 down EMMA

Bovary? A Madame that's written about? (4)

Definition: Bovary? · charade + reversal

  • A → A the indefinite article, taken at face value as a single letter
  • Madame → MME Mme is the standard French abbreviation for Madame, as printed on an envelope

The italics sell you a novel title, and the definition really is its heroine, named by her surname alone. The wordplay treats the same word as a scrap of everyday French correspondence.

3 down SECOND SIGHT

Prediction: more food will lead to fight not starting (6,5)

Definition: Prediction · charade + deletion

  • more food → SECONDS a second helping at the table is called seconds
  • fight → IGHT the word for a scrap, without the F it begins with

The colon is doing the work of an equals sign. On the surface a canteen queue turns nasty; the definition is the fairground gift of knowing what happens next.

4 down TRADE-IN

Traffic; perhaps old vehicle (5-2)

Definition: Traffic · double definition

  • Traffic → to traffic in something is to deal in it, buying and selling
  • perhaps old vehicle → the car you hand the garage counts against the price of the new one

The semicolon is the join. One half is a verb about dealing, the other the noun a dealer uses, and 'perhaps' concedes that a car is only one of the things you can hand over.

5 down AVIGNON

Mixed gin in English ceremonial county - or somewhere in Provence (7)

Definition: somewhere in Provence · container + anagram

  • English ceremonial county → AVON Avon, centred on Bristol and Bath, was an English county until 1996 and still names the area's police and fire services
  • gin → IGN the three letters of the drink, stirred and then dropped into the middle of the county

Everyman keeps the surface thoroughly English, a measure of gin and a shire, and lands the definition in the south of France. 'Ceremonial county' is the signal that a place name is wanted as letters rather than as a place.

7 down CAPRICIOUS

Prosaic echinus every so often removed: that's somewhat whimsical (10)

Definition: somewhat whimsical · anagram + alternate letters

  • Prosaic → PROSAIC seven letters of fodder, handed over whole
  • echinus → CIU the second, fourth and sixth letters of the zoologist's name for a sea urchin, the other four dropped

'Somewhat' reads like a marker for something buried in the clue's letters, and that is the false trail; it only softens the definition. The whole misdirection sits in an unfamiliar name for a common seaside creature.

8 down RARE EARTHS

Wrongfully arrest and hear certain elements (4,6)

Definition: certain elements · anagram

  • arrest → ARREST six letters of fodder
  • hear → HEAR four more, joined on by the clue's own 'and'

'Wrongfully arrest' is a complete news headline, which is why the fodder hides so well. The elements themselves are neither particularly rare nor earths, but the chemists' name stuck.

12 down WATER CLOSET

Where to go in castle tower undergoing restoration (5,6)

Definition: Where to go · anagram

  • castle tower → CASTLETOWER eleven letters of fodder, no letter wasted

The surface stays with the builders while the definition slips into the euphemism a parent uses to a child. WC on a British door or floor plan is short for exactly this phrase.

13 down PROCEDURES

M.O.s involving secure drop (10)

Definition: M.O.s · anagram

  • secure drop → SECUREDROP ten letters of fodder

M.O. is police-drama shorthand for modus operandi, Latin for way of operating. 'Secure drop' sounds like spy tradecraft, which is why the letters sit there unexamined.

14 down TIDAL WAVES

Current events - or, figuratively, the public's responses (5,5)

Definition: Current events · double definition

  • Current events → movements of the sea driven by the pull of the tide, arriving all at once
  • the public's responses → when a whole country reacts together, the reaction is pictured as water sweeping everything before it

'Current events' is deliberately the newsreader's phrase and is nothing of the sort: the currents are in the sea. The second half admits with 'figuratively' that it is only a metaphor.

18 down THE PITS

Rubbish comprising uneaten peach parts? (3,4)

Definition: Rubbish · double definition

  • Rubbish → in British English, calling something rubbish means it is dreadful, the worst of its kind
  • uneaten peach parts → the hard stone at the centre of the fruit is the bit that goes in the bin

The definite article belongs to the idiom rather than to the fruit: peaches supply pits, and only 'the pits' means dreadful. The question mark admits that treating the stones this way is playful.

19 down TRIPOLI

Journey arriving at oil rig regularly somewhere in Libya (7)

Definition: somewhere in Libya · charade + alternate letters

  • Journey → TRIP a short journey is a trip
  • oil rig → OLI the first, third and fifth letters of the two words run together

The surface sends you offshore to an oil worker's commute and the answer is in North Africa. 'Regularly' is easy to read as scenery — a rig visited regularly — when it is the instruction.

21 down DEAN

College official having stripped off on retirement denied knighthood (4)

Definition: College official · reversal + deletion

  • having stripped off → NAKED somebody who has stripped off is naked, and the word is written out before anything is done to it
  • knighthood → K a knighthood shows as K in the honours list, as in KBE and KCMG, and that K is the letter dropped here

The surface is a scandal in the senior common room. 'Denied knighthood' sounds like a career ruined and is one letter refused, which is Everyman's joke.

22 down SKYE

Spot of land grabbed from Murmansk yesterday (4)

Definition: Spot of land · hidden word

  • Murmansk yesterday → SKYE four consecutive letters bridging the gap between the two words, two coming from each

A Russian port and a plain adverb, put together because the join between them is where the island sits. 'Spot of land' is vague on purpose: it could be a field, a farm or a stain on a map.
